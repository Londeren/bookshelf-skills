# Grand Slam Offer: pick the market, raise the value, build the offer in five steps and price it as positioning

Open this sheet to build a new offer or to diagnose one that sells on price: choosing the market and the avatar, the Value Equation, the five steps from dream outcome to trim & stack, and price as positioning.
The order market > offer > persuasion, the normal-market assumption, the niche slap and when to broaden are in sheet 01; scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, naming and the premium / free / discount wrappers are in sheet 03.
The money model behind the offer is in sheet 10; pricing plays, price raises and the Crazy Eight are in sheet 13.

## Market and avatar

#### 02.1. Four indicators of a market
- **Rule:** Before building an offer, check the market on all four indicators — Massive Pain (they desperately need it, not merely want it), Purchasing Power (they have, or can reach, the money your price requires), Easy to Target (associations, mailing lists, social media groups or channels gather them), Growing (not shrinking) — and do not pick a market that fails any one of them, however well it scores on the other three.
- Why it works: The degree of pain sets the price you can charge, an audience that cannot afford you cannot buy, one you cannot find you cannot market to, and a growing market is a tailwind while a declining one fights every effort; picking a market is always a choice, so choose by ability to pay, not out of attachment to an audience.
- Bad → Good: A resume service for the unemployed has pain, plenty of people and easy targeting but no purchasing power, and newspapers had pain, money and targeting in a shrinking market → inside relationships, second-half-of-life coaching for old timers beats coaching college students: more pain, more buying power, easy to find, and at the time of writing more people turning 65 each year than turning 20 ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: Growing does not require a boom: a normal market, growing at the rate of the marketplace with common unmet needs in health, wealth or relationships, is enough, and only a bad, shrinking market is ruled out (see sheet 01).
- Anchor: «When picking markets, I look for four indicators:» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Pricing: Finding The Right Market -- A Starving Crowd

#### 02.2. Channel demand, do not create it
- **Rule:** Build the offer to channel a desire the market already has — the dream outcome prospects already picture, the gap between their current reality and their dreams — through your vehicle; an offer that first has to create demand for itself fails this check.
- Why it works: Selling anything takes demand, and without a market for the offer nothing that follows works; people's deep desires do not change, so the job is to route existing desire through the offer and the monetization vehicle.
- Bad → Good: Setting out to create desire for what the business sells → channelling a desire people already have through the offer and the monetization vehicle.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «We are not trying to *create* demand. We are trying to *channel* it.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Finding The Right Market

#### 02.3. The prospect must feel understood
- **Rule:** Judge an offer's pitch or copy by whether the prospect would feel understood — their pain articulated and their dream outcome depicted back accurately, then how the vehicle gets them there — not merely by whether a reader understands it.
- Why it works: Writing succeeds when the reader understands, persuasion only when the prospect feels understood, and naming the pain a prospect feels accurately is what almost always gets them to buy what you offer.
- Bad → Good: A pitch that describes the product → a pitch that articulates the pain the prospect is feeling.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «The point of good persuasion is for the prospect to feel *understood.*» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Finding The Right Market

#### 02.4. Your First Avatar
- **Rule:** When the business has customers, redefine the avatar in four steps: 1) survey your customers; 2) sort the replies by those you like most, who spent the most and stayed longest, and analyse only the top 20%; 3) write down the fewest qualifiers they all share, usually three to five; 4a) state the customer requirements up front and make all advertising speak to that avatar, and 4b) reverse-engineer the buying process the best customers went through and make every lead go through it on purpose.
- Why it works: Growth comes from selling more customers or making them worth more, and this does both — marketing becomes tailored and the customers you sell are the highest-value ones; it is Pareto on steroids: 20% of customers bring in 80% of revenue, so replacing the other 80% with high spenders grows the business 5x.
- Bad → Good: At Gym Launch the top 20% gave four sets of qualifiers — demographics, business requirements (a signed lease, 1+ employees, $10,000+ a month in revenue at the start), aspirations and buying reasons — and the survey showed 78% of top customers had consumed at least two pieces of long-form content before buying, so two such pieces were injected into every lead's journey and reps hand-picked two or three per prospect (Lost Chapters 2025).
- When not to apply: Not a beginner process: with no customers yet, start with the industry you know most about, set a narrow target, and run the customer analysis again once there are customers to survey.
- Anchor: «There are four steps to installing this process I outline them below Then, I share what» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Your First Avatar

#### 02.5. Sell only to customers who meet the requirements
- **Rule:** Once the avatar's requirements are set, sell to no one who fails them, shift effort to the channels the qualifying customers come through, and do not strip qualification steps from the buyer journey to raise lead volume.
- Why it works: A business that takes anyone with a pulse and a credit card gets high churn, high acquisition costs, low retention and lower satisfaction; removing qualification steps has each time raised lead volume and lowered the money made.
- Bad → Good: A competitor in the same market that took every buyer who could pay made 70x less profit through its different customer segmentation → the Vista method, broken down by a private equity speaker: score existing customers by who stays longest and pays most, cut the channels that brought the low-value ones and double down on the channels that brought the best (Lost Chapters 2025).
- When not to apply: Narrowing means serving fewer customers in the short term and may cost revenue while the change is made; and the target is the number of qualification steps that gives the highest return on advertising over the long haul, not the most steps.
- Anchor: «Stop selling anyone who does not meet your» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Your First Avatar

#### 02.6. Niche the same product down
- **Rule:** To raise the price a product can carry, apply the same core product to a narrower avatar, rung by rung, renaming and re-messaging it for that avatar until the buyer reads it as made exactly for them.
- Why it works: Riches are in the niches, as Dan Kennedy taught the author: the pieces may be identical to the generic version, but applied to one avatar and sold with messaging that speaks to it, buyers find it more compelling and get more value from it in a real way, so the same product can carry 100x the price.
- Bad → Good: Time Management at $19 → Time Management For Sales Professionals at $99 → for B2B Outbound Sales Reps at $499, since one sale nets that rep $500 → narrowed once more to B2B outbound reps selling power tools and gardening at $1000 to $2000; likewise generic weight loss at $19 against the same eat-less-move-more program for shift-nurses at $1997 ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: Meant for most businesses under $10M a year; beyond that, broadening depends on the total addressable market (see sheet 01).
- Anchor: «Let’s just niche down one last level…. “Time Management for B2B Outbound Power Tools & Gardening Sales Reps.” Boom.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Pricing: Finding The Right Market -- A Starving Crowd

## The Value Equation

#### 02.7. The Value Equation
- **Rule:** Measure and build every offer on the four drivers of the Value Equation — increase the Dream Outcome and the Perceived Likelihood of Achievement, decrease the Perceived Time Delay (from purchase to the promised benefit) and the Perceived Effort & Sacrifice — and keep an element in the offer only if it moves at least one of them.
- Why it works: The top two are divided by the bottom two rather than added to them, on purpose: as the bottom approaches zero value becomes infinite, and these four are what create or detract from the value someone is willing to pay for.
- Bad → Good: A sceptic sure that $42,000 a year cannot be legitimate converts once four questions are answered in his own terms — would he pay $42,000 to make an extra $239,000 this year (yes, if he knew he would make it back), what he would have to do (about 15 hours a week), how long it takes (eleven months), how much up front (nothing, pay as you start making the money) — the four drivers as four questions (example — $100M Offers 2021, ch. 5 Pricing: Charge What It’s Worth).
- When not to apply: Every driver is perceived: the offer gains value only once the prospect perceives the higher likelihood and the lower delay and effort, so delivering them without communicating them does not count.
- Anchor: «As you can see from the picture, there are four primary drivers of value. Two of the drivers (on top), you will seek to increase. The other two (on the bottom), you will seek to decrease.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 6 Value Offer: The Value Equation

#### 02.8. Get the bottom of the equation to zero
- **Rule:** Put more of the offer work into cutting time delay and effort & sacrifice than into bigger claims on the top of the equation; when the competition is free, compete on speed (Fast Beats Free).
- Why it works: Larger-than-life claims are the easiest to make and so the least unique, since anyone can promise; delay and effort are the harder, more competitive side, the marketplace rewards making things immediate, seamless and effortless — why done-for-you costs more than do-it-yourself — and only fast beats free, since many will always pay for speed.
- Bad → Good: Beginner marketers' bigger and bigger claims → plastic surgeons' copy aimed at the effort and sacrifice of the alternative vehicle: "tired of wasting countless hours in the gym, tired of trying diets that just don't work".
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «The harder, and more competitive, are the Time Delay and Effort & Sacrifice. The best companies in the world focus all their attention on the bottom side of the equation.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 6 The Value Equation

#### 02.9. Dream outcomes cancel out
- **Rule:** When your offer satisfies the same desire as a competitor's, differentiate on perceived likelihood of achievement, time delay and effort & sacrifice, not on the dream outcome.
- Why it works: Identical dream outcomes cancel out, so the other three drivers decide perceived value and price — why one thing that makes someone beautiful is worth $50,000 and another $5; and people pay for certainty, so raising their belief that it will work for them adds value while your work stays the same.
- Bad → Good: The surgery and the time it takes are the same or better in experienced hands, yet a sane person pays far more to be a plastic surgeon's 10,000th patient than the first, and might ask to be paid for being the first: only the perceived likelihood changed.
- When not to apply: The dream outcome decides value only when two different desires are compared, not two vehicles for the same desire.
- Anchor: «That being said, when comparing two products or services that satisfy the *same* desire, the value from the dream outcomes will cancel out (since they are the same).» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 6 Value Offer: The Value Equation

#### 02.10. Talk in terms of status
- **Rule:** Express the dream outcome in terms of the status the prospect believes it will gain them, and in copy spell out how other people will perceive the achievement.
- Why it works: The dream outcome that most directly raises a prospect's status is generally the one they value most — whole categories of offers are valued above others for that reason alone — and connecting the dots to how others will react makes the same benefit more powerful.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Between two offers for the same desire the dream outcome cancels out; see 02.9.
- Anchor: «Talk in termsof things your prospect believes will increase their status, and you will have your prospects drooling.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 6 The Value Equation

#### 02.11. Long-term outcome and short-term experience
- **Rule:** When the promised outcome takes months to arrive, build a fast emotional win into the first days after purchase: the long-term outcome is what they buy, the short-term experience is what keeps them long enough to get it.
- Why it works: An early win reinforces the decision to buy, makes them trust you more and makes them likelier to follow the rest of the system to the end; people who win early keep going more often than those who do not.
- Bad → Good: Gym owners buy an extra $239,000 a year that takes a while to arrive, so delivery gets their ads live and their first $2,000 sale closed inside seven days; weight-loss clients were introduced to another client for an immediate social benefit and put on a more aggressive diet at the start, though the bikini body may be twelve months away ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: Stated for services whose promised outcome takes months to arrive.
- Anchor: «There are two elements to this driver of value: Long-term outcome and short-term experience.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 6 Value Offer: The Value Equation

#### 02.12. Logical vs Psychological Solutions
- **Rule:** When a problem of value — waiting, price, effort — resists the obvious fix, try a psychological solution that changes how the prospect perceives it rather than a bigger logical one.
- Why it works: Logical solutions are what everyone tries, so they have usually been tried already — if one worked the problem would be gone — and what is left are psychological problems; value rises when the perceived delay and sacrifice shrink, not only the actual ones.
- Bad → Good: Make the elevator faster → floor-to-ceiling mirrors so riders forget how long they rode; make the trains faster, for billions → a dotted map of when the next train comes, for a few million, which gave the London tunnel system its biggest rise in rider satisfaction; make it cheaper → make fewer of them and raise the price.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «As a business owners and entrepreneurs I increasingly approach problems to find *psychological* solutions, rather than *logical* ones.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 6 The Value Equation

## Building the offer: five steps

#### 02.13. The five steps of creating a Grand Slam Offer
- **Rule:** Build the core offer in this order: #1 the prospect's dream outcome; #2 every obstacle they will meet on the way; #3 each obstacle restated as a solution; #4 every way you could deliver each solution; #5a trim to what is highest value and lowest cost to you; #5b stack what remains into one high-value deliverable.
- Why it works: Working through every problem and every way of solving it yields an offer that cannot be compared to anything else on the market, so the prospect decides on value rather than on price.
- Bad → Good: One weight-loss offer runs through the steps below: problems in 02.14, solutions in 02.15, delivery vehicles in 02.17, trimming in 02.19 and 02.20, the stack in 02.21.
- When not to apply: The five steps make the core offer only; enhancements (scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, naming) come after it (see sheet 03).
- Anchor: «**Step #4:** We figured out all the different ways we could deliver those solutions.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Value Offer: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack

#### 02.14. List every problem in the customer's sequence
- **Rule:** For step #2, take each core thing the customer has to do in the order they will meet it, including what happens immediately before and after they use the product, and under each list every reason they could not do it or keep doing it, prompted by the four problem buckets: not financially worth it (Dream Outcome), won't work for me (Likelihood of Achievement), too hard (Effort & Sacrifice), takes too much time (Time).
- Why it works: Problems always trace back to the value drivers and the solutions give the prospect permission to buy; one missing answer can stop a purchase, the more problems you list the more you get to solve, and answering the next problem as it appears makes the offer more valuable and compelling.
- Bad → Good: For weight loss: buying healthy food, cooking it, eating it, exercising regularly, and under each objections from all four buckets — not financially worth it; it won't work for me, I won't stick with it, outside factors will get in the way; it is hard, confusing, I will suck at it; it takes too much time, I am too busy, it will take too long to work — 16 core problems with two to four sub-problems each, 32 to 64 in all.
- When not to apply: The buckets only get your brain going; do not let them limit the list, and if it is easier, list everything you can think of.
- Anchor: «Now we’re gonna go full circle here. Each of the above problems has four negative elements. And you guessed it, each aligns with the four value drivers as well.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 9 Value Offer: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part I: Problems & Solutions

#### 02.15. Turn every problem into a "how to" solution
- **Rule:** For step #3, rewrite each listed problem as a solution by putting "how to" in front and reversing the complaint, one per problem, and use the resulting list as the checklist the offer has to cover.
- Why it works: It becomes a checklist of exactly what must be done and solved for the prospect, and if only one of those needs is missing from the solution it can cause someone not to buy; repeats across problems are expected, since all of them trace back to the four value drivers.
- Bad → Good: Takes too much time → how to buy healthy food quickly; is expensive → how to buy healthy food for less than your current grocery bill; is undoable if I travel → how to get healthy food when traveling.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «*. . . is hard, confusing, I won’t like it. I will suck at it→* How to make buying healthy food easy and enjoyable, so that anyone can do it (especially busy moms!)» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 9 Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part I

#### 02.16. List delivery vehicles divergently
- **Rule:** For step #4, write down every way you could deliver each solution, including ways you are not actually willing to deliver; cutting comes later, in the trim (02.19).
- Why it works: Listing past what you would do jogs you out of the version you would default to; life pays for divergent thinking — many right answers, one far more right than the others — while school trains the convergent kind because it is easy to grade.
- Bad → Good: The Brick Exercise as a warm-up: 120 seconds to write as many uses of a brick as you can, then again asking how big the brick is, what it is made of and how it is shaped, which turns up far more uses than the first list (example — $100M Offers, ch. 8 Value Offer: The Thought Process).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Think of all the things that might enhance the value of your offer.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Part II: Trim & Stack

#### 02.17. Product Delivery Cheat Codes
- **Rule:** Vary the delivery vehicle for each solution along the cheat codes: level of personal attention (one-on-one, small group, one to many); effort expected from the client (DIY, DWY, DFY); if live, the medium (in person, phone, email, text, Zoom, chat); if recorded, the format (audio, video, written); how fast you reply, on which days and during which hours; and the 10x to 1/10th test — what you would provide if they paid 10x your price (or $100,000), and how you would still make them successful at one tenth of it.
- Why it works: Stretching the mind in either direction gives widely different solutions, and the solutions to one problem give ideas for others you would not otherwise have considered.
- Bad → Good: For the one problem "buying healthy food is hard": one-on-one — in-person grocery shopping, a personalized list, a phone call during the shop; small group — a group shopping trip, a group list workshop, buying and delivering the food; one to many — a live-streamed or recorded grocery tour, a DIY grocery calculator, a grocery buddy system, pre-made insta-cart carts.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «1. What level of personal attention do I want to provide? one-on-one, small group, one to many» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Value Offer: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack

#### 02.18. Solve every perceived problem
- **Rule:** Make the offer answer every obstacle the buyer believes they will have; when a prospect raises one it does not yet solve, build a solution for it rather than holding to your preferred way of solving the problem.
- Why it works: A single unsolved item is repeatedly the reason someone does not buy; refusing to get romantic about how you solve the problem, and solving it instead, is what makes an offer people cannot say no to.
- Bad → Good: A prospect who ate lunch out every day was about to be lost because the program insisted on home-cooked food → an eating-out guide for restaurants, offered on the spot, closed the sale; the guide then went to every prospect, and templates kept being added until no single objection was left.
- When not to apply: Leaving an obstacle unsolved does not mean selling no one, only selling fewer people than you could have.
- Anchor: «You must resolve every obstacle a buyer believes they will have to convert the highest amount of people.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Part II: Trim & Stack

## Trim & stack

#### 02.19. Trim & Stack (Step #5)
- **Rule:** For step #5a, cost every listed vehicle and remove the high cost, low value items first, then the low cost, low value ones, keeping only low cost, high value and high cost, high value items, and lean hardest on high value, one-to-many vehicles.
- Why it works: High value, one-to-many solutions have the widest gap between cost and value — a high one-time cost to create and almost no additional effort afterwards — so they bring high-margin profit for years, which is why software becomes so valuable.
- Bad → Good: An excel application built before his first gym turned a client's goals into over 100 meals matched to their macronutrients and calories, the exact grocery amounts and bulk-prep instructions: about 100 hours to build once, then about 15 minutes for each truly personalized, expensively priced plan; the meal plans were later used by 4,000+ gyms ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: Small-group and one-on-one delivery are not banned; keep those high cost items for big value adds only.
- Anchor: «Next, I look at the cost of providing these solutions to me (the business). I remove the ones that are high cost and low value first. Then I remove low cost, low value items.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Value Offer: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack

#### 02.20. Step back one level at a time
- **Rule:** When the highest-value delivery you can imagine costs more time or money than you will bear, replace it with the nearest lesser version you can deliver at scale, stepping back one notch at a time until the commitment is one you can live with — or raise the price until it becomes worth it.
- Why it works: Once the maximum service is imagined, the useful question is whether a lesser version of that experience can be delivered at scale, and a lower-cost alternative of equal value is always preferred.
- Bad → Good: Moving in with a client to do their shopping, exercising and cooking would make them sure of losing weight but is not worth any price short of a gazillion dollars → ask which lesser version of that experience scales, and step back from there.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Just take one step back at a time until you arrive at something that has a time commitment or cost you are willing to live with» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Part II: Trim & Stack

#### 02.21. Problem → Solution Wording → Sexier Name for Bundle
- **Rule:** For step #5b, write each part of the stack as the problem, then the solution wording, then a sexier name for the bundle, with the actual delivery vehicles listed underneath, and put all the bundles together into one high-value deliverable.
- Why it works: Presented this way the bundle solves all the perceived problems, not just some, gives you conviction that what you sell is one of a kind, and makes the offering impossible to compare with the one down the street.
- Bad → Good: A weight-loss program that would be compared with a gym membership → Foolproof Bargain Grocery System ($1,000), Ready in 5min Busy Parent Cooking Guide ($600), Personalized Lick Your Fingers Good Meal Plan ($500), Fat Burning Workouts ($699), The Ultimate Tone Up While You Travel Blueprint ($199), The Never Fall Off Accountability System ($1,000), The Live It Up While Slimming Down Eating Out System ($349): $4,351 of stated value sold for $599 ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Problem → Solution Wording→ Sexier Name for Bundle .» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Value Offer: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack

#### 02.22. Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction.
- **Rule:** Sequence a new business, offer or acquisition channel in three stages — generate demand first, then get people saying yes to the offer, and only then add friction to the marketing or offer less for the same price; on a first Grand Slam Offer, over-deliver even where it does not scale and fix operations later out of the cash flow.
- Why it works: Until demand flows in you cannot tell whether what you have is good, and doing more for every customer with cash coming in beats optimising a business with zero cash flow and no idea what to adjust; the Sales to Fulfillment Continuum trades ease of sale against ease of fulfilment, and the goal is the sweet spot where it sells well and is easy to fulfil.
- Bad → Good: The original model was 21 days on site per gym at his own cost for hotels, car, food and ad spend, keeping 100% of the cash collected against a refundable $500 deposit; one gym he did not want to fly to was shown how and did all the work itself, collected almost $44,000 in 30 days, 4x its previous month, and the model became done-with-you at about a third of the price, serving hundreds of gyms a month instead of eight, with the same promise and a new how (example — $100M Offers 2021, ch. 5 Pricing: Charge What It’s Worth).
- When not to apply: Over-delivery is a stage, not the permanent operating model: it holds for the first offer, or while flow has not yet been created.
- Anchor: «I have always lived by the mantra, “Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction.” This means I generate demand *first*.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 10 Value Offer: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack

## Price as positioning

#### 02.23. Grand Slam Offer
- **Rule:** Call an offer a Grand Slam Offer only when no prospect can compare it with anything else on the market and it joins a premium price, an unbeatable guarantee, an attractive promotion and an unmatchable value proposition to a money model — payment terms that get you paid to acquire new customers.
- Why it works: It hits all three requirements for growth at once — higher response, higher conversion and premium prices — and they multiply rather than add, until acquiring a customer is so cheap against what you make that cash flow stops being the bottleneck and the limit becomes your ability to do the work.
- Bad → Good: Same ad spend, old model that lost half the ad spend up front and broke even only after 30 days → a Grand Slam Offer with 2.5x the response, 2.5x the close rate and a 4x up-front price, collecting $112,000 on $10,000 of ad spend, 22.4x more cash up front ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: Not to be confused with a commodity offer, which sells the same fulfilment on price: the work can be identical and only the perception differs; build the guarantee and other enhancers with sheet 03 and the payment terms with sheet 10.
- Anchor: «an offer you present to the marketplace that cannot be compared to any other product or service available, combining an attractive promotion, an unmatchable value proposition, a premium price, and an unbeatable guarantee» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 3 Pricing: The Commodity Problem

#### 02.24. Category of one
- **Rule:** Differentiate the offer until the prospect's choice is between your product and nothing, a value-driven purchase, rather than between you and a competitor, a price-driven one.
- Why it works: When comparison is impossible the prospect has to stop and assess value, which recalibrates their value-meter; in that new perceived marketplace you are a monopoly and can make monopoly profits, while commoditized offers all look alike and race to the bottom.
- Bad → Good: $1,000 down and a $1,000/mo retainer for agency services, the same offer as everyone else (you pay us to work, maybe you get results) → pay once with no retainer and just cover ad spend, I generate and work your leads, you pay only if people show up, 20 people in your first month or the next month free, plus daily sales coaching, tested scripts, tested price points and offers and sales recordings ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «it allows you to sell in a “category of one,” or, to apply another great phrase, to “sell in a vacuum.”» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 3 Pricing: The Commodity Problem

#### 02.25. Optimise for money, not for customer count
- **Rule:** Judge a price or offer decision by the money it makes over the customer's lifetime, not by how many customers it brings; of the prices you have data on, pick the one that makes the most money.
- Why it works: The objective of a business is making money, not getting people to buy; money is what keeps you in business and able to help people longer.
- Bad → Good: The price that wins the most buyers → the price in the total return column: the most people converting at the highest total lifetime value (example — Pricing playbook, Three Metrics To Determine value-Driven Pricing).
- When not to apply: Holds unless you are running a different short-term strategy.
- Anchor: «Getting people to buy is NOT the objective of a business. Making money is.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 5 Charge What It's Worth

#### 02.26. Do not compete on price
- **Rule:** Do not use a lower price as the competitive lever, and never position the business as the second cheapest in its market; position it as the most expensive.
- Why it works: Price can only go down to zero while value can go infinitely high, so cutting price is a one-way road to destruction; and, as Dan Kennedy put it, being the second cheapest carries no strategic benefit, while being the most expensive does.
- Bad → Good: Prices slashed to get more customers leave a full client load barely making it on thin margins, because more clients cost more money and time and that money comes out of the margin (example — $100M Offers, ch. 2 Grand Slam Offers).
- When not to apply: The exception is a revolutionary way of cutting your costs to 1/10th of the competition's.
- Anchor: «So, unless you have a revolutionary way of decreasing your costs to 1/10th compared to your competition, don't compete on price.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 5 Charge What It's Worth

#### 02.27. Virtuous Cycle of Price
- **Rule:** Charge a premium and spend the extra margin on the client — an exceptional experience, the best people, systems — so the higher price raises clients' emotional investment, perceived value and results and draws the best clients, who are easiest to satisfy and cost less to fulfil.
- Why it works: Run the other way it is the vicious price cycle: cheaper clients invest less, value the service less, get worse results and become more demanding, while the thin margin leaves less to spend on them and drains profit per customer, self-value and conviction in the sales process; raising the price runs the loop in reverse.
- Bad → Good: Trey priced insanely cheap believing customers at dirt cheap rates would be the most grateful; they were the worst customers he ever had, a customer service nightmare, and more of them still meant losing money every month (example — Price Raise playbook, Raise Prices).
- When not to apply: The product has to deliver: big-ticket prices take outworking your self-doubt and the confidence of having done it so many times that you know this person will succeed.
- Anchor: «Let me introduce you to the virtuous cycle of price.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 5 Pricing: Charge What It’s Worth

#### 02.28. Price far above the market
- **Rule:** When setting a premium, price so far above the market that a buyer concludes something entirely different must be going on — not slightly above the average, and never at or just under it.
- Why it works: A higher price directly raises perceived value, and only a gap that wide creates a category of one with monopoly profits; pricing at the market is pricing for market efficiency, where competitors keep offering a little more for a little less until no one can, the competitors being copied are dead broke, and the typical models were built by funded companies able to run at a loss for years.
- Bad → Good: Low-price competitors at $500 a month and one premium player at $5,000 → $16,000 for a 16-week done-with-you intensive, three times the highest and 32 times the lowest, then 35 percent upsold into a three-year $42,000/year agreement, against an average gym owner take-home of $35,280/year ($100M Offers 2021).
- When not to apply: Never charge more than the product is worth; what the price must sit far above is the cost to fulfil it, since a large gap between cost and price is the only way to be unreasonably successful.
- Anchor: «And the goal isn’t just to be slightly above the market price — the goal is to be so much higher that a consumer thinks to themselves» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 5 Charge What It's Worth

#### 02.29. Price to Value Discrepancy
- **Rule:** Keep value above price at all times, widen the gap by adding value rather than by cutting price, and raise the price only after value has risen enough that the buyer still gets a deal.
- Why it works: People buy because they believe what they get is worth more than what they give and stop the moment it is worth less; price can only fall to zero while value can rise without limit, so the goal is more yeses at a higher price through a wider gap.
- Bad → Good: A private photography company found what its customers valued most, tripled down on it and eliminated everything else; time per customer went down, satisfaction went up, and over two years the average ticket rose from $300 to $1,500 and weekly profit from $1,000 to $38,000 (example — $100M Offers 2021, ch. 6 Value Offer: The Value Equation).
- When not to apply: Cutting price is the wrong way to widen the gap most of the time, not always; the exception is in 02.26.
- Anchor: «They believe what they are getting (VALUE) is worth *more* than what they are giving in exchange for it (PRICE). The moment the value they receive dips below what they are paying, they stop buying from you.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 5 Pricing: Charge What It’s Worth

#### 02.30. Price so that it stings
- **Rule:** When the customer has to do something themselves to get the result, set the price so that paying it stings a little.
- Why it works: The sting forces and focuses attention — those who pay the most pay the most attention — and the more invested buyers are, the more likely they are to achieve the result.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Stated only for offers where the customer's own action produces the result.
- Anchor: «Ideally, this means pricing your services or product in such a way that it *stings* a little when they buy.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 5 Charge What It's Worth
