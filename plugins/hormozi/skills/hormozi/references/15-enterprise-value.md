# Enterprise value: make the business an asset that runs, grows and sells without the owner

Open this sheet when an owner wants the business to run without him, to be worth a multiple rather than an income, to get lead-getting work out of employees, or to expand without overextending: why enterprise value, the four steps out of the day-to-day, employees who get you leads, wealth alchemy, overextension and its tests.
Source strength: 15.1 and the employee rules (15.12–15.17) rest on the Employees chapter of $100M Leads (2023) and Section D of the Lost Chapters (2025); every other rule rests on four video transcripts — oral speech, auto-captions, some elements said only once — and weighs less than the books; where a video and a book say the same thing, the book is the anchor.
Scope: the mechanics of removing the owner from acquisition, delivery and sales are in; hiring and management as a topic are out. The three levers as a diagnostic and the returns on lead-getting employees (cost per engaged lead, CAC, LTGP:CAC) are in sheet 01; making customers worth more and keeping them is sheet 14.

## Why enterprise value

#### 15.1. A business that makes money without you
- **Rule:** Count a business as an asset only when it makes its profit without you; one that makes money only with you in it is a high-paying job and a bad investment for anyone else.
- Why it works: If an asset makes millions without you, somebody else could use it to make millions without them, which is what makes it a good investment and what investors buy: you get rich from what you make, you become wealthy from what you own.
- Bad → Good: The same business at $5,000,000 of revenue and $2,000,000 of profit: needing you around the clock, it is a high-paying job → running without you, you get your time back and the $2,000,000 of annual profit, especially if climbing, is easily worth $10,000,000+ today (Lost Chapters, 2025); likewise two owners at $10 million topline and $2 million bottom line: Frustrated Fred, required 80 hours a week, adds about $500,000 a year to his net worth after 50% tax and living expenses, while Wealthy William's team runs the day-to-day and his business, at six times profit, is worth $12 million (example — the videos, lecture on a business that runs without you, 2025).
- When not to apply: The 6x multiple and the 50% tax in the Fred and William example are illustration numbers built live on a board, not benchmarks.
- Anchor: «it’s climbing, could easily be worth $10,000,000+, right now So your business went from» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), SECTION D: EXPANDED EMPLOYEES CHAPTER

#### 15.2. Cash flow first, then enterprise value as the wealth vehicle
- **Rule:** Once the business covers your cash needs, build wealth by growing the value of what you own rather than by taking profit as income: reinvest profit into the business instead of chasing tax strategy.
- Why it works: Income is taxed to oblivion and nothing multiplies on it, while growth in enterprise value compounds untaxed until sale; by the time a business has enterprise value it has usually already thrown off more cash than the owner needs, and that surplus reaches him in a tax-inefficient way; a high-value enterprise can also be borrowed against, used for lines of credit and raised on.
- Bad → Good: An extra $500,000 of profit taken as income adds about $250,000 after 50% tax → inside a business that trades at 6x profit it adds $3 million of net worth (video, 2025); $3 million of profit in a high-tax state leaves $1.5 million after tax, but spending it to add $12 million of revenue and roughly $120 million of enterprise value puts the effective rate near one and a half per cent (example — the videos, wealth alchemy, 2023–2024).
- When not to apply: If you do not know where next month's rent is coming from, enterprise value is not your game yet; the untaxed compounding holds only while you keep building and do not cash out; and the author says business is not the only game in life worth winning.
- Anchor: «Then you want the most efficient tax vehicle for building wealth, which is your enterprise value.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the answer on enterprise value

#### 15.3. The only three things you can do to increase Enterprise Value
- **Rule:** Count an initiative as building enterprise value only if it gets more customers (acquisition), makes them worth more (delivery) or decreases risk in the business (operations); keep the internal operations leader supporting acquisition and delivery, and never let that person take the big calls of the business.
- Why it works: A business with no massive risk that can get as many customers as it pleases while its LTV keeps scaling is a valuable business; operations exists to support the other two — keeping you out of prison and the machine running — so its decisions should be the ones that help the business get more customers or deliver on them better.
- Bad → Good: The head of internal operations (legal, HR, payroll, CRM) taking the big calls → operations as a vendor to the heads of acquisition and delivery, supporting their decisions.
- When not to apply: The operations rule assumes the three functional leaders are in place: acquisition, delivery and internal operations.
- Anchor: «those three legs of the stool roll up to the only three things that you can do to increase Enterprise Value in business you can get more customers you can make them worth more» — video “13 Years of No BS Business Advice in 79 Mins” (2024), point 16

## The four steps and step one: the self-inventory

#### 15.4. Frustrated Fred and Wealthy William: the four steps
- **Rule:** To turn a business that requires you into one that runs without you, work four steps in this order: a self-inventory of everything you do (15.5–15.8), trading doing for managing and leading (15.9), removing yourself from marketing, which is acquisition (15.11), and then actually taking your 90 days off.
- Why it works: A business valuable only to you is valueless to anyone else; once it can make the profit without you it becomes something other people would want to buy, and your wealth then grows at the business multiple instead of at post-tax savings.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Something will break during the 90 days off: that is part of the test, not a sign it came too early — treat whatever breaks as the next item of work and continue.
- Anchor: «So the first step of actually taking it from frustrated Fred to wealthy William is you do a self-inventory.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

#### 15.5. Step one: the self-inventory
- **Rule:** Run step one in this order: if you do not know what you do, a time study first (an Excel sheet with a row every 15 minutes, a timer, one word noted per slot, for a week); then a list of every single thing you do, as granular as humanly possible; a project, process or person slotted against each item; the list coloured and solved green to red (15.6); decision trees and a money box around the decisions (15.7); a scorecard and KPIs for the role and a pass/fail test (15.8).
- Why it works: Turning each item into something someone else can do is how you get out of the day-to-day without breaking the machine; the time study supplies the raw list and makes the most productive week of your life, because you are proving your productivity to yourself; a project is a one-time thing that sometimes creates a process, a person does the item continuously, and underutilised people already on your team can take some slots at once.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The elements come from a spoken walkthrough and are listed in sequence only in the author's closing recap; run the same time study on any key person the business depends on too much, and no technology beyond a spreadsheet and a timer is needed.
- Anchor: «we put our big list together. We red, yellow, greened it. We then created a decision tree.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

#### 15.6. Red, yellow, green: solve green to red
- **Rule:** Clear the inventory in the order greens, then yellows, then reds: green is an item you can hand to somebody and teach now, yellow needs a one-time project or process you already know how to build, red is a skill you lack or a person you need and have not got.
- Why it works: Greens can be got out quickly and yellows take a little more time, so starting there keeps the hand-off moving instead of stalling on the hardest items; reds come once greens and yellows are done.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «The red is where it's something that I either don't know how to do or there's a person that I know I need to have, but I don't have. And so I solve these in green to reds because the greens you can get out quickly.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

#### 15.7. If this then that, and the money box
- **Rule:** For a duplicatable role with recurring questions, document the decisions as well as the tasks: write if-this-then-that rules of behaviour for each common scenario, set the money amount under which the person decides without your supervision plus a cap on the total, and use the monthly financials as the feedback loop.
- Why it works: Duplicatable jobs with many people in one function get standardised questions, so common scenarios can be decided once in advance instead of escalated each time; the cap gives a set number of shots to fix something, and a mistake stays inside a known number.
- Bad → Good: When the Prestige Labs support manager said new people were hard to onboard, the whole role was written as if-this-then-that pairs — change address, card, flavour or billing cadence, cancel, refund — each with one documented answer; a money box reads like decisions under $500 each, up to $5,000 in total, ten shots to fix something; and unchecked discretion let the author's team spend $250,000 a month on surprise five-star client dinners, which, once cut, changed neither happiness scores nor reviews (video, 2025).
- When not to apply: Complex roles meet one-off scenarios and standardise less, so this applies less there; the threshold depends on company size — under $500, $1,000, $10,000, sometimes $100,000 — and the author expects mistakes in the loop, checking whether each unlocked decision generates true value.
- Anchor: «we want to start saying if this then that these are rules of behavior right they're decision trees for common scenarios» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

#### 15.8. How do you and your role make the company money? The scorecard and the 80% test
- **Rule:** Before handing a role over, build its scorecard from that question; for a role whose link to revenue is indirect, list what bad work would cost the business, reverse each mistake into something that must be done right, and measure what percentage of the time it is; then graduate the person only when a test shows that at 80% of your skill they get 100% of the result.
- Why it works: A decision tree you unlock should unlock value, and people who see how their work relates to how the company makes money see the impact they already have; nobody matches you at one tenth of your reps, but someone taught only the right way, without your bad mistakes, can start at half your level — if you can do it right with part of your time, someone else can do it better with all of theirs.
- Bad → Good: The author's camera operator could only answer that he holds the camera → bad camera work wastes the whole team's time, makes media perform worse through framing or lighting, wastes footage, leaves equipment ill-fitted and files unsaved → his metrics become the percentage of the time footage is saved correctly, named correctly, on the right drive and timestamped, lighting and camera are set up right, and the shoot starts on time.
- When not to apply: Many of your team will not be able to answer the question, and that will frighten you; at the start the author accepts 80%, sometimes 60%, as long as there is a clear path to the person getting better.
- Anchor: «And so, I'll give you a really powerful frame that you can talk to anybody in the business with. You can say, how do you and your role make the company money?» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

## Step two: from doing to managing and leading

#### 15.9. Trading doing for managing and leading
- **Rule:** Swap your hours of doing for a fraction of that in managing, then replace yourself again by hiring a manager, until your job is recruiting A players, strategy and prioritisation, attracting talent and aligning incentives — not doing A-player work or today's tactics.
- Why it works: Your work ethic can beat any one person in the business but never all of them: a mountain of work is possible alone, Mount Everest takes more hands and more shovels, which is what an organisation is.
- Bad → Good: 200 hours a month of doing, roughly 50 hours a week, traded for 20 hours of managing is a tenth — a 10x gain in leverage — and hiring a manager repeats the trade one level up, four hours for one.
- When not to apply: The author does the arithmetic aloud and his figures slip (200 hours a month turns into "50 to five"), so the ratio of leverage, not the figures, is the point.
- Anchor: «So you want to start trading doing for managing and leading. So here's the simple math behind this.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

#### 15.10. The litmus tests
- **Rule:** Judge how far the business runs without you on an escalating ladder, and do not count flat as a pass: it does not burn down without you; you leave for a month and come back to it intact; you return to find it stronger than you left it; three months away and it has grown.
- Why it works: A business that grows while you are away works for an investor as a faster-growing annuity, which earns a big multiple and is why they pay a lot for it; if it is merely flat while you are gone, the magic is still in you, and as soon as your attention moves it can go down.
- Bad → Good: Treating survival — the business did not burn down while you were away — as the pass mark → coming back after a month to a business in a better position, and after three months off to a bigger one.
- When not to apply: In a super capped market where there is no way the business can grow, flat is fine, though the vast majority of businesses can grow year over year; for the decision to open another location, 15.22 applies.
- Anchor: «And that's kind of my my litmus test is the baseline is does it burn down without me.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

## Step three: marketing without the founder

#### 15.11. Install marketing systems instead of founder ads
- **Rule:** When your face is what gets the customers, remove yourself from marketing by installing standing processes that harvest marketing the business already produces, in place of direct-to-camera founder ads: weekly screenshots of praise where your customers gather; incentives for testimonials (unlockables, credit, a trial of a higher tier); life cycle ads, where a testimonial triggers pulling that customer's recorded sales call, onboarding and delivery checkpoints from the CRM and scheduling and compressing them into one timeline ad; captured key delivery moments (a ribbon cutting, a new-kitchen reveal, getting on the scale, monthly financials); a bounty to support staff for every mini testimonial won in chat; existing review-site reviews turned into ads; and the funniest one-star reviews used to warn off the wrong buyer.
- Why it works: When customers come because of your face, you are the key man — it is why you cannot leave and why revenue drops when you do — and while being relied on feeds the ego, an important business needs you to become less important; a life cycle ad shows the customer scared, unsure and hesitating before signing up and then getting the outcome, instead of a claimed before and after.
- Bad → Good: The author pays a support rep five dollars for each mini testimonial captured in chat after a ticket is resolved, and screenshots it; after Liquid Death's marketing, he runs the funniest one-star reviews as an ad telling people who are like those reviewers that they won't like the product, because casting out the wrong type of person pulls in the right type.
- When not to apply: The author names the ways as he goes and counts "six or seven" himself, so treat the list as a pattern rather than a fixed count; he adds an affiliate program and whitelisted TikTok shop creator ads (2025) as further options.
- Anchor: «There are seven different ways that you can install systems into the business to capture media and marketing on your behalf without it having kind of quote direct to camera founder ads.» — video “How to Build a Business That Runs Without You” (2025), the lecture

## Employees who get you leads

#### 15.12. The Internal Core Four
- **Rule:** When you need more workers in order to advertise more, get them with the Core Four aimed at potential employees — warm outreach becomes asking your network, cold outreach recruiting, posting content posting job openings, paid ads promoting job postings; customer referrals become employee referrals, affiliates associations, guilds and listservs, agencies staffing firms — and when you need more, do more.
- Why it works: Employees are just other people you let know about your stuff, so the actions that get them line up with the actions that get customers, and like getting customers the process can be made reliable.
- Bad → Good: A cold outreach goal missed two quarters running traces back through a chain: a rep lost every four weeks with churn below the industry average, one in four candidates from HR hired, one qualified candidate for every ten screened — forty interviews per low-skill frontline hire, capping hiring at about one a week — so the problem was not the method, skills or offer but too few people doing cold outreach → once hiring outpaced churn, within six weeks, cold outreach sales had doubled by quarter end to more than half of total sales (example — $100M Leads, #2 Employees).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Remember the core four? Well, they work for getting employees too.» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees, How To Get Employee Leads

#### 15.13. The 3Ds: document, demonstrate, duplicate
- **Rule:** When you cannot afford people who already know how, train a hired employee to get leads in three steps: document — write the job as a checklist exactly as you do it, and accept the draft only when you can do an A+ job on a work block following only your own directions; demonstrate — do it in front of them, walking through the checklist step by step and adjusting it wherever they stop or slow you down; duplicate — they do it in front of you while you observe, you fix the checklist until it is right, and they follow it until they get it right.
- Why it works: If the checklist is right the outcome will be the same, and if it is off you find out fast; the clarity to aim for is a checklist a stranger could follow alone and get your results if you vanished tomorrow, and after the first few employees the kinks are worked out.
- Bad → Good: Breaking your flow to take notes while you work → recording yourself doing the task in several ways and writing the checklist as an observer.
- When not to apply: When the instructions are fine and the trainee still underperforms, the checklist is not the problem: competence is not performance (15.14).
- Anchor: «activities. I think about and actually approach training with this 3Ds» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees, How To Get Employees To Get You Leads

#### 15.14. Rewrite the step that needs explaining
- **Rule:** When a trainee needs a step explained, gets it wrong or gets confused, rewrite that step of the checklist — it is too complicated or several steps in one — instead of explaining it or blaming the trainee.
- Why it works: A trainee's confusion is a defect of the checklist, not of the trainee; an inferior checklist can be forced to work, but it turns into a nightmare when someone else takes over training.
- Bad → Good: Pushing a checklist through with long explanations and repeated demonstrations, which leads to training trouble that never ends → rewriting each step that needed explaining until the checklist works on its own.
- When not to apply: When the employee knows exactly what to do but is not good at it yet, leave the instructions alone and give more reps — slow, then smooth, then fast.
- Anchor: «confusing.]{.calibre24}[ If we have to explain what a step means» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees

#### 15.15. Judge on following directions
- **Rule:** Judge and reward lead-getting employees on how well they follow directions rather than on whether they got the right result; when they followed the directions exactly and still got the wrong result, praise them for following them and correct the checklist on the spot.
- Why it works: Train people to follow directions and they will; then a wrong result from followed directions points at the directions, which you control far more than the person.
- Bad → Good: Taking over when a trainee messes up → pausing, stepping back and letting them try again, acknowledging every successful step, because mistakes are what training is for and fast feedback cycles make people learn faster.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Focus on your employee's ability to follow directions more than» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees

#### 15.16. Hire anyone willing for ground-level advertising jobs
- **Rule:** For ground-level advertising jobs, hire anyone willing and put the effort into training rather than into selection.
- Why it works: Anyone can be taught a ground-level job, you cannot know anything about a person until you have trained them well and given them a fighting chance in the field, and for low-level jobs you will never be short of labour.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: "Get picky when you have to make massive investments in hyper-specific-multiple-six-figure-C-suite employees."
- Anchor: «pick is not as important as how you train the ones you do.» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees, Conclusion

#### 15.17. The performance diamond
- **Rule:** When a lead-getting employee's performance drops, work through four causes in order: communication (they don't know THAT you want it), training (they don't know HOW — check by watching them run the checklist as in training, never by asking whether they understand), motivation (they don't WANT to), circumstances (something is stopping them); once it has dropped for a week or two, open the next weekly one-on-one with the Best Diamond Hard Feedback Question, credited to Leila: name the period and the task whose performance has moved away from the norm, then ask what they think has got in the way and how you can help.
- Why it works: Working the diamond shows whether the problem is truly the employee's or, more often, something the owner messed up along the way, and since you both usually want them to succeed, finding the cause lets you fix it; people are conditioned to say yes out of fear of saying no, and the question opens a joint problem-solving conversation instead of blaming character.
- Bad → Good: Asking whether they understand, taking the yes as proof of training and holding it against them later → having them show you the checklist.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «This is the performance diamond I use for diagnosing performance problems If I» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Keep Your Employees Getting You Leads—The Performance Diamond

## Wealth alchemy

#### 15.18. Wealth alchemy and the permanent CAC
- **Rule:** In a business with a permanent, recurring or reoccurring customer base, plan against the permanent CAC — the cost of acquiring a customer times the number of customers it takes to land one who stays, counting anyone with you two-plus years as permanent — and run the chain from permanent CAC through the annual recurring revenue each permanent customer brings, on to the multiple the business trades at.
- Why it works: Only the permanent base carries the multiple, so the arbitrage between permanent CAC, annual value and multiple is where wealth is made; you get the cash and the enterprise value — not either or but and — and the enterprise value grows untaxed until the day you sell.
- Bad → Good: A free trial costs $100 to start and converts one in three, so a customer costs $300; one in three customers stays, so a permanent customer costs about $900 and at $100 a month brings $1,200 of annual recurring revenue; at 10x topline each carries about $12,000 of enterprise value, so 10,000 permanent customers cost $9 million and bring $12 million a year plus roughly $120 million of enterprise value (video, 2023–2024).
- When not to apply: The chain holds only where the permanent rate is real: the 10x topline multiple is fairly typical for B2B SaaS only with good retention metrics; the figures were worked live with round numbers and the transcript garbles the per-customer value ($122,000 where the chain gives $12,000); a transcript does not override the books.
- Anchor: «it's literally just a massive Arbitrage between the permanent cost of acquiring customers relative to the annual value relative to the Enterprise Value multiple» — video “How to Make Money So Fast It Feels ILLEGAL” (2024), the wealth alchemy part

#### 15.19. Advertise to the avatar that stays
- **Rule:** Once you have measured which share of customers become permanent and how they differ from the rest, re-aim the advertising at that avatar, even at a higher cost per customer, as long as the cost per permanent customer falls.
- Why it works: A higher hit rate on permanence beats a lower price per customer, because the permanent customer is the unit the enterprise value is built from.
- Bad → Good: One customer in ten becoming permanent, at $1,000 per permanent customer → advertising only to the avatar that looks, smells, walks and talks like the permanent ones: even if its CAC doubles, one in three becoming permanent makes a permanent customer cost $600 (video, 2023–2024).
- When not to apply: Without a measured permanent share and a visible difference that sets the permanent customers apart from the other nine, there is nothing to re-aim at.
- Anchor: «if we changed our advertising and attracted only those types of customers we would be able to get a higher hit rate on that» — video “How to Make Money So Fast It Feels ILLEGAL” (2024), the wealth alchemy part

#### 15.20. Pick the opportunity vehicle by its multiple
- **Rule:** Choose the business vehicle by what a dollar of its revenue is worth at exit, since the same acquisition cost and the same annual revenue produce very different enterprise value under a topline multiple than under a profit multiple.
- Why it works: The unit economics are identical; what differs is what a year of that revenue is worth in the vehicle, so the same effort buys a far larger asset in one than in the other.
- Bad → Good: $900 per permanent customer and $1,200 a year each in a business valued on the bottom line — $12 million of revenue, $3 million of profit, 7x EBITDA — is about $21 million of enterprise value → the same customers in a B2B SaaS at 10x topline are about $120 million (video, 2023–2024).
- When not to apply: A topline multiple that size assumes good retention metrics; and the weaker vehicle is not condemned — $9 million turned into $21 million is still a 2-point-something multiple the author would take.
- Anchor: «this is why picking a good opportunity vehicle because in both of these situations you still spent the $900 per customer» — video “How to Make Money So Fast It Feels ILLEGAL” (2024), the wealth alchemy part

## Overextension and the tests

#### 15.21. Overextension is a who problem
- **Rule:** Before adding a second location or unit, check that someone good enough to run the existing one without you is already in place, and do not expand if not; if you are already overextended, take one of the only two ways out — bring in truly impressive people, likely paid for out of your own income, or prune the tree: cut your losses, reconcentrate, get it right, then scale back out the right way.
- Why it works: Overextension is a who problem — too few good people were left behind who can run it without you; opening a second site while the first is not running well drops the first one's revenue, which was the profit, splits the star person and the owner across two places, and leaves the same money with twice the risk; overexpansion is one of the number one reasons people go out of business.
- Bad → Good: The owner moves to the new location: revenue at the first drops, and that drop was the profit, while fixed costs stay; the new site underperforms, the star operator is at the first one, the owner is split, and he earns what one location earned with twice the risk; opening a third to fix it leaves three locations, three times the risk and no money (video, 2024).
- When not to apply: It happens at all sizes but most in the $1M to $3M range, where the owner is already out of selling and delivery and wrongly assumes the business no longer needs him (see 15.22).
- Anchor: «overextension is typically a who problem which is you don't have enough good people that you left behind who can actually run it without you» — video “No BS Business Advice to Get Rich in 2026” (2024), the overextension section

#### 15.22. Owner is not CEO: the phone test and the six-month test
- **Rule:** Before taking time off or opening the next location, backfill yourself with an operator: hand them your phone for at least a month, paying an override on the business, a profit share or stock explicitly in exchange for the phone not ringing; role-play the scenarios until their answer is never to call you; then run the six-month test — six consecutive months in which the business holds its level or grows while you are not directly involved.
- Why it works: Owner and CEO are two roles that one person happens to hold; if you work every hour but no longer sell or deliver, the rest of what you do is operating the business, so when you leave it stops running as well — somebody has to operate behind you.
- Bad → Good: A pipe bursts and the operator's answer is to call you → wrong answer: all you would do is call a plumber, so they phone the plumber themselves; if it is an emergency, 911; either way, not you.
- When not to apply: The test is void if you keep working in the day-to-day through it — nothing changed, the business kept running with you as its CEO, and you only delayed the next location by six months; the name "the phone test" comes from the 2025 lecture, which never explains it, and the mechanics from a talk published in 2024.
- Anchor: «and the six-month test I was alluding to is the business has to either maintain or grow for six straight months without your direct involvement» — video “No BS Business Advice to Get Rich in 2026” (2024), the overextension section
