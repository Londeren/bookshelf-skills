# Sales Call: run, script or diagnose a close call from the intro to the next booked meeting

Open this sheet when the user wants to prepare, run, script, review or diagnose a sales call that has to close: what selling is, preparation and tone, the introduction, the nine steps of discovery, the five-step offer, the five objections and the objection loop, BAMFAM, and the objection scripts compressed to their moves.
Every rule comes from the ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), whose scripts sell ACQ's own offer to business owners: carry the moves to the user's product, not the ACQ wording.
Getting the prospect onto the call, no-shows and pipeline work are in sheet 08; the referral process after the close is in sheet 14; downsells by payment plan and by features are in sheet 11.

## Selling and preparation

#### 09.1. Selling: maximizing the likelihood a prospect buys
- **Rule:** Treat selling as maximizing the likelihood that the prospect buys, nothing more, and a sales process as the arrangement of conditions that raises that likelihood: when you plan or review a call, work on the conditions you control, not on out-arguing the prospect or on whether they were in the mood.
- Why it works: If selling is a matter of likelihood, every condition you control moves the odds, and nothing is left to whether the prospect was in the mood.
- Bad → Good: Persuading or convincing one prospect by argument → arranging conditions so that buying becomes the most likely response, the way salty food makes drinking more likely.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «First, **selling** means *maximizing the likelihood a prospect buys*. That’s it.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), What is Selling?

#### 09.2. Breathe the script: word-for-word, naturally
- **Rule:** On every call, at every level of seniority, deliver the script word-for-word, eyes closed, naturally — no paraphrase, no personal spin, no improvised reordering — and get to "naturally" by knowing the script so well it becomes natural, never by practising how to sound natural.
- Why it works: If you have to remember what to say, look for it or read it, you are not paying attention to the prospect, so you say the wrong stuff and do not close; more sales are lost from closers putting their own spin on things than from the script, whose wording came from more calls than one closer could have in a lifetime; and training to speak naturally is only training to pretend.
- Bad → Good: "Similar words in about the same order", which the author calls wrong → the script word-for-word, eyes closed, naturally.
- When not to apply: Word-for-word holds for a script inside the process it was written for: the appendix scripts sometimes seem to bend the handbook's rules because the missing variables are covered elsewhere in the process or by the brand — the inbound closing script is light on the Pain Cycle because the prospect watches one or two videos before the call, and the scripts assume prospects who already know the company — so copy a script only together with its process, and do not expect it to be calibrated for a company the prospect has never heard of.
- Anchor: «You need to deliver the script word-for-word, with your eyes closed, *naturally*. More sales are lost from people putting their own spin on things.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Breathe the Script, Important Note

#### 09.3. Memorizing the Script: the blackout drill
- **Rule:** Before going live on the phones, memorize the script by the blackout drill: print at least two copies, read the script out loud, black out one random word with a marker, read it out loud again letting memory fill the gap, and continue until the whole script is blacked out, checking yourself against the clean copy.
- Why it works: The closer has to breathe the script rather than read it, and letting memory fill each blacked-out gap forces recall instead of recognition.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**Memorizing the Script.** You will memorize the script. Here's how to do it:» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Onboarding: Schedule & Training

#### 09.4. Gametape: sell along, do not just watch
- **Rule:** When reviewing call recordings, alone or with the team, keep the script in hand and sell along with the salesman out loud, pausing and rewatching the hard parts until you nail them.
- Why it works: Watching only collects the words; the results come from roleplaying the recording, not from listening to it.
- Bad → Good: Watching gametape to pick up the words → roleplaying it out loud with the script, rewinding the hard parts until they are nailed.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Have your script with you and sell along with the salesman. This is not about just getting the words. To maximize the results, you must also roleplay it.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Onboarding: Schedule & Training, Gametape

## Tone

#### 09.5. 150–170 words per minute
- **Rule:** On every phone call keep your speed at about 150–170 words per minute (ACQ Closer Handbook, 2025), a little slower than normal conversation, and hold that range on live calls, not only in training.
- Why it works: Too fast and the prospect cannot track you or senses your stress; too slow and you sound drunk; an average range is easy to measure.
- Bad → Good: The right speed in training, then speeding up on live calls when excited or stressed → the same range live; otherwise, at worst the prospect attends to your stress instead of your words and you lose trust, attention and the sale, and at best they cannot keep track of what you say and you still lose the sale.
- When not to apply: The range is an average, not a limit on each sentence: it leaves room for natural shifts in speed.
- Anchor: «The ideal range is about 150–170 words per minute.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Tone, Talk Slower

#### 09.6. The prospect does four fifths of the talking
- **Rule:** When reviewing a call, check that you spoke about a fifth of it — on a close that goes perfectly and takes fifteen minutes, about three minutes yours and twelve the prospect's (ACQ Closer Handbook, 2025); a larger share means you are talking too much.
- Why it works: At 150–170 words per minute a 500-word script takes just over three minutes (09.5), so any talk beyond that share is the closer's own.
- Bad → Good: Occupying most of the call with your own talking → a fifteen-minute close split twelve minutes to the prospect and three to you.
- When not to apply: The figure describes a call that goes perfectly on the closer's side; it is a measure for reviewing your own call length and talk ratio.
- Anchor: «if a call goes perfect on your side and takes 15 minutes to close, then the prospect spoke for 12 minutes and you spoke for three.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Tone, Talk Slower

#### 09.7. Pauses and pitch: (…) (.) (—) and (?)
- **Rule:** Deliver the script's tone marks as printed: (…) a short pause that draws the word out, (.) a medium pause like the end of a sentence, (—) a long pause much longer than you would normally use; and wherever a question mark (?) stands, raise the pitch at the end of that word, whatever its position in the sentence.
- Why it works: Pauses focus attention on what you just said and longer pauses focus more, so the more attention a word gets the more important it becomes; a group of words does not become a question by itself — the rising pitch is what signals that you asked for information, and it packs many words into one.
- Bad → Good: The fit line of the introduction — I just want to make sure our product is a fit... And — if so — I'm happy to walk you through it — with its pauses after "fit", "and" and "if so", says "we will make you an offer only if you are a fit" without the closer ever saying it, as obvious as screaming it while staying professional and chill; the drill that proves it writes one sentence, "I didn't say he hit his wife", seven times with the pause after a different word, and the meaning changes each time.
- When not to apply: Long pauses will not feel natural at first, which the author calls normal; the longest pause of all goes after the buying question (09.8).
- Anchor: «We use three types of pauses. Short, medium, and long:» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Tone; notation repeated ACQ Closing Script

#### 09.8. The longest pause goes after the buying question
- **Rule:** After you ask for the sale and state the price — and after every re-ask inside a loop — stop talking and hold the longest pause of the call, 8 seconds or more (ACQ Closer Handbook, 2025).
- Why it works: If the prospect is not forced to address the buying question head on, they will avoid it; across five mega studies a pause of 8+ seconds after the ask raised close rates by 23 to 40%.
- Bad → Good: Filling the silence after the buying question, which the author says loses up to 40% of deals → Drop Price & STFU: state the price, then stop talking (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Tone, Bottom Line).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Five mega studies by Harvard, UCLA, and other fancy places showed a 23 to 40% increase in close rates by pausing for 8+ seconds after asking for the sale.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Tone, When You Don't Talk

## Introduction

#### 09.9. The intro: minimum questions, minimum words
- **Rule:** Open every close call with only the required questions, in the fewest possible words, in the proper tone: no small talk, no pitch, no filler, no lies, no skipped parts, no fast talk.
- Why it works: The intro frames the whole call and a thirty-minute call can be destroyed in thirty seconds; minutes spent at the start are missing at the end, and a long intro can cost the sale and the referral that sale would have brought.
- Bad → Good: A long, forced intro → three beats in the first 30–60 seconds, each closed by a confirmation question: name, company, product, recorded line and "how's it going?"; the time box, "We only have 20 minutes though. Cool if we jump in?"; the agenda — a couple of minutes on metrics to check fit, the product only if a fit, set-up if it makes sense.
- When not to apply: Fewest words never means dropping the four elements the law requires in the opening — who you are, where you are calling from, why you are calling, and that the call is recorded: the author runs the same four through an absurd opening to show that the format is not what kills calls; a bad reaction comes from the prospect not liking or trusting the rep, the company or the reason for the call.
- Anchor: «The intro is a place to ask the minimum required questions, with the minimum required words, delivered in the proper tone.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Introduction

#### 09.10. An unwatched pre-call video is watched on the call
- **Rule:** When the prospect says in the introduction that they have not watched the pre-call video, ask them to watch it now while you go on mute, instead of talking them through it.
- Why it works: The five-minute video saves about twenty minutes of the call (ACQ Closer Handbook, 2025).
- Bad → Good: Talking the prospect through what the video covers → asking them to watch the five-minute video now, you on mute, and starting once they finish.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «that's the video, it's just 5 min and will save us about 20 here today. Do you mind giving that a quick watch, I'll go on mute and we can jump in once you finish?» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), ACQ Closing Script, INTRO

## Discovery

#### 09.11. The 9 Steps of Discovery and the Pain Cycle
- **Rule:** After the introduction and before the offer, run discovery in nine steps — Current, Desired, then the Pain Cycle of Obstacle, Reason, Pull Teeth, Recap, Label, Confirm — and repeat the Pain Cycle (steps 3–8) until you hold either one label built from several related problems or several labels from several different problems, all in the prospect's own words and mapped to your solution categories; only then move to the final recap and the offer.
- Why it works: Business owners never volunteer clearly labelled problems, so they have to be pulled out, and every reason they cannot get results is another reason for them to buy.
- Bad → Good: Waiting for the prospect to hand you a problem that is already labelled, urgent and funded, which never happens → pulling it out cycle by cycle, with a list of results to ask about ready for the next cycle.
- When not to apply: Ideally one Pain Cycle is enough; run another only when the problem found is not big enough.
- Anchor: «Discovery follows a nine-step process that cycles through identifying problems until you have enough to make your offer:» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Discovery, The 9 Steps of Discovery

#### 09.12. Only ask about results you can deliver
- **Rule:** In the Obstacle step ask only about results in areas where your offer gets good results, and when the problem found there is not big enough, replace it with another such area through a further Pain Cycle.
- Why it works: Only problems you can solve are worth surfacing, and every good result you offer becomes a result of theirs you can ask about.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «We only care about problems we can solve. This means one simple thing: we only ask the prospect about results in areas we can get good results.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Discovery, Step 3: Obstacle

#### 09.13. Rapid-fire metrics, each answer recapped
- **Rule:** When discovery reaches the business metrics, announce a rapid-fire block of questions to understand the business and check fit, recap every answer before you ask the next question, and end the block with the constraint question.
- Why it works: Announcing the block as rapid fire frames it as understanding the business and checking fit, and recapping every answer keeps the prospect confirming.
- Bad → Good: ACQ's block for business owners: annual revenue, profit, main offer, how they get clients, new clients per month, spend per client, how long they have owned the business — each opened with "OK so [RECAP]" — ending on the biggest thing holding back growth, offered as a short list of labels (09.15).
- When not to apply: Only the mechanic transfers — announce the block, recap every answer, end on the constraint; the question list itself is ACQ's.
- Anchor: «Great OK so I'm gonna rapid fire through a list of questions. Just to better understand the Business.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), ACQ Closing Script, DISCOVERY

#### 09.14. Pull Teeth: tell me more, then give me an example
- **Rule:** Whenever an answer in discovery is vague, confusing or incomplete — and whenever you are unsure how to respond to an objection — ask the same two questions in the same order: ask them to tell you more, then ask them for an example.
- Why it works: Only two things matter, more information about the problem and a mapping to your solution, and an example makes the prospect connect the problem to a life experience; on objections the two questions also buy you time and make the prospect do the thinking.
- Bad → Good: Inventing elaborate handling for each kind of bad answer → the same two questions every time; "I don't think this will work for me" → tell me more about that, then give me an example, and the specific answer that comes back is corrected as a misunderstanding and closed with "Does that clear that up?" (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Looping, Pro Tip).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «You'll note, no matter the type of bad answer, *you respond the same way*. You ask them to tell you more, and then you ask them to give an example.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Discovery, Step 5: Pull Teeth; reused Looping

#### 09.15. Recap, Label & Confirm into the five labels
- **Rule:** Close out every problem a Pain Cycle surfaces in three moves — recap it in the prospect's words, label it in your words, ask whether you got it right — using only five labels: Marketing (reach, too few or poor leads, ad conversions, personal brand), Sales (cannot close, nobody can afford it, cannot find good salespeople, inconsistent sales), Product/Delivery (high churn, low lifetime value, no repeat buying), People (weak team, no help, overwhelmed, cannot fill a role), Profit (not making enough, cash-flow constrained).
- Why it works: Business owners describe their problems in a million ways, but every one can and should be mapped to a solution you offer, and the label is the word carried into the offer, so it has to be confirmed before the offer is built on it.
- Bad → Good: Recap "not getting as many leads as you want" → Label "some marketing issues" → Confirm "Does that sound about right?"
- When not to apply: The scripts read the list back to the prospect in shorter, slightly different forms — a closed list of marketing, sales, people, profit or operations when a prospect on a set call cannot name the constraint, and "Marketing? Sales? People? Profit?...Something crazy?" in the closing script.
- Anchor: «Thankfully, we only describe them in five—and these five, listed below, are the labels we use to map to our offer later.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Discovery, Steps 6–8

#### 09.16. Final Recap (Stacking the Pain)
- **Rule:** When the Pain Cycles are complete and before the transition to the offer, stack the pain: say all your recaps again as one, give the whole a single label one level up, and ask the prospect to confirm.
- Why it works: You are only sharing what you learned and making sure they agree, and that agreement is what the offer is then built on.
- Bad → Good: Three agencies tried, $50K spent, leads that did not convert, hesitant to invest again → "So it sounds like the real issue isn't just vendors, ads, and leads - it's that you need an entirely new marketing and sales process." → "Does that sound right?"
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Once you’ve completed your Pain Cycles, you need to stack the pain. To stack the pain, get your Recaps together and say them all again.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Discovery, Final Recap

## The offer

#### 09.17. The offer in five steps
- **Rule:** After the final recap, make the offer in five steps: Transition (say you think you can help and ask whether they would like to hear how), Map (their problems onto three chosen solutions, 09.18), Stack (list every solution and benefit pair so far, three times in all), Ask (whether they are ready to move forward, only after the third stack is confirmed), Drop Price & STFU (state the price and stop talking, 09.8).
- Why it works: Typical salespeople go straight to pitching and abandon the pain points they just collected; mapping them instead crafts a personalized offer on the spot.
- Bad → Good: A list of benefits followed by a request for money → the stack built up: transition and permission, solution 1 with its assurance, benefit and a confirmation question, then solutions 1+2 restated, then 1+2+3, then the whole stack repeated from three angles, then the buying question, the price and the card menu.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «To make the offer, we have to transition from discovery smoothly. We do this after we recapped all the problems.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Offer, How to Make The Offer

#### 09.18. Map: Problem, Solution, Assure, Benefit, Confirm
- **Rule:** Map each problem in five beats — remind them of the problem, say how you will solve it specifically (think feature), assure it (why that thing is great, to decrease risk), state the benefit, ask for confirmation — across exactly three solutions: break a single problem into micro problems during discovery so it fills all three, and group more than three problems so one solution answers several.
- Why it works: Prospects change but the solutions stay the same, so labels, assurances and benefits can be prepared ahead of time and the closer never has to invent anything on the fly.
- Bad → Good: For a "Not enough leads" problem: Solution — you get to ask questions of the director of marketing; Assure — he leads ad and content strategy for the companies they advise; Benefit — he can help you improve your funnel conversion, content and ads; Confirm — "Do you think that would help?"
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Connect their problems to our solutions, assurances, benefits, then confirm that it would help.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Offer, How to Make The Offer

#### 09.19. Two minutes, about 320 words
- **Rule:** Deliver the whole offer in about two minutes — at 150–170 words per minute, roughly 320 words (ACQ Closer Handbook, 2025) — and spend those words on your solution stack.
- Why it works: That is plenty of words if you know what you are doing; needing more means you are wasting words.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The budget covers the offer section of the call only.
- Anchor: «Remember, talking between 150–170 words per minute gives you about 320 words to make your offer.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Offer

#### 09.20. Plug and chug: nothing invented on the call
- **Rule:** Before close calls, write out your labels, solutions, assurances and benefits, so that during the offer you only plug the prospect's problems into them and compose nothing live.
- Why it works: Prospects change but the solutions stay the same; improvising risks saying something dumb, wrecking your tone, or both.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source; the plugged-in wording of one mapped problem is the example of 09.18.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «With proper preparation you need only to “plug and chug”. You should never have to come up with anything on the fly and risk saying something dumb, screwing up your tone, or both.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Offer

#### 09.21. Never ask "do you have any questions?"
- **Rule:** Never ask the prospect "do you have any questions?", in the offer or anywhere else on the call.
- Why it works: The question asks the prospect for reasons not to buy; the author benches a rep who asks it and makes an example of them.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «If you ever ask “do you have any questions?” you will be benched and made an example of. I do not recommend asking prospects for reasons not to buy.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Offer, PRO TIP

## Objections and looping

#### 09.22. The 5 Objections to Buying
- **Rule:** Whenever the prospect is asked to buy and does anything other than buy, sort the reason into one of five buckets and use that bucket's handling: Time — no time to get the value (09.28); Money — cannot or will not pay the price (09.29); Decision-Maker — needs someone else's permission (09.30); Preference — expected something different or would rather do something else (09.31); Stall — must wait before buying (09.32).
- Why it works: A refusal can be phrased a million ways, and a million cannot be prepared for, while five buckets can.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The buckets are not equal: by the author's own count Money accounts for roughly 50% of objections (ACQ Closer Handbook, 2025).
- Anchor: «People may say a million different things to avoid buying. But when you chunk it up there are only *five we care about*.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Objections, The 5 Objections to Buying

#### 09.23. Handling the First Objection: Confirming Value, then the triage question
- **Rule:** On the first objection of the call do not answer it: acknowledge it, ask permission for one question, ask whether doing the product could help get them closer to their goal, and then, whatever the answer, ask a triage question for the biggest thing holding them back and their main concern, isolating it by asking whether anything other than that is holding them back; only then start looping (09.24).
- Why it works: You need to know whether the prospect thinks the product is valuable before playing objection whack-a-mole, and the triage question makes them abandon their reflex objection so the one worth handling is isolated.
- Bad → Good: Answering the first objection as stated → acknowledge ("I completely hear you"), ask permission for one question, then the value question and the triage question; the answer to the value question sets the branch — a yes with a new objection is confirmed and isolated, an uncertain "Yeah.." is probed ("you don't seem super confident"), a certain yes is asked what makes them say that, and "I'm not sure" is probed for the main concern (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, ACQ Looping Script).
- When not to apply: Only the first objection is handled this way; once value is confirmed, every later objection goes straight into the loop (09.24).
- Anchor: «Before getting into objection whack-a-mole, we need to know something very important: Do they think our product is valuable?» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Looping, Handling the First Objection

#### 09.24. The Objection Loop: acknowledge, address, ask
- **Rule:** Handle every objection after value is confirmed in three moves — acknowledge and agree with it, address it, ask for the sale again immediately — and repeat the loop on each further objection until the prospect buys or the call runs out of time; an answer that does not end in a fresh ask has not handled the objection.
- Why it works: Adding to their reasoning and giving them the chance to buy again at once saves them embarrassment; the salespeople who ask for the buy the most times get the most closes, and prospects eventually run out of objections while you never run out of loops.
- Bad → Good: A good answer to an objection that stops there → "This is just too expensive" → "Super reasonable" → weigh the cost against a year of trial and error → "Perfect, let's get started then." (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Objection Looping In Action).
- When not to apply: The loop assumes that anything short of a clear no is the normal fear of doing something new, so a prospect who says outright they do not want the offer is outside it; when time runs out, book the next call (09.27) rather than loop on; and heavy looping signals a mistake earlier in the script, since the great majority of sales close on the first or second ask.
- Anchor: «Looping is a technique to overcome objections by acknowledging their objection, presenting your additions, and then *looping* the prospect back to the offer.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Looping, The Right Way to Handle Objections

#### 09.25. AGREE… AND…, never "But"
- **Rule:** Open every loop by agreeing with the objection and join your addition with "and"; "but" never appears in the acknowledgement.
- Why it works: "But" works like a big fat eraser that tells the prospect to ignore everything before it, which disrespects them and costs the sale; agreement disarms a prospect who expects an argument and buys you more chances to ask again.
- Bad → Good: Joining the acknowledgement with "but" → "It's a lot... I agree... and... I think it'll be totally worth it for your business... do you agree?"
- When not to apply: The author goes further in a personal note he flags as controversial: that the content of the answer after the agreement matters almost not at all, since his top closes ("that is the exact reason you should come", "most people just make it work anyways", "best case you xyz, worst case you zyw") only agree and ask again; he frames it as wanting the closer rich rather than right and as his own view rather than a team standard, so keep the prepared answers of 09.28–09.32.
- Anchor: «“And,” never “But”. Think of “But” like a big fat eraser. It means ignore all the words before it.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Looping

#### 09.26. Address the content, never the objection itself
- **Rule:** However silly or false the objection, never insult, challenge or catch out the objection itself; address only its content, with your tone tightly controlled.
- Why it works: A prospect made to feel stupid, cornered or caught out acquires a better objection — that the closer is an asshole — and calls off the deal.
- Bad → Good: "Can't afford it" answered by pointing at the BMW and the new deck, until the prospect says "I think we're done here" and the door slams → the same goal with no confrontation: "That's a fair point", then ask what would have to happen for this to make financial sense.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «no matter how silly the objection, you must *never ever* insult the objection itself. You will *only address its content*.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Looping, The Right Way to Handle Objections

## BAMFAM

#### 09.27. BAMFAM (Book A Meeting From A Meeting)
- **Rule:** When a call cannot close today — a time constraint, a decision-maker the prospect depends on, or a banking delay on a very high ticket — book the next call before this one ends: say the call is running long and that you think this could really help, offer two named times, restate the agreed day and time, send the invite while still on the call and have them confirm receipt, then send a follow-up text right away; with another call right after, keep a three-minute buffer to do this.
- Why it works: Both of you are on the call now; if you will not book a call while on a call, you have no chance of booking it by text afterwards.
- Bad → Good: "We'll follow up offline" → a specific time on the table: "I'll have to see" → fifteen minutes tomorrow; "Let me call you back" → today or tomorrow, at one of two named times.
- When not to apply: The banking-delay reason applies to very high ticket only; the setter's BAMFAM between the first and the second call is 08.18.
- Anchor: «And that's totally okay. Sometimes closing takes more than one call. When it does, secure a time for the next call while on the current call.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), BAMFAM

## Objection scripts as moves

#### 09.28. A time objection is a priority objection
- **Rule:** Answer "not enough time" as a priority objection, never as a real limit: find lower-value things the prospect spends time on and get them to agree those are worth less than taking the time for what you offer.
- Why it works: Lack of time is never the real objection, only a sign that the offer is not a high enough priority.
- Bad → Good: Taking "This isn't a good time" at face value → "That makes sense", then restructuring the commitment so it only takes 30 minutes a day and asking again (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Objection Looping In Action).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «This is solved by finding lower value things they spend time on... and *getting them to agree they're not as valuable as taking the time to do what we're offering*.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Objections, The 5 Objections to Buying

#### 09.29. Money: the cost of the problem against the cost of the solution
- **Rule:** Answer a money objection — the prospect cannot or will not pay the price — by first isolating it (if they had the money today, would anything else stop them from starting?) and then showing how much the problem costs compared with the solution, working from facts such as monthly cash flow and cash on hand, with a recap in between.
- Why it works: The hypothetical proves money is the only objection left before any work is done on it, and from there money is a matter of facts rather than feelings.
- Bad → Good: "It's really expensive" → agree it is a lot and make that the reason it will work — a high price makes the buyer care and act; would a $500 version even seem valuable? — add a line on price against value that the author attributes to Warren Buffett (its wording is not carried here), ask whether they see the value, and close; "I don't know if I see the value" → ask what the free content they already consumed has done for them: growth proves the source works and the product is its tactical version, no growth means they could not apply it, which the product supplies — both branches end in an ask (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Money Objections).
- When not to apply: When every payment route has been offered and refused, stop pushing payment and book the next meeting (09.27) — the author's stop condition on money, against looping until the buy; payment plans and downsells are in sheet 11.
- Anchor: «#2 Money - The prospect says they are not able or not willing to pay the price.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Objections

#### 09.30. Decision-Maker: three steps
- **Rule:** Solve a decision-maker objection in three steps: ahead of time, find out whether the prospect needs someone's permission and, if so, get that person on the call; ask whether the decision-maker would be fine with this based on past approvals and, if approval would come anyway, encourage deciding now; otherwise book a follow-up call with the decision-maker.
- Why it works: If they would get approval anyway there is no reason to wait, and if they would not, the decision-maker has to be in the conversation.
- Bad → Good: "I need to talk to my spouse or business partner" → isolate the partner as the only blocker, ask what their biggest concern would be and loop that concern as its own objection, offer the reversal — if the partner hates the idea, they call you and you make sure they are taken care of — and ask for the order; if they still need to talk to them, BAMFAM that conversation (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Decision-Maker).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «We solve this in three steps. First, we figure out if they need permission ahead of time. If so, we get the decision-maker on the call.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Objections, #3 Decision-Maker

#### 09.31. Preference: three steps
- **Rule:** Solve a preference objection — they expected something different or would rather do something else — in three steps: remind them of the problem they want solved, draw out what their current way costs them in the short, medium and long term, then explain why your way is ultimately to their benefit.
- Why it works: The prospect has to realize that if you change the variables that create the outcome, you change the outcome.
- Bad → Good: "I went to a similar [PRODUCT] and I didn't like it" → first diagnose the bad experience (how many people were there, did they get personalised help from the speakers, was it just not tactical enough), then ask permission to describe your structure, open by ruling out the thing they disliked, walk through how it actually works, check their confidence and ask "Is there any reason we shouldn't do this?" (example — ACQ Closer Handbook, Preferences).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «To solve this, first we remind them of the problem they want solved. Second, we pull out the short-, medium-, and long-term consequences of their current ways.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Objections, #4 Preference

#### 09.32. Stall: turn "I need to think about it" into a forced choice
- **Rule:** Meet a stall — the prospect says they must wait before buying — by asking permission to be upfront, saying that almost everyone locks in and the exceptions come down to one of two reasons (they do not see the value, or logistics), checking the value in passing, and asking whether it is number two or something crazy; then loop the real objection that comes back (09.24).
- Why it works: A stall with no stated reason becomes a forced choice from a short list, which produces the real objection the loop can handle.
- Bad → Good: "I always take 24 hours to make a decision" → respect the rule, ask whether new information is making them reconsider, share that the large owners you watch decide quickly and logically once they see value, confirm they see it, and ask whether it would be absolutely crazy to break the rule; "It's a big commitment / I need time" → name the decision as big and the nervousness, then ask whether, having paid, they would let things fall through the cracks or come in and implement — the nervousness becomes the sign they will take it seriously.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Yeah so typically almost everyone is ready to lock in their spot… and if not it’s really because of 1 or 2 reasons.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Stall Objections
