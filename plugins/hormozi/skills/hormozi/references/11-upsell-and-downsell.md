# Upsell and Downsell: what to offer after a yes and after a no

## Upsells: the rules and the Classic Upsell

#### 11.1. Upsell whatever comes next; the profit is in the later offers
- **Rule:** After every purchase, make a next offer that solves the problem the purchase just revealed (more of what they got, a better version, or something new and complementary), plan the profit on the second, third and fourth offers rather than on the first, and when two upsells are available offer the more profitable one first.
- Why it works: When an offer solves a problem, another appears, so the next offer sells into a need the last one created; the thing you sell the most is often not the thing you make the most profit on, since upsells often make the majority of the profit, and the order of offers decides which profit you capture before the customer stops buying.
- Bad → Good: Counting on a $2.00 burger that makes $0.25 of profit, which would need roughly 10,000 burgers a day to survive → asking "Do you want fries with that?" for another $0.75, making it a meal with a drink to take profit from $0.25 to $2.00 (8×), then supersizing for a buck more to reach $3.00 (example — $100M Money Models, Section III: Upsell Offers).
- When not to apply: The claim is that the front offer is not always where the profit is, not that it never is: the mistake is building the business on the assumption that it always is.
- Anchor: «Anytime you offer something *next*, you upsell. Upsells play a key role» — $100M Money Models (2025), Upsell Offers Conclusion

#### 11.2. The Classic Upsell: "You can't have X without Y"
- **Rule:** Offer the fix for the customer's next problem at the moment they become aware of it, framed as "You can't have X without Y"; close with "You don't want anything else, do you?" (Say No To Say Yes), and add closing bonuses one at a time (Surprise And Delight), giving every buyer all of them, including those who said yes before the later ones were offered.
- Why it works: The core offer solves one problem and creates another, and current customers always have a higher chance of buying than strangers, so timed right they upsell themselves; people are trained to answer no to that closing question, and here the no is agreement to what you just offered; bonuses added one at a time surprise and delight, and giving everyone the same thing means nobody feels left out later.
- Bad → Good: A fur shop advertises free earmuffs with coat storage; when customers come to collect the muffs and drop off their coats, they are told the muffs will be stored as well for $30, with the question whether they want to store anything else, so they pay to store what they were just given free: the free bonus created the problem the upsell solves. Other chains: car wash → sealant; bicycle → helmet → lights → puncture-resistant tires ("you can't have a bike without a helmet"); exercise course → nutrition course, since no amount of exercise makes up for a bad diet.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «The Classic Upsell offers a solution to the customer's next problem *the» — $100M Money Models (2025), The Classic Upsell, Description

#### 11.3. The Faster People Get Access To Stuff, The More They'll Value It
- **Rule:** Make an upsell available as soon as possible, ideally placing it in the customer's hands before they have said yes, and integrate the next thing you want to sell into the delivery of the thing they just bought.
- Why it works: The longer it takes to access something, the less value it has in the moment, and it is far harder to give something back than to say no; built into delivery, the next thing prompts the customer to ask for it.
- Bad → Good: Meal plans that included supplement suggestions made people ask about supplements; training that suggested software led business owners to buy it.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «upsell, make it available as soon as you can. Bonus points if you put it» — $100M Money Models (2025), The Classic Upsell, Important Notes

#### 11.4. Book-A-Meeting-From-A-Meeting (BAMFAM)
- **Rule:** End every appointment by scheduling the next one, so the customer leaves knowing when they will see you next and why.
- Why it works: The more times you can upsell, the more people you will upsell, and the more people you upsell, the more money you make; the same move on an unclosed sales call is 09.27, and between the first and the second call 08.18.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «by scheduling the next appointment. Don't let them leave without» — $100M Money Models (2025), The Classic Upsell, Important Notes

#### 11.5. Upsell Guarantees, Warranties, and Insurance
- **Rule:** Instead of including a guarantee, warranty or insurance for free, sell it as an add-on at 5–50% of the price, offered after the customer has agreed to buy.
- Why it works: You are buying and selling risk: when the fee brings in more than fixing or replacing the product costs, it is nearly all profit with no new operations, and a claim is covered by delivering again rather than by refunding.
- Bad → Good: An art studio that used to replace damaged portraits for free → now 30% of customers pay an extra 10% for that cover, which is pure profit (2025); the playbook version is one line after the purchase, asking whether they would like the standard warranty added, rung up as an extra product and checked at claim time (example — the Pricing playbook, Pricing Play #10: Guarantee and Warranty Upsells).
- When not to apply: Price it only where you know your claim rate and your cost to replace: the guarantee's revenue has to exceed the cost of honoring it; the one-line checkout version works especially well in main street and traditional businesses.
- Anchor: «*So instead of doing it for free, just add 5--50% onto the price in» — $100M Money Models (2025), The Classic Upsell, Important Notes

## The Menu Upsell

#### 11.6. The Menu Upsell: unsell, prescribe, A/B, card on file
- **Rule:** When several offers are available, run the Menu Upsell in this order: unsell what the customer doesn't need, prescribe what they do need, ask their preference between A and B, then settle payment by offering to put it on the card on file.
- Why it works: Each step replaces the question of whether to buy: crossing out what they don't need builds goodwill and highlights what they do, prescribing explains how to use it as if they already have it, an A/B question removes the option of not buying, and the card on file lowers the hidden costs of buying (picking a card, taking it out, being reminded of ugly past buying decisions).
- Bad → Good: Dog food: unsell the small bag, the puppy food and the separate vitamins the food already contains; prescribe a joint chew at each meal and a heartworm wafer every 90 days, and book next month's visit now; ask beef or chicken flavour; close on the card on file.
- When not to apply: The A/B step needs several offers that solve the same problem; where offering a choice is inconvenient and you have only one thing that solves the problem, the Prescription step is the one to use (11.8).
- Anchor: «Menu Upsells combine up to four tactics: Unselling,» — $100M Money Models (2025), Menu Upsell, Description and Summary Points

#### 11.7. Unselling
- **Rule:** Before prescribing, cross out what the customer doesn't need, choosing the lower-margin items to cross out where appropriate, and encourage employees to unsell and help customers "game the system" on purpose.
- Why it works: Going out of your way to cross out what the customer doesn't need builds enough goodwill to upsell what they do need; unselling lower-margin items incentivizes higher-margin upsells; employees have inside knowledge, and using it to show customers how to get the most value means everyone wins.
- Bad → Good: He kept products in stock just to cross them out: after confirming a customer wasn't trying to gain weight he crossed the weight-gainer off her sheet, crossed off the testosterone booster the same way, prescribed what was left, and she bought without hesitation.
- When not to apply: Unselling is for what the customer doesn't need, not for what you have run out of: when an item has sold out, still make the sale, take payment and change the delivery expectation, which lets you sell far more selection without carrying inventory.
- Anchor: «*Unselling.* You unsell by telling customers what they don't need so» — $100M Money Models (2025), Menu Upsell, Description

#### 11.8. The Prescription Upsell
- **Rule:** When offering a choice is inconvenient and you have only one thing that solves the problem, don't ask whether they want it: explain how it integrates with what they already bought and give personalized, detailed instructions for getting the most value from it.
- Why it works: Explaining how to use it as if they already have it removes the option of not buying; detailed, personalized instructions upsell more people than vague, general suggestions.
- Bad → Good: Step-by-step dosing instructions written on scratch paper for one insistent customer → at the next appointment, written on the order form itself beside each item, with how much to take and when, closed by pointing to the instructions and offering to charge the card on file; thirty-day profits rose from then on.
- When not to apply: Where several products solve the same problem and a choice is easy to offer, ask A or B instead (11.9).
- Anchor: «important components. First, you have to explain how it integrates with» — $100M Money Models (2025), Menu Upsell, Description

#### 11.9. The A/B Upsell
- **Rule:** Where several products solve the same problem, ask which of two the customer prefers, A or B, instead of asking whether they want one.
- Why it works: When you give people the option not to buy, some don't buy; either answer to an A/B question results in an upsell.
- Bad → Good: Nineteen nutrition consults with no sales → the pitch dropped for a question about the customer's own breakfast shake, chocolate or vanilla, which sold at once; the next item was offered as kiwi or strawberry lemonade with the seller's own stated preference attached, the close was the card on file, and the next twenty customers in a row bought.
- When not to apply: It works for multiple offers that solve the same problem; with only one, use the Prescription Upsell (11.8).
- Anchor: «asking their preference. Instead of asking ***if*** customers wanted to» — $100M Money Models (2025), Menu Upsell, Description

## The Anchor Upsell

#### 11.10. The Anchor Upsell: anchor, gasp, rescue, main offer, payment
- **Rule:** When a cheaper main offer has the same core functions as a premium one, present the premium anchor first at 5–10× the main offer's price, get "The Gasp", come to the rescue by asking whether they care about what makes it premium, present the main offer, then ask how they want to pay.
- Why it works: After a premium version at 5–10× the price, the main offer looks like a much better deal, so more people buy it; anchored customers spend more than they normally would, and some still buy the expensive thing; the bigger the gasp, the more they bought.
- Bad → Good: A shopper with a $500 budget tries on a suit that turns out to be $16,000; at his visible gasp the owner asks whether he cares much about the designer and drapes a second suit at $2,200, pulled before the reaction because he knew the gasp was coming, which is bought along with $300 of socks, handkerchief and shirt that all seem cheap afterwards.
- When not to apply: If the customer doesn't gasp, they probably find the premium offer reasonable, so just offer to put it on the card on file; and anchor only with something you are actually willing to deliver: if someone buying it would stress you, raise its price until a purchase makes you smile (the Pricing playbook, Pricing Play #9: Ultra High Ticket Anchor).
- Anchor: «● Present anchor. Get gasp. Come to the rescue. Present core offer. Ask» — $100M Money Models (2025), Anchor Upsell, Description and Summary

#### 11.11. Same primary features, different secondary features
- **Rule:** Give the main offer the same primary features as the premium anchor and change only secondary features.
- Why it works: Most people just want the primary thing, a suit, while the material and designer are secondary, so the primary features at a fifth of the price make the main offer a great deal, and some customers still buy the premium version.
- Bad → Good: Lawn care: the owner's cell number, fancy mulch, natural pest control and bi-weekly maintenance at $1,000 a week against the team's number, generic mulch, normal pest control and the same bi-weekly maintenance at $200; a newsletter: all back issues plus new issues 24 hours early at $199 a month against new issues on time at $19.
- When not to apply: For the anchor to work at all, the premium offer should also be 5–10× the price of the main offer (11.10).
- Anchor: «● The main offer and the premium offer should have the same primary» — $100M Money Models (2025), Anchor Upsell, Summary

#### 11.12. If You Treat The Anchor Like A Fake, So Will The Customer
- **Rule:** Offer the premium anchor as something you actually want the customer to buy, and move to the main offer only once they pause, hesitate or request something else.
- Why it works: If you go through the motions, the person never really considers the anchor because you never really offered it, and you lose trust and waste time.
- Bad → Good: Glossing over the premium offer, going through the motions, then reporting that anchoring doesn't work → selling the premium in earnest and moving on only at a pause, a hesitation or a request for something else.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «hesitate, or ask for something else, do you move to the next thing.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Anchor Upsell, Important Notes

## The Rollover Upsell

#### 11.13. Who gets a Rollover Upsell
- **Rule:** Aim Rollover Upsells, which apply part or all of what a customer already paid as credit toward your next offer, at four groups: old customers six or more months since their last purchase, upset customers as an alternative to a refund, other people's upset customers, and regular customers.
- Why it works: Previous customers are still customers; rolling over beats refunding when you did a bad job; and a competitor's unhappy customer becomes a hot lead once you credit what they paid elsewhere.
- Bad → Good: 200 personalized winback videos offering old customers $4,000 of credit converted about 20% and produced roughly $1,900,000 of annual revenue from one day of recording (2025); for other people's customers, scrape contact details from negative product reviews and credit whatever payments they have left with that vendor toward a longer agreement with you.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «● Who to upsell: old customers, upset customers, other people's upset» — $100M Money Models (2025), Rollover Upsell, Description and Summary Points

#### 11.14. How To Price Your Rollover Upsell: at least 4× the credit
- **Rule:** Roll the credit into something more expensive, priced at least 4× the credit, so that applying the whole first purchase discounts the new offer by 25% at most.
- Why it works: A discounted offer makes money only if profit remains once the discount is applied; the rules of discounting apply, and bigger discounts make less profit per sale but get more sales.
- Bad → Good: Rolling the credit into something that just gives back what they already paid for → rolling it into an offer priced at least 4× the credit, where the whole credit is a discount of 25% at most.
- When not to apply: A rollover that leaves no profit after the credit is not worth making; 4× the credit is the author's floor.
- Anchor: «● Price your next offer *at least* 4x higher than the credit. This makes» — $100M Money Models (2025), Rollover Upsell, Summary Points

#### 11.15. Do Rollover Upsells before refunding
- **Rule:** Before issuing any refund, offer the purchase as credit: toward a do-over when the job was done badly, or toward something different if that is what they want.
- Why it works: Doing rollovers before refunding has saved the author tons of customers and cash.
- Bad → Good: A customer paid $200 for a teeth cleaning and didn't think their teeth got whiter → instead of a refund, they are told they need more to get more, and the $200 is credited toward a whitening package of multiple sessions, an at-home kit and several deep cleanings.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**Do Rollover Upsells** ***before*** **refunding.** This has saved me» — $100M Money Models (2025), Rollover Upsell, Important Notes

#### 11.16. Spread the credit, and make it one-time only
- **Rule:** Apply rollover credit as a monthly discount spread over the new term rather than as free time up front, let the customer keep the discounted rate only while they complete the credited payments, and make the credit a one-time offer they must take the moment it is presented.
- Why it works: People fall off if they don't pay something, so a discount stretched over the long haul keeps skin in the game and keeps the recurring revenue going instead of restarting from zero; tying the rate to the credited payments gets them to buy and to stick, like price protection; and they don't get to sleep on a one-time offer, while whoever passes can still pay full price later.
- Bad → Good: Alex's challenge winners put their $600 toward three months of membership and churned before the first out-of-pocket payment, in effect "buy six weeks, get three months free" → Justin rolled the same winnings into a year-long membership at $50 off per month, so winners started paying immediately and still got their money back over the year; on a $200-a-month product, $600 of credit becomes $50 off for twelve months, and the customer keeps paying $150 a month (example — $100M Money Models, Win Your Money Back).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «answer. "We just give them fifty bucks off per month for a year."» — $100M Money Models (2025), ch. Rollover Upsell

## Downsells: the rules

#### 11.17. The Rules of Downselling: another offer, built as a trade
- **Rule:** After a no, make another offer (any offer made after a no is a downsell, changing how they pay or what they get), make every concession a trade in which you get something for what you give, and personalize the offer rather than pressure the person.
- Why it works: A no is a no to this offer, not to you or to every offer, and it is an opportunity to find out what they really want and profit from it; downselling works by finding combinations of giving and getting until there is a match.
- Bad → Good: Treating a no as a rejection of everything and giving up → on a price objection, knocking $100 off in exchange for four things (a review on all review sites, a video testimonial, public posts at the beginning, middle and end of the program showing progress, and introductions to two friends who would want to do this too), closed with "Deal?"; the advertising is worth more than $100 to the seller and less than $100 to the buyer (example — $100M Money Models, Feature Downsells).
- When not to apply: A downsell never sells the same stuff at a lower price: that is discounting, not downselling (11.19).
- Anchor: «First, we cover my rules of downselling---*they apply to all my downsell» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section IV: Downsell Offers, The Rules of Downselling

#### 11.18. Offer The Same Things In New Ways
- **Rule:** Build downsells only out of what the business already sells, in new payment terms or new combinations, never out of new products created so that everyone has something to buy.
- Why it works: Otherwise you create a hundred businesses' worth of products and problems; downselling is less about having 100 products to offer and more about having 100 ways to offer your product.
- Bad → Good: One personal training service sold as one, two, three or four sessions per week instead of a new product for every budget (10.37).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «just think of downselling more like a hundred ways to offer the stuff» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section IV: Downsell Offers, The Rules of Downselling

#### 11.19. Don't Drop Your Price Just To Get Somebody To Buy
- **Rule:** Never lower the price of the same offer in the moment to win a sale: someone who wants to pay less now gets a payment plan (11.20), someone who wants to pay less overall gets a Feature Downsell (11.29), and there is no negotiating with "business terrorists", people demanding the same thing for less.
- Why it works: Dropping your price is not downselling, it is discounting; customers talk about price, and when someone finds out another customer got the same thing for less "just because", you upset people and create an ethical problem.
- Bad → Good: A lead said she couldn't afford it → the seller asked when she got paid, offered half down now and half on the first, then a third down today across three payments, then asked what she actually could do; she could pay in full on the first, so her card was taken and charged on the second, and the price never moved, only when the money arrived (example — $100M Money Models, Payment Plan Downsells).
- When not to apply: A planned price test is a different thing: a set price offered to a set number of people, decided in advance, is not charging somebody less in the moment because you were scared of losing the sale. On a one-on-one sale the same rule reads: add a bonus, never a discount (03.12).
- Anchor: «do, don't change the price just to get someone to buy because...» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section IV: Downsell Offers, The Rules of Downselling

## Payment Plan Downsells

#### 11.20. The Payment Plan Downsell: seven steps from most cash up front to least
- **Rule:** When a prospect rejects the offer on price, run up to seven steps in this order and stop at the step where they buy: (1) reward paying in full rather than punish paying over time, quoting the price with interest included and offering prepayment as the discount; (2) third-party financing, credit card or layaway; (3) half now, half later; (4) check that they still want the thing (11.22); (5) three payments; (6) evenly spread payments; (7) a free trial.
- Why it works: A huge percentage of the time "it costs too much" really means "it costs too much up front", so a payment plan gets the buyer the way a discount would while still collecting full price over time; the first number you say anchors the whole conversation, and fewer, bigger payments cancel less: in Profitwell's churn data from 14,000 businesses, monthly billing had 10.7% monthly cancellations, quarterly 5% and annual 2% (2025).
- Bad → Good: Quoting $10 now or $15 over time because of $5 in interest → quoting $15 with the interest already included and offering prepayment as the way to get it for $10 and save $5, which is what most people do: the same math, a friendlier offer, and the full price becomes the price anchor.
- When not to apply: Payment plans lose money when people cancel before you turn a profit and, most of all, when would-be full payers switch to a plan and cancel early: after adding plans, the close rate should rise while the percentage of appointments paying in full stays the same; if paid-in-fulls drop, the plans are costing you.
- Anchor: «My Payment Plan Downsell process takes up to seven steps. The process» — $100M Money Models (2025), Payment Plan Downsells, Description and Summary Points

#### 11.21. "Seesaw" Downselling
- **Rule:** If you prefer fewer steps or have less experienced salespeople, use Seesaw Downselling instead of the seven steps (11.20): shift gradually from paid-in-full toward equal payments, framing prepayment as the way to a discount and zero monthly payments, and adjusting the down payment until the monthly rate suits them.
- Why it works: It frames the payment plan as the negative option and highlights the benefits of prepaying while still rewarding bigger down payments with lower monthly payments, and it makes the sale a team effort rather than a negotiation.
- Bad → Good: Asking for the full amount → asking whether they would rather have giant monthly payments or tiny ones; on "tiny", stating the normal price and the huge discount with zero monthly payments if they prepay today; if they can't afford it, adjusting the down payment until they get a monthly rate they like; if they still say no, asking whether they still want the product and, if they do, walking through the options together.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «● "Seesaw" Downselling gradually shifts from paid-in-full to equal» — $100M Money Models (2025), Payment Plan Downsells, Important Notes

#### 11.22. The Temperature Check: 8 or above, keep going
- **Rule:** After two changes in a row are refused, in payment plans or in features, ask how badly they want the thing on a scale from 1 to 10; at 8 or above, continue down the payment plan options; a 7 or below gets the question "Why not a 10?" or "What would a 10 look like?" and a move to a different offer that recombines the features.
- Why it works: Someone who doesn't want the thing won't be satisfied by any payment plan, so effort goes only where the desire is; a 7 or below tells you they fit something else, which is what Feature Downsells are for, and alternating payment-plan and feature downsells in the same sale makes you very difficult to refuse.
- Bad → Good: After the half-down option fails, the seller pauses for the 1–10 question: at 8 or above, "don't worry, we're gonna figure out a way to make this happen" and the payment ladder goes on; at 7 or below, "why not a 10?" and a move to selling something different.
- When not to apply: Payment-plan downselling applies only to prospects who want the product; wanting it is the precondition this check tests.
- Anchor: «wanna do this?"* If they say 8 or above, keep offering payment plans and» — $100M Money Models (2025), Payment Plan Downsells, Example Of Payment Plan Downsell Process

#### 11.23. Paycheck dates, re-run declines, built-in upsells
- **Rule:** Once a payment plan is sold, schedule the payments on the customer's paycheck dates rather than as monthly instalments, re-run a declined card a few times the same day, and periodically offer plan customers the original prepay discount if they clear the balance.
- Why it works: Most people get paid every two weeks, which boosts 30-day profit far more than monthly payments, and charging on payday means fewer declines; paychecks get deposited at different times, and re-running recoups about a third of declined payments (2025; a step the author learned from John, an early mentor); customers forget they still have the prepay option, and if you incentivize people to pay faster, they pay faster.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «paid. Fair enough?"* I like scheduling payments off of paychecks since» — $100M Money Models (2025), Payment Plan Downsells, Example Of Payment Plan Downsell Process

## Trial With Penalty

#### 11.24. Trial With Penalty for recurring offers; call it a Free Trial
- **Rule:** Use Trial With Penalty (the customer gets the product free so long as they meet your terms and pays only if they don't) as the downsell for recurring products and services in businesses where the customer has to do work to get results; for physical products and one-time services use Pay Less Now or Pay More Later instead; in customer-facing language, call it a Free Trial.
- Why it works: The two offers differ in what happens after the trial period, so they fit different delivery shapes; and anything that reveals the penalty scares and confuses people, because no one wants to be penalized.
- Bad → Good: Naming the offer anything that reveals the penalty → calling it a Free Trial and, if asked why it works this way, answering "This is just how we've always done it" or "People get the best results this way."
- When not to apply: It is neither an ordinary free trial ("here's my thing, see if you like it") nor Win Your Money Back, which pays money back when the customer meets the terms; the author has only made it work where the customer has to do work to get results, and asks readers to tell him if they find other types of business it works for.
- Anchor: «one-time services. And I use Trial With Penalty as a downsell for» — $100M Money Models (2025), Trial With Penalty, Important Notes

#### 11.25. How To Downsell The Trial
- **Rule:** Offer the trial only after the customer has made clear they won't take your first offer, then: always get a credit card (if they still refuse after "that's just how we've always done it", end the conversation); get them to agree to stay long term if the program works for them (no agreement, no trial); explain the fees only after getting the card, with the customer initialling separately next to each fee clause; and explain all criteria up front, making the check-in meetings required terms and naming the benefit of each.
- Why it works: As a downsell the trial changes only what they pay today, not what they pay in total, and it saves people otherwise lost to a no; the penalty exists only if there is a card to charge; there is no point giving a trial to someone who won't stay, and setting the expectation keeps you from promising a quick fix you can't ethically deliver; fees explained before the card bring more resistance, and the separate initials force the sales team to explain them; the check-ins are the upsell opportunities.
- Bad → Good: One offer loses everyone who says no: closing three of ten → downselling another four onto a Trial With Penalty and upselling three of those after the trial, so three sales become six; the fees, explained after the card, are framed as keeping the customer on track: both sides do their part, a miss costs a little fee that gets them back on track, and following through gets them all of it for free.
- When not to apply: Where asking for a card against a free offer meets resistance, charge a very low first-month price instead of nothing, so the card is likely to go through once automatic billing begins; and the trial can also serve as an attraction offer, as the HR software company in the author's story used it, though his own placement is last.

#### 11.26. Trial terms that activate and retain
- **Rule:** Take the trial's terms from the Win Your Money Back criteria, the actions that activate and retain a customer; bill a small fee for each missed criterion rather than one lump fee on the first miss; and after a billed miss, offer a way to make it up.
- Why it works: By the end of the trial the customer has done the things that make great long-term customers and advertise the business for free, because the penalty forces them to use the product; the author prefers a $50 bill per mess-up to a single $500 fee; people get discouraged after getting billed, and a make-up gets them back on track and converting.
- Bad → Good: HR software at $500 onboarding then $99 a month: the $500 isn't paid up front provided the buyer attends onboarding (three sixty-minute Zoom calls), does the homework, activates the employer profile and gets employees set up by the end of the third call, otherwise the fee is charged; the buyer did the training, learned the complicated software, and stayed rather than learn anyone else's.
- When not to apply: One lump fee fits only where missing once really ruins the customer's result, and the author has seen both work; the billing is justified only if they miss the make-up too.
- Anchor: «rather than giving less---if you can afford it. The criteria should» — $100M Money Models (2025), Trial With Penalty, Important Notes

#### 11.27. Upsell from each of the three trial outcomes
- **Rule:** Meet every trial customer mid-trial or at the end and upsell by outcome: if they like it, meet anyway and offer a longer term or a higher-value version (or both); if they hate it, ask what they would have liked to be different, take the blame, and offer your higher level thing; if they didn't use it, reach out multiple times, say you need to meet, and offer to drop the fee in exchange for the meeting.
- Why it works: Successful customers get even more value out of the better and more profitable offers; with an unhappy customer only one person can be angry and it needs to be you, and about half of them buy the higher level thing; a non-starter is worth a waived fee rather than a one-star review.
- Bad → Good: Blaming the customer who says the trial didn't work → agreeing they are right, blaming yourself for missing it, and offering the higher level thing because you now understand their needs, which about half buy.
- When not to apply: Waiving the non-starter's fee is the author's preference, since a small fee is not worth a one-star review, and he leaves the choice to you.

## Feature Downsells

#### 11.29. The Feature Downsell formula
- **Rule:** To charge less without discounting, take something away, lower the price and, in so many words, ask "how about now?", using lesser quantity, lower quality, lower price alternatives or cutting optional components, and remove features from the highest value to the lowest.
- Why it works: People weigh how much money they save against how much value they lose, so they see the value in what was removed only once they see the price difference; removing high-value features first makes many customers re-upsell themselves onto the more expensive offer.
- Bad → Good: Replace 5-minute response times with overnight ones, framed as saving money while still getting answers with a small delay; the same lever runs across hours, location, cancellation terms, speed of delivery, live against recorded, in-person against remote, DIY against DWY against DFY, and guarantee terms; a painter who can't be afforded hands over the paint and leases a spray machine at a daily rate.
- When not to apply: Remove stuff they hate and lower the price a lot, and more people take the downsell; remove stuff they love and lower it only a little, and more people take the original offer.
- Anchor: «Feature Downsells have a simple formula: take something away, lower the» — $100M Money Models (2025), Feature Downsells, Description and Summary Points

#### 11.30. Standardize the downsell process; end with "The Minimum"
- **Rule:** First cut something valuable and lower the price only slightly, to get them to reconsider the original offer; if that fails, keep removing features and lowering prices until they buy, with the Temperature Check (11.22) after two refusals in a row; name the cheapest combination "The Minimum" and, when every other package is rejected, ask whether they want only the minimum package and nothing more.
- Why it works: The first cut exists to make them reconsider the original offer and price, and the rest exist to find the best deal for them, since the author would rather people get something than nothing; the name implies they have to get at least that thing, and the closing question gets them to say no in order to say yes, as in the Classic Upsell (11.2).
- Bad → Good: Name the most expensive combination after an aspirational status (The Whale Package, The Total Transformation, High Roller), on the airline First-Business-Economy pattern, and the cheapest one The Minimum.
- When not to apply: The sequence presupposes that you know what your customers find most valuable, since the first cut has to take away something valuable.

#### 11.31. Feature Downsell Your Guarantees
- **Rule:** If the offer has a guarantee, make removing it a standard step of the Feature Downsell: on a price objection, ask whether they would rather pay less without the option to get their money back or keep the money-back guarantee at the original price.
- Why it works: People value security, and they see the guarantee's value only once it is removed and the price difference is visible, so many flip back to the full-price offer; cutting a feature is not a discount and does not devalue the product.
- Bad → Good: A single full-price option, where 25 of every 100 people on a call bought → with the guarantee downsell added, 35 buy the main offer and 40 take the downsell, tripling the close rate from 25% to 75% while raising both full-price buyers and up-front cash (2025).

#### 11.32. Free Orientations Boost Do-It-Yourself Feature Downsells
- **Rule:** When a lead has refused every done-for-you offer, invite them to a free orientation on the same topic the next day, and at its end offer a do-it-yourself product for the same problem.
- Why it works: It is money from people who would otherwise have said no: about half of those invited showed up and almost all of them bought supplements (2025); people want the same problem solved in different ways, so offering more ways to solve it gives more at-bats.
- Bad → Good: A gym owner stopped escorting non-buyers out and instead handed them, free, a USB drive with everything needed to do the program at home plus a booked nutrition orientation; almost 100% of those who said no to membership came, were sold supplements instead of workout programs, and spent 50% more on supplements than his regular clients, which his team calls "the no sale sale" (example — the Lost Chapters, Upsell Offer: Free With Alternate Revenue Stream).

## Free With Alternate Revenue Stream

#### 11.34. Free With Alternate Revenue Stream: an essential, seamless first upsell
- **Rule:** When you give the front end away free and monetize a second stream, make the paid thing the next natural thing the prospect needs to succeed with the free one, sell it through a multi-step process with a frictionless upsell, and treat that first upsell as a must-have; with cold traffic, let the first transaction close a trial plus a credit card and the second sell the product.
- Why it works: The play's effectiveness rests on how essential the prospect perceives the next thing to be and how seamless the upsell is, and a frictionless upsell can reach take rates of 90% or more (2025), which liquidates the acquisition cost up front; someone who does not buy that upsell is unlikely to stay, since it is the greatest predictor of back-end conversion; without the card first, cold traffic brings a lot of no-shows.
- Bad → Good: A Paired Upsell gives thing A free in exchange for buying thing B: storage gives a free month with the locks for the unit, the only ones that fit their doors; an Independent Upsell gives A free, then encourages B: a weight loss clinic gives a free 28-day program and sells a $400 supplement package at the nutrition orientation. If 80% take a $300 upsell at 80% margins, each free thing earns $300 × 80% × 80% = $192, and that is only the first upsell.
- When not to apply: Use it only where the business already has an alternative revenue stream with margins high enough to fund fulfilling both the free thing and the paid thing; the author removed the chapter because he did not think enough businesses would be able to use it.
