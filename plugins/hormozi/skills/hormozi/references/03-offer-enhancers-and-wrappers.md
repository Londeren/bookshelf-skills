# Offer enhancers and wrappers: make a finished offer sell harder without changing it

Open this sheet when a core offer exists and has to sell harder without being rebuilt: scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and names from $100M Offers, and the premium, free and discount wrappers of the Lost Chapters for cold audiences.
Building the core offer and the Value Equation is sheet 02; the order of changes when an offer fatigues and what to do when response is low are sheet 01.
Attraction offers inside a money model (Win Your Money Back, Giveaway, Decoy, Buy X Get Y Free and the rest) are sheet 10; continuity discounts are sheet 12.

## Enhancing the offer

#### 03.1. The five offer enhancers
- **Rule:** When the core offer is built and you are packaging it for the market, enhance it from the outside without changing it, with five levers: scarcity to cut supply and raise prices, urgency to lower the prospect's action threshold, bonuses to raise demand and perceived exclusivity, guarantees to raise demand by reversing risk, and names to re-stimulate demand and awareness in the target audience.
- Why it works: These forces position the product in the prospect's mind, are often more powerful than the core offer itself, and together shift the demand curve in your favour.
- Bad → Good: A charity fundraiser, in a year of higher demand, raised its ticket from $15,000 to $25,000 and cut the number of tickets; the products stayed unchanged, yet it took in an extra million dollars before the event started and nearly $5,400,000 from 100 people, $54,000 a head ($100M Offers, 2021).
- When not to apply: Only the offer-side levers belong here; commitment and consistency, status, peer pressure, goodwill, celebrity endorsements and competition also work in a sale, but the author places them with selling, treated in a different volume.
- Anchor: «1. Use *scarcity* to decrease supply to raise prices (and indirectly increase demand through perceived exclusiveness)» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 11 Enhancing The Offer

#### 03.2. The Delicate Dance of Desire and the Hormozi Law
- **Rule:** Keep supply, and so the satisfaction of desire, below the demand you generate: raise demand with persuasive communication to sell more units, cut real or perceived supply to sell those units for more, never let one promotion satisfy all the demand it created, and delay the ask in proportion to its size (Hormozi Law: the bigger the ask, the longer the runway before it).
- Why it works: Desire comes from not getting what you want: unmet demand compounds into more desire and urgency the next time you open, while a promotion that pulls the trigger too early sells fewer units each time until there is not enough demand for a single sale; demand for services is fractal, one fifth of prospects will pay five times the price; and the longer the runway, the bigger the plane that can take off.
- Bad → Good: The same two-day workshop sold as 10 units at $500 sells the entire pyramid, and the next round sells fewer slots → sold as two one-day 1-on-1 workshops at $5000, it skims the top with 80 percent not purchasing, makes more money at lower cost, and the next promotion opens three spots at the same price and sells them all.
- When not to apply: Assumes a regular business, not one chasing mass-market penetration for another strategic advantage; the opposite extreme fails too, since satisfying zero desire makes no money and eventually leaves people feeling rejected.
- Anchor: «When we increase the demand, we can sell more units. When we decrease supply, we can sell those units for more money. The “perfect profit combination” is lots of demand, and very little supply, or *perceived* supply.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 11 Enhancing The Offer

## Scarcity

#### 03.3. Three scarcity caps: Total Business Cap, Growth Rate Cap, Cohort Cap
- **Rule:** In a service business that wants customers consistently, create scarcity with one of three caps (a Total Business Cap of X clients, a Growth Rate Cap of X new clients per week, ongoing, or a Cohort Cap of X clients per class or cohort), and under a total business cap raise capacity periodically by 10-20 percent, then cap it again.
- Why it works: You can only handle a certain number of new clients anyway, so you might as well let prospects know; the cap builds a waiting list, and because some demand is always left unmet, the moment the door opens prospects jump in and price resistance disappears.
- Bad → Good: Said on the call: my agency will only ever service twenty-five customers, period; or a growth rate cap of five new clients a week, three spots already gone and six more calls booked this week, so take the spot or wait for the reopening; or a hundred clients four times a year, doors opened, then closed.
- When not to apply: Built for services, where scarcity is trickier than with physical products (those use limited releases, 03.5); the 10-20 percent raise is for your highest tiers or service levels run under a total business cap.
- Anchor: «With services, especially if you want to consistently get customers, it can be a little trickier to use scarcity. But I will show you a few simple ways to employ scarcity ethically to increase your take rates on offers.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 12 Scarcity

#### 03.4. Honest Scarcity
- **Rule:** Define the number of clients (or, on a free lead magnet, of downloads) you are willing to take in a given period, advertise that number, and tell prospects how close to it you are.
- Why it works: Fear of loss is stronger than desire for gain, so people move to secure a scarce resource; hearing that you are three-fourths of the way to capacity moves them over the edge, and scarcity implies social proof, since a decent number of people have already decided to work with you; only you get to draw the line where you are full.
- Bad → Good: A free checklist available to download now is something a reader might get to eventually → the same checklist on a page that lets only twenty new people download it each week gets checked, gets a notification list for when it runs out, gets clicked the moment the next twenty open, and is far more likely to be consumed.
- When not to apply: Scarcity limits how many can buy; limiting when they can buy is urgency (03.7-03.10), a separate lever even when the two are used together.
- Anchor: «you might as well define a number that you are willing to take on in a given time period, then advertise that» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 12 Scarcity

#### 03.5. Always sell out, and announce it
- **Rule:** Size every limited release (units of a physical product by flavour, colour, design or size, or seats in a workshop, training, event, seminar or consulting upsell) below what you believe you can sell so that it always sells out, and when it has sold out, tell everyone.
- Why it works: Selling out consistently beats over-ordering and failing to create scarcity; the strategy compounds, since next time everyone remembers you sold out fast; and the announcement is social proof that others thought it worth it, so, the choice having been made for them, people desire it more and are far more likely to take the next offer.
- Bad → Good: A release stocked with more units than will sell → this month's 100 boxes of mint chocolate cookie protein bars, sized to sell out and announced once gone; Chanel ships one or two of each piece to each store, so every item is the last or second-to-last in stock, which lets it price far above market.
- When not to apply: The method stacks only when repeated over time and not too often; about once a month is the sweet spot for most of the companies the author knows that do it with regularity.
- Anchor: «Important point: to properly utilize this method you should *always* sell out.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 12 Scarcity

#### 03.6. Once You're Out, You Can Never Come Back
- **Rule:** On a capped service level with a small group, tell members that anyone who leaves can never return.
- Why it works: This type of scarcity makes people think extra hard about leaving; the author reports it working in his gyms, in a mastermind he was in and in the higher level of Gym Lords.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Works best with small groups; as groups become much bigger the tactic loses some teeth, which the author says from experience.
- Anchor: «This works best with small groups (like the above example). As groups become much bigger, the tactic loses some teeth» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 12 Scarcity

## Urgency

#### 03.7. Cohort-based rolling urgency
- **Rule:** When you can start clients on a regular cadence, start them in cohorts and close on the next kickoff: sign up today and join the group that starts Monday, otherwise wait for the next kickoff date.
- Why it works: Urgency is a function of time: a defined cut-off lowers the prospect's action threshold, and deadlines drive decisions; the reminder that they start Monday or wait a week has pushed many sales over the edge, and the less frequently you kick off, the more powerful it is.
- Bad → Good: The juiced version adds that a client dropped out a few weeks ago, so one opening exists in Monday's cohort, and points out that they pay the same either way, only starting later.
- When not to apply: Needs a business that can start clients on a regular cadence, though it works even when it can take them in unlimited numbers; it limits when people start, not how many (that is scarcity, 03.3-03.6).
- Anchor: «*“If you sign up today, I can get you in with our next group that kicks off on Monday, otherwise you’ll have to wait until our next kickoff date.”*» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 13 Urgency

#### 03.8. Rolling Seasonal Urgency
- **Rule:** In a local business, re-release the same core service under a new seasonally named promotion with its own visible start and end date, instead of running the same campaign again.
- Why it works: A new name each season gives a real differentiator with a start and a finish, and deadlines drive decisions; a dated new wrapper brings urgency plus novelty, which outperforms the same old campaign.
- Bad → Good: One unchanged promotion run month after month as New Year Promotion (ends Jan 30), Valentines Lovers Promo, Sexy By Spring Special and Fools in Love April Promo (ends April 30), with the dates on the landing page and in the copy; a new campaign and landing page with new dates take five minutes.
- When not to apply: The author's number one strategy for local businesses, which must vary their marketing more often than national advertisers; for a business that sells year round, see 03.9.
- Anchor: «Putting a new wrapper with a date on the same core service gives you urgency and novelty that will consistently outperform the “same old” campaigns.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 13 Urgency

#### 03.9. Pricing or Bonus-Based Urgency, and a clean pipeline at every price change
- **Rule:** When you sell year round, put the deadline on the promotion, price or bonuses, never on the availability of the service; and never raise your prices without first telling the people in your pipeline.
- Why it works: Saying you will not serve them after the date would be a lie, but talking about the promotion elicits the same urgency while keeping your integrity; announcing a price rise shows a position of strength and brings an influx of cash from the fence-sitters in the pipeline.
- Bad → Good: A roofer claiming he won't serve anyone who buys after the date → start today to lock in the discount that brought you in; we swap promotions about every four weeks, and this one is among our best lately.
- When not to apply: For businesses that serve clients all year round, where the service itself cannot honestly be withdrawn at a deadline.
- Anchor: «if you talk specifically about the promotion you can often elicit the same urgency on buying in the prospect while maintaining your integrity» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 13 Urgency

#### 03.10. Exploding Opportunity
- **Rule:** When the offer exposes the prospect to an arbitrage or a market inefficiency that corrects itself over time, state that decay explicitly in the offer.
- Why it works: Every second of delay costs the prospect disproportionate gains, which forces a fast decision instead of waiting it out for a better offer.
- Bad → Good: No example carried into the skill: the source lists its examples just before the anchor sentence, and none was extracted as a unit.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «All of these examples show opportunities that decay with time, so if you find yourself in front of an opportunity like this, make sure to emphasize it!» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 13 Urgency

## Bonuses

#### 03.11. Bonus Bullets
- **Rule:** Always offer bonuses and give each one a special name with a benefit in the title; say how it relates to their issue, what it is, how you discovered or built it and how it will improve their lives; prove it (a stat, a past client, personal experience); paint their life after using it; put a justified price tag on it; prefer tools and checklists to more training; aim each at one specific concern or obstacle about why they can't or won't succeed, or at what they will logically need next; add scarcity or urgency to the bonuses themselves; and make the bonuses together worth more than the core offer.
- Why it works: Enumerating and stacking the parts raises the perceived value of the offer and widens the price-to-value discrepancy until it is too big to bear; the Value Equation still reigns, so a tool or checklist, lower in effort and time than another training, is worth more; and bonuses that eclipse the core tell the buyer the core must be worth even more.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «That being said, there are a few key things to remember when offering bonuses:» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 14 Bonuses

#### 03.12. Presenting Bonuses 1-on-1, never discounting the main offer
- **Rule:** When selling one on one, ask for the sale before mentioning any bonus and reveal the bonuses only after they sign up; if they do not buy, add a bonus that matches the obstacle they named and ask again, and never discount the core offer to close.
- Why it works: Bonuses revealed after the yes create a wow experience that reinforces the decision; people have a hard time rejecting reciprocity, so a bonus aimed at their obstacle makes them feel almost obligated to buy; a discount teaches customers your prices are negotiable and puts you in weakness, while an added bonus keeps the price intact from strength and goodwill.
- Bad → Good: Cutting the price for a hesitant prospect → agreeing with the obstacle, adding a bonus that answers it, asking whether that is fair enough, and asking for the sale again.
- When not to apply: Stated for one-on-one selling only; group selling lies outside this book. The downsell rule of the same name, change how they pay or what they get but never the price, is 11.19.
- Anchor: «When selling one on one, you ask for the sale *first,* before offering the bonuses. If they say yes, then after they have signed up, you let them know the *additional* bonuses they're going to get.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 14 Bonuses

#### 03.13. Advanced Level Bonuses: Other People's Products and Services
- **Rule:** To raise the value of an offer without raising your cost of fulfilment, get non-competing businesses to supply bonuses in exchange for free exposure to your clients, repeat it across several providers, and negotiate both a group discount for your clients and a commission for yourself.
- Why it works: It is free marketing for them, access to the highest quality prospects, your customers, and high-value bonuses for you at no cost; with enough of these relationships the savings and true-to-price bonuses can justify your entire price, and each commission turns a bonus into a revenue stream.
- Bad → Good: A pain clinic stacks free massages, two chiropractic adjustments ($100), a low-inflammation food discount ($50), brace and orthotic discounts ($150), a free PT session and pool month ($100) and pharmacy discounts ($100/mo), so the bonuses alone outvalue the $400 offer, and referral commissions ($100 chiro, $100 orthotics, $50 health club, $100 pharmacy) add about $350 of profit per customer; the author's Prestige Labs gives sponsored gym owners' clients 30% off and pays the gym owner 40% of sales netted after the discount.
- When not to apply: Only with businesses that are not direct competitors, such as adjacent ones that solve the next need your customer will have.
- Anchor: «You can get other businesses to give you their services and products as a part of your bonuses in exchange for exposure to your clients for free.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 14 Bonuses

#### 03.14. Wow Factor: break the offer into parts and pull out the best
- **Rule:** When you fulfil the offer yourself, break its deliverables into component parts and present them one at a time as stacked bonuses rather than as one undifferentiated offer, pulling out the most distinct pieces that could almost stand on their own, especially short ones high in value (Wow Factor: what you would not want a buyer to miss).
- Why it works: Until the pieces are enumerated they are unknown to the prospect, and in a big offer valuable nuggets get lost in the mix; each added bonus widens the price-to-value discrepancy without cutting the price, and a checklist or infographic nobody would pay much for alone is perceived as very valuable as a bonus.
- Bad → Good: Everything the business fulfils left inside the core offer, the best pieces never highlighted → the short, high-value checklist or infographic pulled out, named and stacked as a bonus.
- When not to apply: The rest of the core offer, which is fulfilled anyway, stays inside the price.
- Anchor: «You want to take the most distinct ones that can almost stand on their own and pull those out to highlight them. This is especially true for things that are short in length but high in quality or value.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 14 Bonuses

## Guarantees

#### 03.15. If you do not get X result in Y time period, we will Z
- **Rule:** Word every guarantee as a conditional statement with all three slots filled: the result X, the time period Y, and what you will do, Z, if they do not get it.
- Why it works: The "or what" is what gives a guarantee teeth; without it the guarantee sounds weak and diluted, which is what most marketers write.
- Bad → Good: We will get you 20 clients guaranteed → 20 clients within your first 30 days or your money back plus the advertising dollars you spent with us.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «What makes a guarantee have power is a conditional statement: If you do not get X result in Y time period, we will Z.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.16. Judge a guarantee by net sales, not by refund fear
- **Rule:** Spend a disproportionate share of offer design on reversing risk, and accept or reject a guarantee on the arithmetic of net sales after refunds, never on the fear that people will take advantage of it.
- Why it works: Risk is the single greatest objection to any product or service, so reversing it is the most immediate way to make an offer more attractive; Jason Fladlien saw an offer's conversion go 2-4x just by changing the quality of the guarantee ($100M Offers, 2021); for a guarantee not to pay, the added sales would have to be fully offset by added refunds, which is unlikely.
- Bad → Good: 100 sales with 5 refunds (5%) leave 95 net sales → with the guarantee, 130 sales with 13 refunds (10%) leave 117 net: 1.23x, 23 percent more, all of it to the bottom line.
- When not to apply: People who buy because of the guarantee can become very bad customers, unwilling to do the work; the author's fix is to tie the guarantee to what the client must do to succeed (03.19).
- Anchor: «So, for the most part, the stronger the guarantee, the higher the *net* increase in total purchases, even if the refund rate increases alongside it.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.17. Unconditional vs Conditional Based on Business Type
- **Rule:** Choose the guarantee type by ticket, buyer and cost of fulfilment: broad guarantees for lower-ticket B2C, more specific and conditional ones as the ticket rises and the buyer is a business, and a conditional guarantee or an anti-guarantee, not an unconditional refund, when your product or service carries a tremendous cost of fulfilment.
- Why it works: In lower-ticket B2C many buyers never take the time to claim; higher-ticket business buyers call for specific guarantees, which may or may not include refunds or conditions; and a refund on a high-cost service makes you eat the refund plus the cost of delivering.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The choice is set by ticket size, B2C versus B2B and fulfilment cost, not by preference.
- Anchor: «Bigger broader guarantees work better with lower ticket B2C businesses (many people just won't bother taking the time). The higher the ticket, and the more business oriented it is, the more you want to steer towards specific guarantees.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.18. Satisfaction guarantees: low ticket and good fulfilment only
- **Rule:** Offer an unconditional satisfaction or no-questions-asked guarantee only when you are good at fulfilling your promises and the ticket is low.
- Why it works: It is the strongest guarantee, in effect a trial where they pay first and then see if they like it, so it gets a lot more people to buy and the refunds are typically made up by higher and faster closing; but you can do everything right and still be asked for the money back.
- Bad → Good: A weight-loss program answered the doubt the guarantee raises (would it still be in business with a guarantee that crazy if it were bad at this?), narrowed the promise from the six-week goal, since the coach cannot eat for the client, to $500 of value and service, and closed on both ends: best case the goal and the money put toward staying on, worst case a check and six weeks of free training; two people took it out of 4,000 sales in three and a half years ($100M Offers, 2021); Jason Fladlien pitches his as a request not for a yes or no today but for a fully informed decision, which can only be made from the inside, with one email to support getting the money back.
- When not to apply: Steer clear if you are not good at fulfilling your promises; it turns very risky as tickets and fulfilment costs rise, and the more conditions are added, the faster it loses its teeth.
- Anchor: «*But you have to be good at fulfilling your promises.* If not, steer clear. I believe this offer works much better in lower-ticket situations.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.19. Conditional guarantees: key actions as conditions, better than money back
- **Rule:** When you know the key actions a client must take to succeed, make them the conditions of the guarantee, and make the payout better than money back.
- Why it works: The conditions get clients results and keep them accountable while you still reverse risk, so in a perfect world everyone qualifies for the guarantee but has reached the result and does not want it; a payout above the refund matches their investment with an equal or higher perceived commitment, and given the choice between a refund and the promised outcome, the vast majority take the outcome.
- Bad → Good: Jason Fladlien's $2997 ecommerce course: spend $X advertising your store with the methods inside, and if you make no money he buys the store for $25,000; claimed to add $3M in sales with only ten $25,000 payouts ($100M Offers, 2021); a promise to work personally with a client until the offer converts holds only if they already spent $10,000 on the existing offer using the structure, on a lead-generation offer that was free, or, in another form, if they reply within twenty-four hours and use the products you prescribe.
- When not to apply: The outsized payout suits products with high margins only; a personal service guarantee without such contingencies would work but be a nightmare to honour.
- Anchor: «If you know the key actions someone must take in order to be successful, make those part of the conditional guarantee.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.20. Anti-Guarantee: all sales final, with a reason why
- **Rule:** State the guarantee boldly with its reason why even when you have none: if all sales are final, own that position and give a creative reason a consumer immediately understands and that shows real exposure or vulnerability on your side.
- Why it works: The reason works as a damaging admission, and the more real exposure you show, the more effective it is; since some sort of guarantee is standard, having none is attention-worthy.
- Bad → Good: All sales final with no explanation → we are about to show you the lead-generation process we run right now, funnels, ads and metrics included, and because that opens up how our business works, all sales are final; for high-ticket customized work the stance becomes a filter: if you need a guarantee before you jump, you are not who we want, we want motivated self-starters.
- When not to apply: Meant for items that are consumable or massively diminish in value once given (a line of code, a script, anything easy to steal once seen), high-ticket work needing a lot of customization, and services with a tremendous cost attached.
- Anchor: «You must come up with a creative “reason why” the sales are final.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.21. Implied Guarantees: Performance Models, Revshares, and Profit-Sharing
- **Rule:** When the outcome is quantifiable, measurable with transparency, and you trust or control collection, use an implied guarantee agreed before the work begins (a performance fee per sale, per show or per pound lost; a revshare such as 10% of top line or 25% of growth over baseline; a profit share; ratchets such as 10% over X, 20% over Y, 30% over Z; bonuses or triggers); a business taking on its first guarantee starts here or with a service-based guarantee and loosens later.
- Why it works: If you do not perform, they do not pay, which makes you accountable to their results and weeds out low performers; the alignment fosters collaboration and a long-term relationship, and with all sales final there is no fear of refunds, so from there you keep it and scale or move to less restrictive guarantees for volume.
- Bad → Good: A revshare or performance fee paired with a minimum: the greater of $1000 or 10% of revenue generated.
- When not to apply: Needs transparency on the outcome and trust or control that you will get paid; the drawbacks are tracking and collection.
- Anchor: «**What The Client Gets:** If you do not perform, they do not have to pay. If you perform,your compensation has been determined based on an agreement decided upon *before* you begin working.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

#### 03.22. Stacking Guarantees
- **Rule:** To strengthen an offer that already carries a guarantee, stack a second one: an unconditional guarantee with a conditional one on top, or two conditional guarantees around different or sequential outcomes.
- Why it works: Spelling out a sequence of outcomes with timelines future paces the prospect into an outcome they now believe is far more likely, and shifts the burden of risk from them onto you.
- Bad → Good: A 30-day unconditional, no-questions-asked guarantee with a conditional 90-day triple-your-money-back guarantee stacked on top.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «For example, you could give an unconditional 30 day no questions asked guarantee then on top of that give a conditional triple your money back 90 day guarantee.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 15 Guarantees

## Naming and refreshing

#### 03.23. M-A-G-I-C naming formula
- **Rule:** Name the offer, each promotion and every item and bonus in the stack from three to five M-A-G-I-C components in any order (a Magnetic reason why, the Avatar, the Goal, a time Interval, a Container word such as Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Intensive or Masterclass), as short and punchy as specificity allows, and in a local market name the sub-market or hyper-local area rather than the city.
- Why it works: Each component does a job in the prospect's head (Attention, Discrimination, Purpose, Timeline, Method); the container word signals a bundle, a system that cannot be held up to a commoditized alternative; three to five components make a name unique and desirable, and the more local the headline, the more it converts.
- Bad → Good: Float Tank Center Session → Free Six-Week Stress Release Challenge, the same thing named so the prospect responds; more worked names: 88% Off 12-Week Bikini Blueprint; Lakeway Moms - $1,500 Off Your Kids Braces; 5 Clients in 5 Days Blueprint; Fill Your Gym in 30 Days (Free!).
- When not to apply: No component is mandatory, and fitting all five usually makes the name too long; a quantifiable claim with a duration is limited by platform rules (03.24).
- Anchor: «If you like understanding the concepts behind my chosen M-A-G-I-C formula. Each roughly translates to: Attention (M-Magnet), Discrimination (A-Avatar), Purpose (G-Goal), Timeline (I-Interval), and Method (C-Container).» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 16 Naming

#### 03.24. No quantifiable claim with a duration where the platform forbids it
- **Rule:** Do not pair a quantifiable claim, such as income gain or weight loss, with a stated duration to achievement unless the platform allows it; where the goal is not a claim as such, use the time interval.
- Why it works: Most platforms will not approve that messaging, because a claim with a duration implies a guarantee of the outcome in that time, which goes against their rules.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: A platform-compliance limit only: anywhere you do not have to deal with compliance, use the duration freely.
- Anchor: «Note: If you’re making any sort of quantifiable claim (like income gain or weight loss) most platforms will *not* approve this type of messaging *with* a stated duration to achievement because it implies a guarantee.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 16 Naming

#### 03.25. Change the wrapper, not the offer
- **Rule:** When an offer fatigues, refresh only its wrapper (name, headline, copy, creative) and keep the services, products and work delivered unchanged; once an offer is monetized, rinse and repeat it rather than change it, and touch the machine behind it only as a last resort.
- Why it works: Offers fatigue over time, and faster in local markets, where the addressable market is only the immediate radius; renaming keeps leads coming forever with the value stack untouched, while changing the offer itself usually just creates inefficiency and operational drag, and entrepreneurs love change for its own sake.
- Bad → Good: Free 6 Week Lean Challenge → Free 6 Week Tone Challenge; Holiday Hangover → New Year New You; for a massage center, Six-Week Stress Release Challenge → 42-Day Relaxing Holidays Challenge, the offer inside unchanged (the order of the heavier changes is sheet 01).
- When not to apply: Reaching an audience once in no way means an offer is fatigued, since most people miss an offer the first time it is mentioned; real fatigue takes years of use, coming sooner in local markets.
- Anchor: «We are *not* changing the actual offer. We are only changing the *wrapping paper*.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 16 Naming

## Wrappers: premium, free, discount

#### 03.26. Do not start with a premium offer: wrap it
- **Rule:** When you are just starting out, your volume is too low, or you cannot bear your cost of acquisition, do not lead with a premium offer: wrap the unchanged Grand Slam Offer in a free or discount front end, prove results, then restructure toward premium.
- Why it works: A promotion is wrapping paper: what is inside stays the same, but it becomes more inherently attractive to a cold audience that needs a reason to move toward you; there is nothing below $0, while the ticket can go infinitely higher once results are proven; and a straight premium offer has to hit a lot of eyeballs before it gets a bite.
- Bad → Good: With no course and no program, the author's first Gym Launch offer gave himself away: he flew out, spent his own money on marketing, worked and closed the leads, kept only what he sold and handed the gym the customers and systems for free; run for 33 gyms over 18 months, it was easy to sell because it was free and made his first million dollars outside the gyms (example — the Lost Chapters, Section A: Attract).
- When not to apply: A premium front end on its own is for businesses that already have the reputation, volume and a bearable cost of acquisition; the free or discount wrapper can come off as reputation improves.
- Anchor: «If I needed to make money, or make a business owner money, I would not start with» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Premium Promotions, If I Lost Everything And Had To Start Over

#### 03.27. If only one offer, make it free
- **Rule:** If you can make only one front-end offer to convert a market, make it free, and do not reject free on the belief that free brings broke people who will not close.
- Why it works: Free is value in advance: Dan Ariely's penny gap showed 9x more people take a free Hershey kiss than one sold for a penny (cited in the Lost Chapters, 2025), and a page on a $1 offer can convert far worse than on a free one; better to wade through weak leads and then add friction (03.29) than to look at an empty calendar.
- Bad → Good: Four separate split tests of free against premium offers, each across 10 representative markets: the gyms closed free and non-free respondents at the same rate and average ticket, while going from a non-free to a free front end most times cut lead costs by five times or more (the Lost Chapters, 2025).
- When not to apply: The author does not claim free is for every offer, every time: free beats premium unless the seller has truly mastered high-ticket selling, and a premium front end whose lead cost runs 5-10x higher needs prices at least 5-10x higher, with price, prospect, process, promotion and product all in one structure; in heavily regulated industries, states or countries, where free offers with stipulations are forbidden, use a discount offer instead.
- Anchor: «Bottom line: If I only had one offer to make to convert or my family would be killed, it would be a» — $100M Leads: 2 Bonus Chapters (2023), Free Promotions

#### 03.28. Always answer Why
- **Rule:** Give every crazy free or discount offer a good and true reason why it is being made; when a free offer does not convert, diagnose which of three is failing: they don't want the thing (change what you give away or how you describe it), they don't believe you (the reason why), or they never see it because you fish in the wrong pond (targeting).
- Why it works: An offer so good it is unbelievable gets no response, while a good enough reason makes people believe you; a free offer is the quickest test of whether anyone wants the thing, so its failure is information about the thing, the believability or the targeting.
- Bad → Good: A bare 90% off all products → going out of business, everything must go within 30 days, hence 90% off; a famous marketer who advertised $1,000 back for every $100 got no responses at all.
- When not to apply: The reason has to be true.
- Anchor: «whenever you give a crazy free offer away, you will always have to answer the next question:» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Free Promotions

#### 03.29. Examples of Friction, judged by Free Money Math
- **Rule:** When a free offer brings more prospects than you can handle or serve, add friction instead of dropping it (more qualifications repeated in the same words across copy, creative and landing pages; a bigger information requirement and other types of questions; more steps; forced consumption; longer ads), and set it where the same ad spend yields the most qualified leads in number, not the highest share of qualified leads (Free Money Math).
- Why it works: The more hoops someone has to go through, the higher the lead quality and the lower the volume; the higher volume is what you want, with friction skimming the cream off the top, so the sweet spot is the friction that maximizes quality volume.
- Bad → Good: Campaign #1 spends $1,000 for 500 leads, 250 of them qualified; campaign #2 spends $1,000 for 200 leads, 160 qualified (80%); the team feels better about #2, but on pure dollars and cents #1 wins.
- When not to apply: Add just enough to weed out the weirdos but not so much that you lose some lazy whales; forced consumption, such as a 40-minute video before any call to action, suits only advertising to a large audience where eyeballs are cheap, since elsewhere the volume is too low to justify it.
- Anchor: «per day, it could be a problem So we add friction Friction increases lead quality The more» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Free Promotions, Cons #1

#### 03.30. Massive discounts only, shown Four Ways
- **Rule:** Make a discount front end massive (50% or more, never a marginal 5 to 25%), put it on a service whose price people already know, and cycle it through the Four Ways To Display Discounts (percentage off, absolute amount off, relative equivalent off, the discounted price alone) to find the one your audience answers.
- Why it works: 50% or more drives action from a population that would not otherwise act, while marginal discounts don't drive real behaviour and just cut into margin; a discount on something the customer doesn't understand has nothing to be compared against; and people respond differently to the same discount shown differently, so several winners leave more bullets in the chamber when a promotion fatigues.
- Bad → Good: A first week of a lemonade bundle normally $210, sold at $29: New Client Special, 87% off first visit; $181 off the first week, normally $210; Save A Steak Dinner, or Less Than Going Out To Lunch; $29 New Client Special.
- When not to apply: Where people don't know what the service is or what it costs (50% off an agency retainer means nothing), state the price first and then the discount, or make another offer.
- Anchor: «Instead, we’re going to be talking about massive discounts (50% or more) Those are» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Discount Promotions, Understanding Discount Offers

#### 03.31. Splinter the offer instead of Giving Away The Farm
- **Rule:** In a discount promotion, splinter the offer into pieces and discount one core component, never the core offer itself.
- Why it works: If you always discount the core offer, people learn to buy only when it is discounted; the discount is a way to acquire customers, not a business model.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: True discounts on the core offer work only where the pricing model is to raise prices wildly in the regular season and live off the discounting, as clothing retailers do, and even there it is a double-edged sword.
- Anchor: «“splinter” our offer into tiny pieces and just give a core component at a discount—not the» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Discount Promotions, Cons #1 Giving Away The Farm

#### 03.32. Discount buyers are qualified leads, not customers
- **Rule:** Count people who buy the discount front end as qualified leads, not customers: structure the offer so they qualify themselves into the core service, and never build the business on the discount cash as the real way acquisition costs are paid back.
- Why it works: The discount is only the first way to attract and transact with a customer, and its cash will not amount to much; the businesses that complained that Groupon buyers never bought the main thing did not know how to structure their offers to qualify prospects automatically.
- Bad → Good: Groupon-style deals: the owners who blamed bargain hoppers for never buying the core service had counted them as customers instead of building the offer to qualify them into it.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «core service We shouldn’t see people as customers if they buy the discount We should see» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Discount Promotions, Cons #2 Bargain Hoppers

#### 03.33. The Two-Step Sale
- **Rule:** Run a discount front end as a Two-Step Sale: give away something valuable that is not the core offer at an insane discount, take the card over the phone, and at a designated second appointment upsell the far more expensive core; where the delivering person's time is costly, keep it out of the first step: either charge for that step rather than making it free, or, the author's preference, rebuild the first visit so an admin or assistant runs it and the practitioner's time goes only to the most qualified.
- Why it works: People typically show up for what they pay for, at least 85-90% (the Lost Chapters, 2025), so a paid first step all but eliminates no-shows; a card on file turns the upsell into a question of using the card on file, the friction Amazon's one-click purchasing and Disney's wristbands remove because they sell less with it; and an assistant-run first visit removes the cost of no-shows another way, pre-qualifying candidates and putting them in the best position to say yes next time.
- Bad → Good: Ad, opt-in, phone call for a $19 heavy metals test consultation with the appointment set, the $19 visit delivering value, then a follow-up where the $2,100 ten-week detox is sold; in a braces practice the front desk takes the information, X-rays and financing pre-approval, and the doctor drops in for five minutes (the Proprietary 5 min Appointment Method) to set the in-depth appointment where the treatment plan is sold.
- When not to apply: Both fixes, the paid first step and the assistant-run visit, are for services where the time or cost of the person delivering is real, such as a doctor's time.
- Anchor: «An example of a two-step sale would be us giving away a heavy metals test consultation» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Discount Promotions
