# Paid ads: pick the platform and audience, write the ad, fund the tests, assemble ads at volume

Open this sheet when the user is about to run paid ads, is writing an ad, or has ads that lose money or stop scaling. It holds the $100M Leads (2023) method (platform, audience, callout, value, CTA, the three money phases and the budgets) and the GOATed Ads playbook (2025) that runs on top of it: ads assembled from hooks, meats and CTAs, and hooks matched to the level of awareness.
The LTGP:CAC threshold, its two levers, client financed acquisition and the question whether the problem is advertising or sales are in sheet 01; permuting a winner (Ad Kaleidoscope) and the ad frameworks are in sheet 07; scaling by More Better New is in sheet 05; the Value Equation behind the eight elements of 06.9 is in sheet 02.

## Platform and audience

#### 06.1. Four things true of the platform
- **Rule:** Start paid ads on one platform, and pick it only when all four hold: you have used it yourself as a consumer and got value from it, you can target people on it interested in your stuff, you know how to format ads specific to it, and you have the minimum amount of money to place an ad.
- Why it works: Having used the platform as a consumer gives you some idea how it works, and the four conditions are what stays constant while the platforms themselves keep changing.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «what I look for in a platform I want to advertise on:» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step 1

#### 06.2. Lookalike audience: the list in order of quality
- **Rule:** When the platform allows lookalike audiences, assemble the uploaded list in order of quality (your current and past customers first, then warm reach out contacts, then cold reach out leads), adding the next group only until the platform minimum is reached.
- Why it works: The bigger the list and the higher the quality of its contacts, the more responsive the lookalike audience.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: If you cannot make a lookalike audience, start by targeting interests; a list forced up to the minimum sometimes comes out too broad, which the filters of 06.3 then fix.
- Anchor: «Start with your list of current and previous customers. If your» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step #2

#### 06.3. Start with a puddle, broaden only after profit
- **Rule:** Start with a small, specific audience, stacking filters on top of it early; broaden it and loosen the filters only after the smaller audience has been advertised to profitably, and then make it as big as it can be while it still turns a profit.
- Why it works: A more specific list makes the ads more efficient but burns through faster, and the wins from small specific audiences pay for advertising to larger, broader ones; a bigger audience holds more of the wrong people but more of the right ones too, so the ratio of spend to sales drops while the total money made goes up.
- Bad → Good: Worked ladder: a puddle, then a pond, a lake and an ocean; at each step expect the ratio between what you spend and what they buy to get worse while the total money you make goes up, which is also why a scaled budget is padded by twenty percent (06.17).
- When not to apply: The drop in efficiency is acceptable only at the point where you can afford the higher spend, and the risk rises with it; the aim is the largest audience that still turns a profit, not the best ratio. Filters-first is for early campaigns with a limited budget.
- Anchor: «Once we advertise profitably in a small puddle of an audience, we» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I

#### 06.4. An unprofitable ad: check who saw it before the copy
- **Rule:** When an ad is not profitable, check first whether it reached enough of the right people before treating it as a copy or creative failure.
- Why it works: Paid ads go to colder, lower-trust audiences, so a smaller percentage of people respond; putting the offer in front of more of the right people is what gets over that hurdle and keeps ads efficient.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The cause is the audience "most of the time", not always; spend that was profitable and stops being profitable as it scales is read as a wall in ad quality instead (06.22).
- Anchor: «in front of more people. And if an ad isn't profitable, most of the» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I: Making An Ad

## Making the ad

#### 06.5. Three chunks: callout, value, call to action
- **Rule:** Build every ad from three chunks in order (a callout that gets attention, the value that makes them interested, a call to action), and make the callout specific enough to get the right people and broad enough to get as many of them as you can.
- Why it works: People noticing the ad is the most important part of the ad by a lot, and a callout is whatever you do to get that attention, ranging from hyperspecific (one person's attention) to not specific at all (everyone's).
- Bad → Good: Worked example, the Chino Hills ad (Facebook, 2023): text only, all caps, no images, no video, no frills; the callout named the town, the type of person and the number of spots (5 Chino Hills residents), the value was a free 6 week challenge with before and after pictures as the price of entry, and the ad ended on a single explicit instruction plus a link; 19 sold at $299 each, turning $1000 of ad spend into just under $5700 (example — $100M Leads, Run Paid Ads Part I: Making An Ad).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «[A ]{.calibre3}[callout]{.calibre11}[ ]{.calibre3}[is whatever you do to» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I: Making An Ad, Step #3 Call Out

#### 06.6. Four verbal callouts
- **Rule:** Write the headline or first five seconds of an ad as one of four verbal callouts: a Label (words putting people into a group: features, traits, titles, places), a Yes-Question (answering "yes, that's me" qualifies them for the offer), an If-Then Statement (if they meet your conditions, then you help them decide), or a Ridiculous Result (bizarre, rare or out-of-the-ordinary stuff someone would want).
- Why it works: Callouts harness the cocktail party effect and cut through the noise; if people never notice the ad nothing else matters, which is why the first impression is the part of the ad the author tests the most.
- Bad → Good: A label the ideal customer does not place themselves in → a label they actually identify with, since a label only catches attention when the person puts themselves in that group.
- When not to apply: The four are not an exhaustive list, far from it; they are shown to pull back the curtain on how callouts work.
- Anchor: «Here's what I look for with verbal callouts-» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step #3 Call Out

#### 06.7. Local callout: LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON
- **Rule:** In an ad for a local business, the callout names the smallest local area the buyer identifies with together with the type of person (LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON).
- Why it works: People automatically identify with their local area, so the more local the label, the better it works.
- Bad → Good: Americans < Texans < Dallas Residents < Irving Residents: each step down the ladder is a better local callout.
- When not to apply: Given for local ads only.
- Anchor: «So with local ads, the more local, the better. A local» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step #3

#### 06.8. The What-Who-When Framework
- **Rule:** Once the callout has their attention, write the value chunk by running the offer through three lenses in order: The What (the eight key elements, 06.9), The Who (whose perspective experiences them, 06.10) and The When (the prospect's past, present and future), running each value element from each who-perspective through the timeline to get fresh angles.
- Why it works: Together the What, the Who and the When answer why the prospect should be interested; people usually think only of how a decision affects the here and now, so the timeline makes them see the consequences of deciding, or not deciding, right now, and the more angles covered, the more interested they become.
- Bad → Good: Worked example, weight loss: the prospect's own timeline (teased as a kid in the past, struggling to button favourite jeans in the present, moving up another belt loop in the future), then the same timeline through other people (the kid asking why other kids make fun of them, kids complaining that other dads join practice, the doctor saying he may not walk his daughter down the aisle), then copy lines that tag WHO, WHAT and WHEN in one sentence; the bad stuff comes first, then is contrasted with the good stuff if they buy.
- When not to apply: The framework hinges on knowing the Value Equation forwards and backwards (sheet 02); it writes the value chunk, not the callout.
- Anchor: «share with you my What-Who-When Framework. This mental framework hinges» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step #3 Get Them Interested

#### 06.9. The What: Eight Key Elements
- **Rule:** For the What lens, write down eight things about the product, the four value elements and their four opposites (Dream Outcome / Nightmare, Perceived Likelihood of Achievement / Risk, Time Delay / Speed, Effort and Sacrifice / Ease), and show both sides in the ad, making the benefits appear as large as possible and the costs as small as possible.
- Why it works: People who see big benefits and tiny costs in an offer or lead magnet value it and exchange money or contact information for it, and when the cost outweighs the benefits they do not; carrots and sticks together show how the offer delivers more good stuff and less bad stuff.
- Bad → Good: Worked pair: Time Delay shows how slow their current trajectory is, or that they will never get what they want at their current rate; its opposite, Speed, shows and tells how much faster they get the thing they want. Likewise Perceived Likelihood lowers perceived risk with the success of people like them, authority and guarantees, while Risk shows how risky it is not to act and how they will repeat their past failures.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Those are the 8 key elements. Now we fully understand» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, The What

#### 06.10. The Who: two groups of people
- **Rule:** For the Who lens, list two groups, the people gaining status (your customers) and the people giving it to them (Spouse, Kids, Parents, Extended Family, Colleagues, Bosses, Friends, Rivals, Competitors), and state each value element from each of their perspectives.
- Why it works: People are driven primarily by status, and one person's status comes from how other people treat them, so showing how the product changes the way others treat the customer surfaces bonus benefits you would miss looking only from the customer's own perspective.
- Bad → Good: "Risk free" told from the prospect's view only → the spouse who will not nag about the purchase, the kids who notice the parent is less stressed about work, the competitors whose phones stop ringing, the business owner buddies at the golf range who say business must be good.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «outline two groups of people. The first group is the people gaining» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Who

#### 06.11. Long ad, short ad: change only the angles
- **Rule:** When adapting one ad to platforms with different lengths, add or remove value angles and keep the callout (the first few seconds) and the CTA the same.
- Why it works: The only difference between long ads and short ads is how many angles from the What-Who-When Framework there is time to cover.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «away based on the platform, but keep the callouts (the first few» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step #3

#### 06.12. The ad does not sell
- **Rule:** Make the ad and its landing page ask whether the person is interested rather than sell the offer, and take contact information in exchange: after they take the action, get their contact information (the author's default mechanism is a simple landing page).
- Why it works: A person who is interested gives you a way to tell them more, and at that moment becomes an engaged lead.
- Bad → Good: Selling the offer inside the ad or on the landing page → asking whether they are interested and taking their contact information, the permission to contact them, in exchange.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «[To be clear, we aren't selling anything. We are asking if they're» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I: Making An Ad

#### 06.13. Remind them of the step they just took
- **Rule:** In a multi-step opt-in flow (ad, then CTA, then contact information), each next step reminds the person of the action they just took and shows how the next action follows from it.
- Why it works: People like to think of themselves as consistent (Robert Cialdini, Influence), so reminding them of the action they just took gets more of them to take the second one.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «action they just took (CTA), and show how taking the next action aligns» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part I, Step #4

#### 06.14. Show the next step, do not only tell it
- **Rule:** In the CTA, show what the next step looks like (the button being clicked, the fields being filled, submit being pressed) as well as saying it.
- Why it works: When people click and get exactly what they expected, they are more likely to follow through; showing gives the ultimate congruence, aligns expectations and removes the uncertainty about the next step that is the final barrier to conversion.
- Bad → Good: Stating the next step without demonstrating it → the same instruction with the signup flow shown over the ad as a quick screen recording or mock-up (example — ACQ Advertising Handbook, Section III, Ad Framework #5: Challenge With Prize).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «If you want to take this up a level show and tell them. So not only do you make your» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), CTA - Call To Action

## Money: test, scale, budget

#### 06.15. The Three Phases of Scaling Paid Ads
- **Rule:** Spend on ads in three phases, in order: Track Money (tracking of returns set up and working before the first dollar), Lose Money (a monthly amount you have decided you are willing to lose and expect to lose, spent on tests until you find a winner, 06.16), Print Money (once ads make back more than they cost, spend as much as the rest of the business can take, with the budget reversed from the sales goal, 06.17).
- Why it works: Tracking lets you do more of what makes money and less of what does not; the losses are many but small because you know when to shut an ad down, and the wins are few but big because you know when to hit the gas.
- Bad → Good: Spending before tracking, like playing a casino game for as long as you feel like rather than as long as you can afford → tracking set up first, then a loss budget spent while you learn: in Lose Money you are not earning, you are learning.
- When not to apply: Print Money starts only once ads make back more than they cost, and its limit is the other constraints of the business, not the ads; Lose Money is the phase of the first campaigns.
- Anchor: «There are three stages to spending money on ads as I see» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff

#### 06.16. Test budget: 2× thirty-day cash, under 1× with no leads
- **Rule:** Give a new ad a test budget of two times the cash collected from a customer in the first thirty days (not LTGP) before shutting it off, as long as it produces leads; shut off an ad that produces no leads at all before it has spent one times thirty-day cash ($100M Leads, 2023).
- Why it works: Letting ads run too long wastes money and giving up on them before they have had a chance wastes even more; two times thirty-day cash is the author's sweet spot, and below one times there is nothing to learn from an ad that has produced nothing, so the money is better spent on the next test.
- Bad → Good: The author's own two expensive mistakes, letting ads run too long before realising they sucked and giving up on ads before giving them a chance → one cut-off per ad: up to 2× thirty-day cash while leads come in, under 1× when none do.
- When not to apply: The measure is cash collected in the first thirty days, not lifetime gross profit (see sheet 01); the 2× budget holds only while the ad is producing leads.
- Anchor: «I budget two times the cash I collect from a customer in thirty days» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II

#### 06.17. Reverse the budget from the customer goal, padded by twenty percent
- **Rule:** Once ads break even or better, calculate the daily budget backwards: the customers you want or can handle next month × your cost per customer, padded by twenty percent and spread over thirty days, then commit to that number ($100M Leads, 2023).
- Why it works: Past break-even the question stops being how much to spend and becomes how many customers you can handle, and the pad covers ads getting less efficient as they scale.
- Bad → Good: Backing away from the reversed number because it is frightening → 100 customers at $100 each is $10,000, padded to $12,000 over thirty days, $400 a day, and committed to; if the number terrifies you, you are doing it right: trust the data.
- When not to apply: Only at break-even or better (Print Money, 06.15); before that the test budgets of 06.16 apply.
- Anchor: «ads break even or better, I reverse my budget from my sales» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II

#### 06.18. Ten ads, one winner: scale it instead of stopping at the loss
- **Rule:** When a batch of tested ads shows a total loss, find the winner in the batch and put far more money behind it (double, triple, quadruple, 10x down) instead of stopping.
- Why it works: The losses are many but each is small because you know when to shut an ad down, and the wins are few but large because you know when to hit the gas; the aggregate shows a loss while the winner is there to be scaled.
- Bad → Good: $100 on each of ten ads ($1,000 in total); nine lose their $100 and one returns $500, so the account is $500 down and most people stop there → $10,000 into the one winner returns $50,000.
- When not to apply: It presumes each loss was kept small by the cut-offs of 06.16; you might lose nine or ninety-nine times in a row before you win big.
- Anchor: «still down \$500. Many people stop here because they see a \$500 dollar» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff

## Ad assembly and hooks

#### 06.19. The Ad Assembly Process
- **Rule:** Assemble ads from separately produced parts instead of creating them whole: each week make 50 hooks, 3–5 meats and 1–3 CTAs and combine them into 150 to 750 ads; an ad day runs: pick the level of awareness (06.23), write 50 hooks, write 3–5 meats, write 1–3 CTAs, then repeat to find another winner (GOATed Ads playbook, 2025).
- Why it works: It is much harder to make a bunch of ads at once and much easier to make them in parts, and ads scale only by making better ads, which you get only by making far more of them; as a side benefit no one can figure out your top performing ads.
- Bad → Good: A founder selling weight loss on Facebook at $200,000 a month recorded about five new ads a month and called the market saturated because past $5-8k a day in ad spend his cost to acquire customers shot through the roof → every Friday devoted to advertising, his best ads chopped into hook, meat and CTA, 50 more hooks and 3-5 variations of the meat: 150 ads a week, 600 a month instead of 5; the company doubled in two quarters (example — GOATed Ads playbook, GOATed Ads).
- When not to apply: GOATed Ads runs on top of the paid-ads rules of $100M Leads (06.1–06.18), not instead of them.
- Anchor: «the result of this prep is 50 hooks x 3-5 meat x 1-3 CTAs = 150 to 750 ads…per week. This» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), The Ad Assembly Process

#### 06.20. Prep split: 80 % hooks, 20 % meat, ~0 % CTAs
- **Rule:** Split the preparation time for a recording session 80 % on hooks, 20 % on the meat and roughly none on CTAs, and record three to five meats per weekly session (GOATed Ads playbook, 2025).
- Why it works: Ninety percent of the work in advertising is the preparation to record, not the recording; if someone does not make it through the hook nothing else matters, and fewer people see the body of an ad than its hook, so the meat is rotated less often and not used up as fast.
- Bad → Good: Prep time spent on the body of the ad → 80 % of it on hooks, 20 % on the meat, roughly none on CTAs.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «not in the recording itself. Here’s how I split my prep time:» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Next Time You film

#### 06.21. Where to harvest hooks for immediate results
- **Rule:** For hooks meant to bring immediate results, look in this order: winning hooks from your previous ads, winning hooks from your free content, winning hooks from other people's ads (save the ads you like and write down their hooks), winning hooks from other people's free content (content with tons of views in your space), and platform-specific ad libraries last.
- Why it works: Winning hooks in one industry often work in others, and a hook that already worked on one platform or advertising method has the highest likelihood of converting when moved to another.
- Bad → Good: Modelling hooks from a platform ad library first → using it last, since it is hard to tell which of those ads do well enough to be worth modelling, and most people don't know what they're doing.
- When not to apply: On a first run of ads, skip the first source, since there are no past winners yet; big ad libraries are a fun place for ideas more than a guarantee of winners, though the author still checks the hooks of companies he knows run great paid ads.
- Anchor: «ments. I look for high-converting hooks in the following places:» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Writing Hooks For Immediate Results From Previous Winners

#### 06.22. Refresh the creative before widening the audience
- **Rule:** When ad spend that was profitable stops being profitable as you scale it, refresh the creative first; only if that has been done too, widen the targeted audience with expansion hooks, experimental hooks written to reach a colder, broader audience.
- Why it works: The wall is your ad quality, not the market: you have taken the low hanging fruit rather than saturated the platform, and the better the ads, the bigger the audience they convert.
- Bad → Good: Calling the market saturated when the cost to acquire shoots up past a certain daily ad spend → better creative: Old Spice's ads with the man on the horse were funny enough that the author, not a buyer, started buying, and so did 70% of the market; that is an ad that was not capped (example — GOATed Ads playbook, GOATed Ads).
- When not to apply: Expansion hooks are for when something new is needed; the weekly bread-and-butter ads are written off previous winners (06.21). An ad that is not profitable in the first place falls under 06.4.
- Anchor: «You likely need to refresh your creative. But let’s say you’ve done that too. If that’s the» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Writing Expansion Hooks To Enter New Markets

#### 06.23. Five levels of awareness, five hook drivers
- **Rule:** When writing expansion hooks, match each hook's driver to the level of awareness it targets, on Eugene Schwartz's five levels: Most Aware takes an offer-driven hook, Product-Aware a proof-driven one, Solution-Aware a promise-driven one, Problem-Aware a pain-driven one, Completely Unaware a curiosity-driven one.
- Why it works: Hooks that keep focusing on your offer or your proof convert a small slice of a larger audience very well; to grab a bigger slice of the pie you meet the audience where they are, and the larger the audience you reach, the more varied the ads must be.
- Bad → Good: Only offer and proof hooks once ad spend is capped → one weight loss shake, XFast, written five ways: a new formula with 25% more protein and the same great taste (most aware); why 9 out of 10 users reached their goal weight within 3 months (product-aware); lose 15 pounds in 30 days with a scientifically proven meal replacement system (solution-aware); frustrated with crash diets that don't last, there is a sustainable way (problem-aware); the hidden hormonal imbalance making 1 in 3 Americans gain weight (unaware). The agency Digital Boost runs the same ladder for B2B, from 20% off social media management to the unexpected way a business loses thousands each month in untapped revenue.
- When not to apply: These are frameworks, not a magical recipe: labels are clean and reality is messy, the author uses a continuous version of the model, a national brand can run an offer-driven hook to everyone, and a broad curiosity piece can convert a very warm audience, as a movie trailer does; when in doubt, go a little broader.
- Anchor: «Most Aware: These hooks are typically offer driven.» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Writing Expansion Hooks To Enter New Markets

#### 06.24. No offer-driven hook to a cold broad audience
- **Rule:** Do not lead with an offer-driven hook to a broad cold audience unless that audience already knows both the brand and the product.
- Why it works: A national offer-driven hook works only where the brand and the product are already known; for a no-name product that needs explaining, leading with the offer to the whole country is as near as it gets to burning money.
- Bad → Good: A no-name competitor whose product needs education leads with an offer-driven hook to a national audience → Subway's $5 Foot Long, which ran for 20 years as a sandwich ad and worked because hundreds of millions had been spent getting the whole nation to know the brand, and everyone knows what a sandwich is.
- When not to apply: What licenses the hook is the audience's awareness, not the size of the advertiser: a known brand plus a known product may run an offer hook to everyone.
- Anchor: «a product that people need education to understand, leading with an offer driven hook to» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Writing Expansion Hooks To Enter New Markets

#### 06.25. Spread the hooks broader
- **Rule:** If 90% of your hooks sit in the Most Aware bucket, spread them across the colder buckets, and when in doubt write the hook a little broader; when the goal is the widest possible audience, test meme or meme-like hooks built on the culture-specific memes of the group you target (GOATed Ads playbook, 2025).
- Why it works: A broader hook still catches the warm audience and attracts some of the colder audience as well, and a relevant meme works like a moth to a flame, exploding the number of eyeballs exposed to the ad.
- Bad → Good: Nearly all of the 50 hooks written at the Most Aware level → the hooks divided into buckets that hit each level of awareness, or most of them aimed broader than the current hooks (example — GOATed Ads playbook, GOATed Ads Playbook Cheat Sheet).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «If 90% of your hooks land in the “Most aware” bucket, spread them out to capture a» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Writing Expansion Hooks To Enter New Markets

#### 06.26. Double down on the winners
- **Rule:** When a few ads wildly outperform the rest, reuse their hooks to make more variations instead of moving on to something new, and do not retire a winning ad because you are tired of it.
- Why it works: In the beginning you cannot know which ads will hit, so you make many variations for the different segments of the market; and new customers enter the market every day, so a repeated winner is still their first exposure to it.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «And when you find those, double down» — $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads (2025), Scale It
