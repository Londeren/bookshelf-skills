# Retention and referrals: keep customers longer, then turn them into referrals

Open this sheet when a business loses customers too fast, is planning onboarding, payment terms or cancellation saves, or wants more referrals from the customers it already has. It holds the Retention Playbook's Churn Checklist and 5 Horsemen of Retention, the six ways to give more value and the seven ways to ask from $100M Leads, and the Referral Process of the ACQ Closer Handbook; figures from the playbooks and the ACQ Closer Handbook are 2025, from $100M Leads 2023.
The referral growth equation and the product as the problem when customers do not bring customers are sheet 01; continuity offers, cancellation fees and the exit interview traded for the fee are sheet 12; the annual billing pricing play is sheet 13; hunt-mode outreach to past customers for referrals is sheet 08.

## Why retention first

#### 14.1. The leaky bucket business
- **Rule:** Before spending more on selling, run the leaky bucket arithmetic on the month-over-month churn — the share of customers lost in a year and the number of new customers needed just to stay the same size — and when the business loses customers about as fast as it wins them, work on churn before more selling.
- Why it works: A business with a revolving door of customers is hardly a business at all: it has to keep acquiring just to stand still, and if it falls behind at all, it dies.
- Bad → Good: A gym at 15% monthly churn loses 83% of its members within a year: starting January 1st with 250 members, it needs 208 new clients by December 31st only to stay level, and at 12.5% margins, falling two customers a month behind loses the gym (2025).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «At 15% month-over-month churn, a gym owner loses 83% of their members in a year.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist - opening

#### 14.2. Retention budget of one fifth of CAC
- **Rule:** Set the per-customer retention budget at one fifth of the cost to acquire a customer (CAC).
- Why it works: Acquiring a new customer costs five to twenty-five times more than retaining one, so money spent on keeping customers returns far more (2025).
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «1/5th of CAC and you’d be amazed how far that goes.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Why Is Reducing Churn So Important?

#### 14.3. Churn varies by industry: structural churn
- **Rule:** Read a churn figure against the customers' industry: when the customers are very small business owners (VSMBs), count the churn from customers going out of business as structural, accept that this makes it a high-churn business by nature, and still work to decrease it rather than reading the high number as a failure of the retention work.
- Why it works: Customers who go out of business leave however well they are served, so a business serving VSMBs is a high-churn business by nature, which the author calls okay.
- Bad → Good: Shopify and Skool are both large businesses whose customers are, by percentage, very small businesses (2025).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «If you are in an industry where you serve VSMBs (very small business owners),» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Author Note: Churn Varies By Industry

## Find the activation point

#### 14.4. All Roads Lead To Activation: the activation point first
- **Rule:** Put finding the activation point first in any churn work, ahead of every other item of the Churn Checklist, and state it as a template: every customer who does X or gets Y result stays longer than the customers who do not.
- Why it works: Activation points are leading indicators of retention and have the greatest effect on churn of anything on the checklist; segmenting customers by engagement makes every other retention tool more effective.
- Bad → Good: Typical activation points, given only as common ones: first leads for a B2B service, first login and dashboard for software, first use for a consumable (2025).
- When not to apply: Activation points are different for every company: never borrow another company's; derive yours from your own churned and retained customers (14.5) and retest it every 6–12 months.
- Anchor: «Activation points refer to leading indicators of customer retention.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #1: Figure Out Your Activation Points

#### 14.5. How to find your activation point
- **Rule:** Find the activation point in five moves — gather all the data on churned customers; keep those who stayed three months or longer; order them by money spent and take the top 20%; learn everything about them (demographics, psychographics, income, business size, revenue); work out how they used your stuff and how you treated them, keep the factors they share, narrow them to five candidates and work down the list — then update the messaging to attract those customers and the onboarding to drive to the activation point.
- Why it works: A common factors analysis keeps only what repeats across many cases, which strips out one-off bias and shows the core; what the longest-staying, highest-spending customers share is the stuff that keeps people from leaving, and once you find it you can do that stuff.
- Bad → Good: Gym Launch had 8% churn against a 4% target; asking "When do people first get value from the product?" showed that leavers never made their investment back quickly while the longest-staying customers made it back within 30 days, so The Fast Cash Play pushed every client to recoup the investment inside 30 days and churn fell from 8% to 3% within 6 months; a competitor running ads to everyone in fitness sold as many people at about the same CAC, but a first-year customer was worth $5,000 to him and $42,000 to the author, 70x more profit (example — Retention Playbook, Churn Checklist #1, bonus: update your messaging).
- When not to apply: Three months is a convention, not a finding: use whatever time window you want; retest the activation point and the messaging every 6–12 months.
- Anchor: «Here’s how to find your activation point:.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #1: Figure Out Your Activation Points

## Onboard and activate

#### 14.6. Onboard to the activation point
- **Rule:** Onboard every new customer, and sales prospects too, in five moves: outline how to get value and drive them to the activation point; resell what they bought in terms of their own goals; tell them how they unlock more value the longer they stay; tell them the best ways to communicate with you, your team and other customers, so they always know when you will speak next; follow up. Make it as personal as the business allows: custom beats generic, personal beats group, live beats recorded, some beats none.
- Why it works: Onboarding here means one thing, teaching customers to hit the activation points you investigated, and it sets up long-term communication rather than a one-off welcome; done for prospects, it raises the close rate.
- Bad → Good: A portfolio company went from no onboarding to group onboarding and, after a year and change, to one-on-one in a huge volume business: 25% more ascensions, and the higher LTV let it scale ad spend, one of the largest factors in going from $2M per month to $2M per week; the best communities on Skool have a new member make a post with a set structure and comment on 1–2 others, tell them where to start, connect 4–6 people to each other right after onboarding, tell them what unlocks once the homework gets them to level 3, and set as homework what to cancel or stop paying for to free up the money for the membership (2025).
- When not to apply: The form is free (booklet, video, call, event) as long as it teaches the activation point; onboarding in the looser senses the word carries elsewhere is not what this rule asks for.
- Anchor: «Resell the value of the purchase. Important! Frame it within the context of their» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #2: Onboard Your Customers

#### 14.7. Incentivize customer activation with unlockables
- **Rule:** Reward the activation behaviours with unlockables the customer values — courses that unlock, 1-1 consulting calls or bundles of calls at a set duration or status, tickets to live or virtual events, access to higher-tier calls, custom badges and profile markers that say they are "in" — and time extra unlocks just after the major churn points: past the average lifespan, then 30 days after, then 3 months later, then 3 months later again.
- Why it works: A vast majority of Skool host cancellations come from the bottom two tiers of engagement, and the more a customer uses the product the longer they stay; an unlock just past a churn point holds the customers who are on the edge (2025).
- Bad → Good: Most customers leave after the third month → something cool unlocks in month four and holds the ones on the edge.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «You figured out what the perfect customer does. You onboard all new customers to do» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #3: Incentivize Customer Activation; Action Steps Summary

#### 14.8. The four milestones of the customer journey
- **Rule:** Build the customer journey on four milestones every customer should reach — activate, testimonial, refer and ascend — and give the customer something at each one.
- Why it works: Over time customers want to buy more, from you or from the guy down the street; a customer who just bought another thing from you is the least likely to churn, so ascension saves the churn and adds revenue at once.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Activation comes first; testimonial, referral and ascension may happen in different orders depending on the customer and the business model.
- Anchor: «every customer to do: activate, testimonial, refer, ascend.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #9: Make a Customer Journey

## Contact, surveys and the 5 Horsemen

#### 14.9. The 5 Horsemen of Retention
- **Rule:** In a gym, run all five horsemen: track attendance (three or more visits a week sticks, twice a week or less churns); reach out to every member twice a week to praise participation and progress and solve their small problems; send handwritten cards at sign-up and at the three-, six- and twelve-month milestones when you ask for referrals; run member events every 21, 42 or 63 days on an internal schedule, regular to you and random to them; talk to anyone who announces they want to leave before they go (exit interview).
- Why it works: The five are the common factors of gym owners with under 3% month-over-month churn for the last 6 months; cutting churn from 9% to 3% is a 3.3x increase in lifetime value, and events cut churn and bring referrals at the same time (2025).
- Bad → Good: Rolled out across the gyms, churn went up 50% in month 1, then down 50% in month 2 and another 50% in month 3: 10% to 15%, then 15% to 7%, then 7% to 3% (2025).
- When not to apply: Attendance is the observable behaviour of a gym membership; in another business track your own activation point (14.4) instead. Expect churn to rise in month one ("shaking the three": people who would have cancelled anyway, plus people who stopped consuming while automated billing ran on) and do not stop the work for it; judge it on months two and three.
- Anchor: «I found 5 common factors – which became The 5 Horsemen of Retention.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), The 5 Horsemen of Retention

#### 14.10. Intercept Usage Churn
- **Rule:** Treat a customer who still pays but has stopped using the product as churning, and raise contact at the moment usage drops rather than waiting for the cancellation.
- Why it works: Usage churn is a leading indicator of a real cancellation (a customer on an annual contract who stops using the service at six months is likely to cancel at renewal), while churn proper is only counted once they leave.
- Bad → Good: A gym member's churn signature runs 3 sessions a week, then 2, 2, 1, cancel; talking to them the moment attendance drops to two, in week 2 and not later, turns the same member into 3, 2 with a reach-out, then 3, 3, 3 (example — Retention Playbook, The 5 Horsemen of Retention).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «But if you intercept them right when they stop using it and get them to» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #3, Pro Tip: Usage Churn

#### 14.11. The ACA framework for check-ins
- **Rule:** Between larger surveys, contact each customer one on one every two to three weeks with a message in three beats: Acknowledge what you have seen them do, Compliment them on something, Ask a question.
- Why it works: One-on-one reach-outs at that cadence kept more customers than no contact at all.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Only where the price point allows it; and, counterintuitively, wealthier customers typically want less handholding, so dial the cadence down as customer wealth goes up.
- Anchor: «with customers. The subject of the reach out followed the ACA framework. Acknowl-» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #8; Action Steps Summary

#### 14.12. The two-question survey
- **Rule:** Twice a year, show customers the full list of what you provide and ask which single item they would keep if everything else were removed, and which single item they would least mind losing if everything else stayed; then do more of what they keep and cut what they would drop.
- Why it works: The answers reveal the core two or three things the product actually does for them; overwhelm is the number one reason for churn, so retention comes from making sure customers consume the value rather than burying them in it: value per second, not seconds of value.
- Bad → Good: A friend's $500k-per-month newsletter had its lowest churn while delivering two things, one Q&A call a month held until every question was answered and one long physical newsletter a month; everything he added after that, starting with weekly calls, increased churn, and Gym Launch is on its 10th version, each shorter and simpler than the last (example — Retention Playbook, Provide On-Going Value To Get On-Going Customers).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source; the cut to the core two or three deliverables that follows is 13.22.
- Anchor: «removed everything on this list but one, what would you want to keep the most?” and fol-» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #8

## Community and bad customers

#### 14.13. Community linking
- **Rule:** Tie members to each other rather than only to you, in four moves: host group events; introduce a member to another member they would benefit from knowing; start a community podcast that interviews members; elevate micro-celebrities by calling them out in public channels and praising their specific expertise.
- Why it works: It is easy to quit a membership and hard to leave a relationship, and facilitating many deeper connections scales better than one single tie to the group; they come for the bikini, they stay for the community.
- Bad → Good: A woman running a Skool group moved from one-on-one onboarding to one-on-six, not to make it less personal but to introduce members to each other on purpose, and churn was low (2025).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Action Step: Host group events. Manually connect people. Start a community podcast.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #4: Community Linking

#### 14.14. Correct or fire bad customers: three strikes
- **Rule:** Hold customers to a stated standard: tell anyone who breaks it what was wrong and what good looks like, remove a customer after three strikes, and reward good behaviour where everyone can see it.
- Why it works: Bad customers make it bad for everyone else, while rewarding the best contributions in public elevates those members, gives everyone fresh value and signals what gets rewarded.
- Bad → Good: In a Skool group: remove bad posts, telling the poster why they sucked and what good looks like; three strikes on bad posters; pin the best 1–2 new posts daily; give the group four post categories, wins (which become testimonials), fun, discovered (shared with data and proof) and meetups (2025).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Give 3 strikes on bad posters.» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #5: Correct or Fire Bad Customers

## Payment terms

#### 14.15. Add Annual Payment Options
- **Rule:** Let customers who want to stay longer pay to stay longer, in one of three forms: an annual billing option priced at "buy 10 months get 2 free", a big one-time up-front payment with small monthly payments, or a founder rate (a discount for the business owner, starting at 50% off); price the annual option above what the average customer now spends over their stay, and at buy 10 get 2 free when the average stay runs past 12 months.
- Why it works: Paying for a longer stay makes customers stay longer: those who take the annual option stay a full year, which extends the average stay per customer; to raise annual take-up without removing the monthly option, make the annual discount steeper.
- Bad → Good: Priced at buy 10 months get 2 free, typically 10–20% of people take the annual option (2025).
- When not to apply: The Pricing playbook's ladder of billing cycles is 13.2, and its take-up figure for an annual option on a page (10–15%) is that playbook's, the 10–20% here the Retention playbook's (2025). Making annual the only way to pay decreases sales as well as churn, which sometimes makes more money overall: make it mandatory only where you sell by phone or webinar, and in website checkout offer it alongside monthly; offer a founder rate only while the gross margins are still there.
- Anchor: «front with a smaller monthly. Alternatively, create a founders price which incentivizes people» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #6: Add Annual Payment Options

#### 14.16. Big head, long tail
- **Rule:** Match the pricing model to the business model: when the offer carries both one-time and ongoing value, charge a big one-time fee priced on the one-time value (education, implementation or setup) and a small recurring fee priced on the consumable value, and sell the two together in one purchase, never the recurring fee as a second sale.
- Why it works: Each part is appropriately priced, the recurring base keeps stacking, the upsell rate is likely high, and the price anchor between the big first purchase and the small recurring one is what drives the retention.
- Bad → Good: Big one-time fee first, small recurring fee sold separately afterwards, and the price anchor is lost → $6,800 up front for education or setup with $199 a month for the community and everything after in the same purchase, where 30 months of the $199 lifts LTV from $6,800 to $12,800 (2025).
- When not to apply: The head needs a genuine one-time value such as education or setup to be priced on; the recurring part is priced to the consumable value alone, which may be far less. The Lost Chapters' version, initiation fees and up-front commitment as the barrier to exit, is 12.29; the Pricing playbook's billing rule behind it is 13.3.
- Anchor: «Example: I call this a “big head, long tail” where you might charge $6,800» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #6: Add Annual Payment Options

## Saving cancellations

#### 14.17. Cancellation calls are required and taken
- **Rule:** Tell every new customer at onboarding that cancelling requires a call (an exit interview), treat a cancellation notice as the signal to book it, and get the cancelling customer on the phone before they leave.
- Why it works: You can usually save half of the people who get on a cancellation call, the chance of saving them is higher on a call than by email or text, and the other half tell you how to improve; assuming half show up, that cuts churn by about 25%, a 33% increase in LTV (2025).
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: In a high volume, low price business where getting on the phone with churning customers does not pay, use a cancellation video instead that reminds them why they started, resells them and names what they risk losing by cancelling, though the author adds that the call almost always pays through what you learn.
- Anchor: «Tell  new  customers  you  do  cancellation  calls.  Get  the  customers  on  the» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #7: Exit Interviews or Cancellation Videos, Action Step

#### 14.18. Two ways to save a cancellation
- **Rule:** On the cancellation call, let the customer vent and be more upset about it than they are, then ask what would have to happen for them to be happy; save the saveable half either with a redo (you give it another shot and make it right) or with an upsell into the higher program they should have been in, crediting their payment toward it; take the feedback of the rest.
- Why it works: There is room for only one person in the angry boat, and a customer who feels validated instead of fought still wants the result and does not want to leave; their answer is a roadmap to the expectation that went unmet.
- Bad → Good: In text or email: invite the venting, out-upset them, validate completely, then ask whether they would be happy if those things were fixed; and tell them what they lose by leaving, such as custom URLs, posts, access and founder pricing (2025).
- When not to apply: About half of the people who take the call are unsaveable; their feedback, not a save, is the payoff.
- Anchor: «stuff. The other half, you can save them in one of two ways:» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Churn Checklist #7: Exit Interviews or Cancellation Videos

## Referrals: give more value

#### 14.19. Six Ways To Get More Referrals By Giving More Value
- **Rule:** Before and alongside any referral ask, build goodwill through the six ways, each mapped onto a part of an ad: Call Outs → Sell Better Customers (14.20); Dream Outcome → Set Better Expectations (14.21); Increase Perceived Likelihood of Achievement → Get More People Better Results (14.22); Decrease Time Delay → Get Faster Results (14.23); Decrease Effort and Sacrifice → Keep Making Your Stuff Better (14.25); Call to Action → Tell Them What To Buy Next, which the source names without elaborating; the nearest rules are the next offer (11.1) and the ascend milestone (14.8).
- Why it works: The difference between price and value is goodwill; lots of goodwill creates word of mouth, and word of mouth means referrals; price can only be lowered so far for so long, so the lever is giving more value, which on its own does a fantastic job of getting referrals.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «There are six ways I get referrals by giving more value. And it just so» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.20. Call Outs → Sell Better Customers
- **Rule:** When a business has sales but high churn and a plateau, find the traits shared by the customers who succeed most, retarget the advertising call outs at that narrower group, and sell only people who meet those criteria.
- Why it works: Increase the quality of the prospect and you increase the quality of the product: the customers who get the most value have the most goodwill, and those are the most likely to refer.
- Bad → Good: A portfolio company doing PR for generic small businesses had plenty of sales, heavy churn and years of plateau; its lowest-churning customers were all in one niche and raising money from investors, only 15% of the business; once the call outs matched that customer, the plateau broke, growth resumed toward millions per month, advertising got cheaper because the messaging could be more specific, and the new customers referred like clockwork (2023).
- When not to apply: Narrowing to the best-fit group puts the rest of the current business at risk, in the example the other 85% of it.
- Anchor: «Figure out what your most successful customers have in common. Use those» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.21. Dream Outcome → Set Better Expectations
- **Rule:** Lower the promises in the offer step by step until close rates start to drop, then stop lowering and hold them there.
- Why it works: You set the expectations and they shape the experience itself; promises low enough to exceed leave room to overdeliver, which maximises both the number of customers and the goodwill built with them.
- Bad → Good: Promising everything and the kitchen sink to close more deals, which turned fulfilment into a nightmare → lowering the promises until close rates dip, then stopping (2023).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «making offers. Keep lowering them until your close rates lower. At that» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.22. Get More People Better Results: the six-step process
- **Rule:** To lift average customer results, find what the best customers did and make everyone do it: survey customers to find those with the best results; interview them on what they did differently; find the actions they had in common; make every new customer repeat those actions; measure the change in average results, speed and outcome; tie your guarantee's conditions to those actions so more people do them.
- Why it works: The customers with the best results get the most value from the product, so making everyone repeat their actions raises the average result, and with it testimonials and referrals.
- Bad → Good: Gym Launch tracked speed to the first paid ad, speed to the first sale and attendance on calls, and found that gym owners who ran paid ads and made a sale in the first seven days had triple the LTGP; it forced everyone to launch ads and sell inside seven days, and average results, testimonials and referrals all rose (2023).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Here's the process I use to get more people better results:» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.23. Decrease Time Delay → Get Faster Results
- **Rule:** Make wins feel faster by giving them more often, a win being any positive experience of the customer: deliver small things at shorter intervals rather than all at once; on bigger projects share progress updates as often as is reasonable, even without progress, because updates are wins; force as many wins as you can into the 48 hours after purchase; always tell customers when they will next hear from you (BAMFAM, 14.24); add fifty percent to every timeline so you deliver early and never late.
- Why it works: Faster wins raise the perception of speed, the likelihood they stick and how much they trust you; customers form their lasting impression of a business within forty-eight hours of buying; customers should never be expected to forgive lateness; and if you said seven things would happen and all seven do, referring a friend becomes lower risk.
- Bad → Good: A week of work delivered in one go → the same work delivered as daily updates: the same progress with seven times the wins (2023).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Here's five ways I make wins happen faster in the real» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.24. BAMFAM
- **Rule:** End every customer contact with the next one booked (BAMFAM, Book-A-Meeting-From-A-Meeting, a saying the author credits to a public CEO friend, named in the Lead Nurture Playbook as Sharran Srivatsaa), so the customer always leaves knowing when they will next hear from you; never close with "we will circle back later" or an offer to follow up offline.
- Why it works: People are likelier to show up to appointments you scheduled, you have the best chance of scheduling one while you are with them in real time, and a customer left in no man's land does not know what happens next.
- Bad → Good: In an insurance company the author bought, prospects dropped off between the first and the second call while reps built and sent qualified plans in between, lots of work for low return; the one change, BAMFAM, never ending a call with "we will circle back later", made second-call show rates skyrocket and sales rise (example — Lead Nurture Playbook, Pillar IV: Volume, booking the next appointment at the current one).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source; the move is spelled out for the setter in 08.18, for the closer in 09.27 and for the upsell in 11.4.
- Anchor: «a slick saying from a public CEO friend of mine - BAMFAM:» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals - Word of Mouth, Decrease Time Delay

#### 14.25. Decrease Effort and Sacrifice → Keep Making Your Stuff Better
- **Rule:** Run a monthly loop on the product: find the most common problem from customer service data, surveys and reviews; design the fix with feedback from the customers who made the product work despite that problem; build it into the product; give the new version to a small group of struggling customers; if the next round of feedback shows the problem solved, roll it out to everyone, otherwise go back to the fix; then take the next most common problem, and never stop.
- Why it works: There is no perfect product; the easier you make it for customers to benefit, the more goodwill you get and the more likely they are to refer.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «the more likely they'll refer. Here's my process to keep making» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

## Referrals: ask

#### 14.26. Treat the referral ask like an offer
- **Rule:** Build every referral ask as an offer that shows the value the customer gets for referring, big enough to outweigh the risk to their goodwill with the friend.
- Why it works: Referring is always a risk (the customer stakes their goodwill with the friend in the hope of getting more of it), so they refer only when they think it very likely the friend will have a good experience and the benefit to them outweighs the risk; the author tried many referral strategies and most failed until he treated the ask this way.
- Bad → Good: A new salesman shattered a portfolio company's ticket sales records by doing one thing differently: he asked each buyer whom they would want to bring along, then asked for the introduction, and half his sales were referrals; the scripted form shows the value to the buyer: people who do our program with someone else tend to get 3x the results — who else could you do this program with? (example — $100M Leads, #1 Customer Referrals, Seven Ways To Ask For Referrals)
- When not to apply: Incentives raise only the benefit side; the risk side is lowered only by goodwill, by showing you deliver on your promises (14.19).
- Anchor: «Asking for referrals only works when you treat it like an» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.27. Seven Ways To Ask For Referrals
- **Rule:** Combine an incentive, a currency and an ask from the seven ways: One-Sided Referral Benefit — pay your average CAC to the referrer or the friend (the default for spouses and households) and ask for an actual three-way introduction by call, SMS or email, not a name and a number, right when they buy; Two-Sided Referral Benefits — split the CAC, half to the referrer in cash or credit and half to the friend in credit; Ask For A Referral Right When They Buy — on the checkout page or the sales contract, with how they get better results doing it with a friend; Add Referrals As A Negotiation Chip (14.28); Referral Events — points, credits, dollars or bragging rights for bringing friends within an explicit window, typically one to four weeks; Ongoing Referral Programs — talk about the benefits of doing things with others all the time, in free content, outreach and paid ads; Unlockable Referral Bonuses — VIP bonuses, courses, tokens, status, training, merchandise or premium support for people who both refer and leave a testimonial.
- Why it works: The money goes on acquisition either way and the author would rather pay customers than a platform; incentives on both sides are what Dropbox and PayPal used, and Dropbox 39x'd in fifteen months while PayPal reached a million users in two years (2023).
- Bad → Good: The author's local businesses, on a $500 program with a $200 CAC, paid $100 cash to the referrer and gave $100 off to the friend, good for up to three friends (2023).
- When not to apply: The ask capitalizes on goodwill already built with the six ways (14.19), so it comes after or alongside them.
- Anchor: «you a hundred variations that may or may not work, here are the seven» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals, Seven Ways To Ask For Referrals

#### 14.28. Add Referrals As A Negotiation Chip
- **Rule:** When a prospect negotiates on price, trade the discount for introductions instead of giving it: hold the price unless they introduce a few friends in a three-way text on the spot, and when a full-price customer finds out, offer them the same discount on the same terms.
- Why it works: You can ethically charge a different price for the same thing because you changed the terms of the sale, and the objection ends either way: they back off or they give you three friends.
- Bad → Good: Price $500, prospect at $400 → the price stays at $500 down, but the initiation fee is cut if they text-introduce a few friends in a 3-way chat right now; to the full-price customer who hears of it: Stacy got $100 off because she referred three friends, and the same $100 is yours for three friends, so who do you have in mind? (2023)
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Ex: "I can't do anything less than \$500 down, but if you make a 3-way» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals - Word of Mouth, Seven Ways To Ask For Referrals

#### 14.29. The referral gift card
- **Rule:** In a gift-card referral promotion, give every customer a gift card worth about one third of the cost of their program to hand to a friend who signs up, and make it expire 7–14 days after it is handed over.
- Why it works: The deadline forces the referrer to use it, and handing a friend a gift card gives the referrer status and feels like a much bigger deal than a discount.
- Bad → Good: "Join my program for $2000 off" → "I got this gift card for $2000, do you want it? I don't want to waste it"; pair it with the three-way introduction by texting a picture of the card with the friend's name written on it, which also gives a reason to ask for the name; the cards can also be sold at ninety percent off to friends of customers, so the referrer looks generous and you get paid to acquire (2023).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Give the gift card an expiration date within seven to fourteen days from» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals

#### 14.30. The Referral Process
- **Rule:** Right after closing and collecting payment, and whenever a customer opens a text thread with anything positive, ask for referrals in four steps: compliment them on being amazing to work with; ask which people as successful as they are they would want to bring along, a second compliment that also improves their experience; on a yes, have them open a group chat and give them the exact words to paste; after one introduction, ask for more. If the call runs out of time, carry the ask over to text.
- Why it works: Referrals take the least work per sale and give the highest return on time (the author's own figures: one extra deal a day and over 30% of sales from referrals, 2025); business owners are not offended by the ask, especially when it comes with a compliment, and a customer who texts good news wants you to sell them.
- Bad → Good: Skipping the ask for fear of sounding like a douche → straight after payment: "you're the exact type of business owner we love to work with", then the ask for people as successful as them, backed by a reason (a few spots left on that date), then the group text that names the event, the invitation and the rep and ends with "I'll let you guys take it from here", then "anyone else come to mind?" (2025).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Asking for referrals gets you the simplest, cheapest, fastest, easiest, highest-converting opportunities.» — ACQ Closer Handbook (2025), Referrals, Referral Process
