# Ad Kaleidoscope and ad frameworks: multiply a winning ad, pick a proven structure for a new one, check a creative

Open this sheet when an ad has won and needs more versions, when a new creative needs a proven structure, or when a creative must be checked before or after it runs.
It holds the Ad Kaleidoscope, the 20 ad frameworks of the ACQ Advertising Handbook's Blackbook (one rule each, in the handbook's four sections), the rules those frameworks share, and the Best Ad Framework Checklist; figures are from that handbook (2025), and names such as Skool, Tesla or Cybertruck are dated examples.
Making the first ad (callout, What-Who-When, CTA, budgets) and the hook assembly of GOATed Ads are in sheet 06; LTV:CAC as the measure of what an ad brings is in sheet 01.

## The Ad Kaleidoscope

#### 07.1. The Ad Kaleidoscope: Identify, Remix, Remake, Try New
- **Rule:** As soon as a single ad wins, without waiting for a set of winners, run it through the four steps Identify Winners, Remix Winners, Remake Winners, Try New Ideas, putting 80% of the ad-making effort into permutations of winners and 20% into totally new ads, and restart the cycle on every new winner while the old winners stay in the pool.
- Why it works: It harnesses power law (the Pareto Principle): 80% of results come from the 20% of ads that win, so permuting what is already proven yields more winners than starting from scratch every time; it is the author's "more, better, new" growth strategy applied to advertising.
- Bad → Good: Two advertisers each make 100 ads in a month: one makes 100 brand-new ads → the other takes his top five ads of the month before and makes 100 versions of those five, and the author says there is no question he wins the next month.
- When not to apply: Not before any ad has won: ads that do not exist yet cannot be optimized, so an advertiser with no winner uses the 20 ad frameworks (07.7–07.26) and volume (07.2) instead.
- Anchor: «The Ad Kaleidoscope is my tool for taking winning ads and multiplying them.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Part I Kaleidoscope Ads / How To Use The Ad Kaleidoscope

#### 07.2. Find winners by volume, not by luck
- **Rule:** Produce winners by deliberate volume: until a first winner exists, run as many ads as you can; in a shoot, vary hooks, meat, CTAs and backgrounds across many ads; once one wins, cut a large batch of variations of it instead of waiting for another lucky hit.
- Why it works: Random hits get romanticized, but volume cancels out luck: winners come out of deliberate work, and there is no magic in it.
- Bad → Good: Waiting for an ad to get lucky → one shoot day of about 250 ads varying hooks, meat, CTAs, background colours and shirts; the winner of that day (the Bribe ad, 07.19) was cut into 200-plus further variations and its meat has been reused hundreds of times.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «People romanticize random hits, but volume negates luck.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III, Ad Framework #3: Bribe

#### 07.3. Remixing Winners: post-production permutations first
- **Rule:** Right after a winner is identified, make its remixes, all on a computer from the raw footage you already have, in order from easiest to hardest: New Speed (1.1–1.2x, never so fast the voice sounds weird), New Filters, New Background/Border, New Fonts/Captions, New Headline, New Medium (a frame or the hook as a static image), New Format (a version native to each placement on each platform), New Meat (the winning hook and CTA around a different middle section already recorded), New Effects; they go up first while the remakes (07.4) are produced.
- Why it works: Remixes are the fastest variations to make, so they keep ads performing and buy time for the remakes; hook, meat and CTA are swappable parts, so a new meat under the winning hook yields a new ad without a new shoot and without losing what made the ad win.
- Bad → Good: One winning video run as one ad → the same footage under the headline of another winning ad, Hook 1 and CTA 1 kept with Meat 2 spliced in, and the hook exported as a set of four static images asking for the viewer's email to send free stuff; the photo versions were big winners too (example — ACQ Advertising Handbook, Ad Kaleidoscope Checklist, shown at Remixing Winners).
- When not to apply: Remixes wear out sooner than remakes because they look more like the original, so they buy time rather than keep a winner alive for long; months or years of life take remakes (07.4).
- Anchor: «Your first run through the kaleidoscope, you’ll use the same raw footage of your previous winners, but vary the edits.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Part I Remixing Winners

#### 07.4. Remaking Winners: re-record the winning script alongside the remixes
- **Rule:** At the same time as the remixes, not after them, remake the winner: record it again with the winning script kept and change only real-world components, from easiest to hardest: New Clone (just hit record again), New Props, New Examples (names and numbers in the same script), New Setting, New Talent (people who look like the buyers you want more of), New Combination (several changes at once, remix edits included).
- Why it works: The variation is significant while the script that converted stays intact, so the new version stays likely to convert; remakes cost a new recording but can keep a winner performing for months and in some cases years, and even a plain re-record differs because no two sessions share time of day, weather, lighting and sound.
- Bad → Good: Running only remixes, or waiting to re-film before shipping anything → both tracks at once; the Underdog ad (07.20) re-recorded the next month with the lowest performer's $4,664 and name swapped for $6,760 and another name, the rest unchanged, worked again; a product for midwestern men aged 18–25 gets more versions starring other 18–25-year-old midwestern men, and to get more women, use more women.
- When not to apply: Takes a willingness to record again and more effort than remixing, which is why the remixes go up first.
- Anchor: «This means we're actually changing the real world components of the ad.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Part I Remaking Winners

#### 07.5. Run a winner until it stops working
- **Rule:** Keep a working ad running until its performance dies, never retire it because you are tired of seeing it, and once it dies, rerun it through Kaleidoscope variations.
- Why it works: The advertiser sees his ad every day, far more often than the market does, so he tires of it long before prospects even learn his name; the campaign is made for the prospects, not for the person who made it.
- Bad → Good: Replacing a converting ad because the advertiser is bored of it → hammering it until its numbers drop, then feeding it to the Kaleidoscope (07.1).
- When not to apply: The only stopping signal is the ad's performance; the rule covers ads that are still performing.
- Anchor: «And when an ad finally works, keep running it until it stops working—never until» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), PART I: AD KALEIDOSCOPE

#### 07.6. "New" means new to the viewer
- **Rule:** When an ad needs a fresh version, make something new to the viewer, a permutation of what already converted, rather than an ad started from zero.
- Why it works: Buyers call something new far sooner than its maker does, so a variation reads as new while keeping what made the original convert.
- Bad → Good: Treating a new ad as one built from scratch → the highest-converting Skool ad for several months (a man in a Skool cap with question marks around his head, over $100,000 spent on that one creative) permuted into neon, plexus, purple, rainbow, retro, white and yellow versions and versions with a different prop, each performing like the original.
- When not to apply: Not a reason to drop step 4 of 07.1: the other 20% of effort still goes into totally new ideas.
- Anchor: «New does not mean “start from zero”. New means new to the viewer.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Part I What The Ad Kaleidoscope Is

## Ad frameworks, Section I

#### 07.7. Ad Framework #1: Personal Testimonial/Origin Story
- **Rule:** When you can name a situation that makes it less than ideal for a customer to get the outcome, build the ad as one open loop: a wild claim in the first five seconds, yourself in an obviously "wrong" environment, a rapid problems tour naming aloud every objection prospects raise as anti-proof, more stacked negatives, then snap the loop closed with overwhelming proof tied to the opening claim, one plain connecting sentence, a takeaway aimed at the limiting belief the prospect holds, and a soft CTA.
- Why it works: The whole ad builds mystery while eliminating, inside the ad, the implied objections about why the thing would not work in the viewer's circumstances, and it pays the loop off with a massive piece of proof.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: a claim of 191 signups in 19 days → a shaky selfie walk through a new gym in a sketchy residential neighbourhood, each flaw (bars on the windows, stairs people fear, a room like an apartment) named as a damaging admission with a curiosity line → a stack of $500 bills of signed contracts → the lesson that you can sell wherever you want.
- When not to apply: Needs anti-proof: a circumstance that makes the outcome look unlikely for the customer.
- Anchor: «This ad framework functions like one gigantic open loop. The entire ad builds mystery.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section I Ad Framework #1: Personal Testimonial/Origin Story

#### 07.8. Ad Framework #2: Prop Comedy
- **Rule:** For a short, fast-cut ad meant only to get the click, script three to four Yes-questions covering dream outcome, speed, ease and proof (the value-equation questions), each line three to five seconds at most and the last one a CTA; give each line its own prop and mini-setting, the first a setting the avatar recognizes; film each line as a separate clip, link the clips with whip-cuts (end on a fast pan, start the next clip continuing it), lay upbeat music and captions over them, and freeze the final CTA card for one full second.
- Why it works: The ad aims at one thing, the right person clicking with the right intent, and lets music and visuals do much of the work; a recognizable first setting tells the avatar the ad is for them, the whip-cut momentum drags viewers forward, and the frozen card gives them time to read it and act.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original, a Dollar Shave Club style ad: gym owner call-out filmed in a gym → high-ticket online clients (dream outcome) → in as little as 30 days (speed) → it's possible, we're already doing it (perceived likelihood) → click, download the case studies, schedule a call, on a card frozen for a second with the spokesperson pointing at it.
- When not to apply: Where a prop cannot be matched to its line, make the prop fun and interesting instead.
- Anchor: «These types of ads don’t try to do too much. The point is to get the right person to click with the right intent.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section I Ad Framework #2: Prop Comedy

#### 07.9. Ad Framework #3: Emotional Avatar Testimonial
- **Rule:** For a testimonial ad, pick a client who looks like your avatar and film them in a setting that shows it; brief them on six points (life before personally, before professionally, the skepticism they had, why they acted anyway, life after personally, after professionally); cut within half a second to a gloomy shot of the same setting, open on a blunt pain statement, stack five to six rapid-fire pains and stakes on jump-cuts, mark the turning point with a switch to upbeat music, keep the work short, then show the dream outcome with numbers, its speed and ease, one brief damaging admission, a condensed reason why and one crystal-clear CTA on screen.
- Why it works: Talent that looks and talks like the avatar tells viewers the ad is for them, the damaging admission raises credibility and lowers skepticism, and the work only needs a mention to set realistic expectations; one such ad was among the author's best ever, and over 100 variations of it all performed.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: a couple in front of their gym → half-second cut to the empty gym with emotional music → two months from shutting the doors → 31 members, about four grand a month, a second pregnancy, a second job → turning point → 31 to over 200 members, full staff hired, stepped away in four and a half months → damaging admission → reason why → one CTA; the rhythm runs personal stakes → personal, then professional pain → turning point → work → professional, then personal good → skepticism → reason why → CTA.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Pick a client and record them in a setting that communicates that they’re like your avatar visually.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section I Ad Framework #3: Emotional Avatar Testimonial

#### 07.10. Ad Framework #4 Show Don’t Tell
- **Rule:** When the dream outcome can be filmed, show it happening instead of claiming it: have current customers who match the target avatar film their dream-outcome proof moment, frame it with as few words and numbers as possible, in a high-contrast colour, get people moving in unison if possible, let the proof do the talking, and end on a visual CTA because the ad has no narration.
- Why it works: Proof the prospect can map onto his own situation lowers his perceived risk and so persuades more; proof persuades better than almost anything, a demonstration without words works that much better, and non-verbal clips also tend to get more organic reach.
- Bad → Good: A stranger abroad who says his gym filled up → a lookalike down the street whose queue out the door the prospect can see; the original was a packed class clip with 171 NEW SIGNUPS AT $500 across the top, no copy, and a scan-me button as the CTA.
- When not to apply: Needs a dream outcome that existing customers matching the target avatar can film.
- Anchor: «In short, the closer a prospect can approximate the proof to their situation, the lower the risk, and by extension, the more compelling it is.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section I Ad Framework #4 Show Don’t Tell

#### 07.11. Ad Framework #5 Long Term Result
- **Rule:** When you want the exact advanced customer rather than the genre of customer, show bigger results over longer timelines in language only the upper echelon understands: the avatar in their own workspace with a wild before/after headline, one pain sentence as the hook, two or three more pains that only an advanced owner would recognise, the decision point naming your solution and when they bought it, a Fast Win and a Big Win, deeper metrics (LTV, churn, margin, head count, salaries), the soft life benefits, and a CTA in which the peer does the inviting.
- Why it works: Big results on long timelines, set against the one-trick ponies who talk small results on short timelines, show you are legit; talking to the upper market gets it and the smaller market too, while talking only to beginners never gets the advanced buyer, whom no one else addresses for fear of ostracizing the rest; peers persuade advanced prospects better than the seller does, and a dated decision raises credibility a lot.
- Bad → Good: A get-rich-quick, short-timeline promise that sells only to poor beginners → a gym owner in his gym, before/after of 93 to 321 members and 4x client lifespan, one pain sentence (zero profit, 60-plus hours a week), a dated decision (March 2017), Fast Win (revenue tripled within months) and Big Win ($83,000 a month, 321 members, attrition under 2%, six full-time staff), closing on his own thanks.
- When not to apply: Do not judge it on CAC: its cost per call ran above the author's KPIs and he nearly shut off what became his most successful campaign in aggregate, whose customers cost more but paid more, stayed longer, got better results and referred friends; judge it on LTV:CAC (sheet 01).
- Anchor: «Basically, I contrast my results against the “one trick ponies” in my industry. They’re all talking about small results in short timelines.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section I Ad Framework #5 Long Term Result

## Ad frameworks, Section II

#### 07.12. Ad Framework #1 Looking For 5 Avatars...
- **Rule:** When launching something new, starting paid ads or first monetizing an audience, film in an educational setting and open with the one-liner whose slots are the number and type of avatar, the outcome, the biggest pain point they want to avoid, your reason why, your proof and a keep-watching; say as specifically as possible who you are not looking for, why you take only a limited number, why your thing beats what is out there, split the risk by tying part of your pay to their results, and send them to your assistant to be qualified.
- Why it works: A classic of direct response, it works in any niche and worked 70 years ago as it does today; specificity about who is excluded adds credibility, a compelling reason why takes it to the next level, performance pay lowers the prospect's risk, signals intent to deliver and draws more people, and naming the assistant reinforces authority and the by-application frame.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: whiteboard → qualify by niche and revenue (local lead gen agencies at $15,000 a month or more recurring) and by ambition, looking for five → us versus them (most agency helpers made their money helping agencies, not dominating a niche) → one proof number ($98.5 million in a single niche) → a limited-partner reason why → no pay unless it makes them money → an application, then a call from the executive assistant.
- When not to apply: Needs a compelling reason why you take only a limited number; its place is the start of a launch, of paid ads or of monetizing an audience, though it also works for organic audiences.
- Anchor: «This is one of the most classic direct response ads of all time. It works in any niche.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II Ad Framework #1 Looking For 5 Avatars...

#### 07.13. Ad Framework #2: New vs. Old
- **Rule:** For an advanced, complex or brand-new offer, film it in an educational setting, naming the exact avatar within five seconds, make a big claim with details (a big increase inside the same context: same niche, same leads), attribute all their pain to their outdated model, reveal the new way anchored to one simple metric written on screen, drive it home with a case study or hypothetical whose math they can follow, add one line of authority (a single giant credential or number), reverse the risk if you can, finish with a CTA that previews what happens after the click, and close on your hook.
- Why it works: A hyper-specific call-out filters for qualified prospects; a big increase in the same context creates curiosity; prospects who relate to the old way and its problems are hooked to hear the fix; the worked math stands in for proof; one giant credential holds attention before it fades; and previewing the micro-journey removes friction while keeping the by-application frame.
- Bad → Good: A bare number → the original's skeleton: call-out of lead gen agency owners for local businesses → from $1,500-a-month retainer clients to $15,000-a-month performance clients in the same gym niche → the old model's mechanics (clients cancel the moment value dips below price) → the new way on one metric ($100 per person who walks in the door) → a hypothetical (on day 30 double the ad spend instead of asking to double the retainer, and they say yes) → one proof line → risk reversal → CTA previewing the process.
- When not to apply: Works especially well for more advanced or complex products or services and for something brand new.
- Anchor: «It's a classic ADucational framework. You see these a lot, but few do them well.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II Ad Framework #2: New vs. Old

#### 07.14. Ad Framework #3: Why You Will Never
- **Rule:** For a pain/problem-based ad, stand at a whiteboard, address the exact avatar in the first sentence, establish authority with one credential, rapid-fire the prospect-specific warning signs as numbers with thresholds, then name a non-obvious new solution in one sentence that inverts all of them, and CTA to a lead magnet or video sales letter.
- Why it works: It rests on understanding the prospect's problem and solution better than they do: each added statistic builds pressure until the prospect thinks this person has just named his business's numbers and might be able to fix them.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: the four warning signs an agency will never pass $1M a year → agency owners called out → one authority line (68 companies taken past a million in four years) → lifetime value under $10,000, average revenue per client under $2,500 a month, gross margin under 80%, payback period over 30 days, each in the owner's own arithmetic → a model that reverses all four → a seven-minute video, then a 15-minute call.
- When not to apply: The four thresholds are the author's benchmarks for agency owners (2025); in another niche, list that niche's problems as specifically as you can.
- Anchor: «This pain/problem-based angle is super repeatable in any niche. List all the problems out as specifically as you possibly can.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II Ad Framework #3: Why You Will Never

#### 07.15. Ad Framework #4: The “North Star”
- **Rule:** Where one metric predicts the prospect's ceiling, build the ad on it: a direct call-out plus the nightmare that metric causes, a line of authority, a hierarchy of outcomes by level of the metric in which the prospect can place himself, specific pains at each level as concrete moments, then one clear sentence on how your new method fixes the metric and a CTA to the next step.
- Why it works: A predictive metric lets you predict their pain the way neck and wrist thickness predict body fat, so the ad lists what they want and why it is out of reach for them right now; a narrow focus supplies plenty of concrete examples.
- Bad → Good: Lifetime value as the north star for agencies: under $5,000 you never crack $100–300k a year, near $10,000 a million opens, $15,000 opens the $3 million range, over $20,000 makes a million a month realistic → the plateau blamed on the old retainer model → the reverse royalty model as the way out → a seven-minute video, then a 15-minute call.
- When not to apply: The metric can be anything only if it actually predicts the prospect's pain point; the ladder above is the author's for agencies (2025).
- Anchor: «In this framework, I create a “north star” metric. Then I predict their pain point based on this metric (it can be anything as long as it’s predictive).» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II Ad Framework #4: The “North Star”

#### 07.16. Ad Framework #5: No BS Selfie
- **Rule:** For a top-of-funnel ad that sells only the click to a lead magnet, shoot a very short selfie clip while moving, with something in frame that makes you credible for the topic; open with an if-then pain statement built on a specific number, name the solution in one sentence without explaining it, show proof that someone worse off than the prospect got a better result than the prospect has, and swipe up.
- Why it works: Constraining the time forces the words down to what matters, and the funnel and lead magnet do the rest; proof from someone worse off implies that bigger or more experienced prospects can do even better and makes the prospect think if they could, so can I.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: walking selfie with a big house and a nice car behind → an if-then hook on a $10,000 lifetime-value floor for agency owners → a reverse royalty model, named, not explained → one proof line: $6 million-plus agencies run by kids in their twenties → swipe up; the author learned the move in weight loss, calling the meal plan easy enough for kids and non-English speakers before asking whether the prospect could follow it.
- When not to apply: Only where the funnel carries more information later, since it sells the next step and not the product; the author let a house and a car do the selling only for a brief stint and stopped because he did not want that kind of brand, while calling it effective.
- Anchor: «Think of this as an ad for your lead magnet more than an ad to sell. It sells the next step. Nothing more.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II Ad Framework #5: No BS Selfie

## Ad frameworks, Section III

#### 07.17. Ad Framework #1: Trending Format
- **Rule:** Record the ad in whatever video format is popular on the feed right now so it reads as organic content (a snippet of your show, not an ad), open on a recent surprising discovery, stack speed and scaled proof in one line, then remove every classic objection with proof of both the big and the average outcomes; for the lasting version, take the best organic hooks and bolt a CTA onto your best organic content.
- Why it works: Popular formats are popular for a reason, and organic runs ahead of paid because of the volume and speed at which creators test, so today's organic shows where paid ads will be in six to 12 months.
- Bad → Good: Copying "make a podcast ad" → copying whichever format is popular now; the original, a solo clip made to look like a show snippet with the mic to the mouth, opened on how much people made two weeks into a launch, put the top ten at $10–40k a month from zero within the month in one line, and killed objections with hundreds who made a first dollar online and eight models needing no audience or expertise.
- When not to apply: A directly modeled trend ad works only while that format is still in, the podcast being merely the format of its moment; modeling the concept, mining current organic hooks and formats, is what lasts.
- Anchor: «Trend ads capitalize on current popular video formats. The example I show emerged when podcast clips were my entire feed.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III Ad Framework #1: Trending Format

#### 07.18. Ad Framework #2: Value Anchor
- **Rule:** For an offer-centric ad that gives something away free or cheap, build two stacks that anchor high and then price-drop: ask descending price questions about the dream outcome, explain the offer, anchor it at the marketplace rate, give it free or below the anchor, add a last-minute variable incentive (for top performers, a random selection or everyone) that makes it better than free, then CTA.
- Why it works: A free offer still costs the prospect something, so its value has to be established: the descending anchor makes free feel like an unexpected windfall instead of a marketing cliché, and the variable reward turns zero cost into potential gain.
- Bad → Good: Offering it free with no value set → a three-second keyframe of fanned cash on a red background; a thousand dollars for your dream business in 30 days? a hundred? free? → the 30-day offer → what others charge for such training ($1,000, $5,000, $10,000) dropped to free → the top ten flown to Vegas for a day with the author → join free.
- When not to apply: The source allows a different first question: how much it would be worth to make something bad go away.
- Anchor: «How much would you pay for DREAM OUTCOME? $XXX, $XX, $X? →Explain offer→Anchor value of offer at marketplace rate.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III Ad Framework #2: Value Anchor

#### 07.19. Ad Framework #3: Bribe
- **Rule:** For an offer-driven ad whose landing page and video sales letter do the closing, open with a pattern-interrupt request for their contact information, say what they get in exchange, anchor its value against what comparable products really sell for, tie it to the dream outcome, add an incentive with a reason why if you want, and make the CTA the same as the hook.
- Why it works: Shorter, proof-heavy creatives only have to earn the click and let the page close; the direct ask buys time and signals at once that handing over contact details triggers something valuable.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: a prop swung in from behind the back with a quick ask for the viewer's email, to send a thousand dollars of free stuff → the mechanism and dream outcome (build and monetize a community) → market prices of $1,000, $5,000, $10,000 dropped to free → reason why: the author had just become co-owner of Skool → a CTA that repeats the hook.
- When not to apply: It is built only to earn the click and relies on the landing page and VSL behind it to convert.
- Anchor: «It’s a killer offer-driven ad up front driving to a page that did the heavy lifting.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III Ad Framework #3: Bribe

#### 07.20. Ad Framework #4: Underdog
- **Rule:** When your results form a range, lead with the lowest believable result on your leaderboard as a specific number on a blank screen, name the bigger achievers only to pivot back to that small number, and give a big reason why the promotion is a big deal.
- Why it works: People already know the outcome is cool; what they doubt is that they can reach it, and they already wonder about the average person, so beating them to the punch with the low tier sets believable expectations and earns trust, while the bigger names still attract high achievers.
- Bad → Good: Showing only the ceiling → a large $4,664 on a black screen, what the lowest of 20 on the leaderboard built halfway through → a refusal to talk about the ones at $43,000 or about $30,000 a month → back to four or five thousand a month → reason why → a free 14-day trial; repeated with only the person changed, it outperformed every time.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «What's beautiful about it is it sets low expectations—which is my preference.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III Ad Framework #4: Underdog

#### 07.21. Ad Framework #5: Challenge With Prize
- **Rule:** For a giveaway ad, show on camera a prize that is huge for your specific audience, put it on a short deadline, prove a past winner received it, define winning by one metric that matches your business goal and is simple for both sides to track, show how you will help them win, make not winning valuable too (the worst case is walking away with the free stuff), prove the free thing works, and show what happens the moment they click.
- Why it works: Both outcomes become a win, leaving not doing it as the only bad one; a real peer who won proves the prize winnable and the process legitimate, hand-holding answers the fear of getting stuck before it forms, a single path beats a points system, and showing the signup flow removes the last barrier to conversion.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: a metal ball thrown against a Cybertruck in the first second, a hundred grand, given to one viewer in the next 30 days → one condition (build the largest community on Skool in 30 days) → last month's winner at $40–50k monthly recurring revenue → likelihood and ease (one in two who start a paid community make a first dollar, averaging $1,360 a month; weekly live Q&A) → a free start → a screen recording of the opt-in page.
- When not to apply: Huge cash or a car is not required, it only helps: money, cars and vacations work better the broader the audience, and for a narrow one such as gym owners, re-outfitting their gym would be more compelling.
- Anchor: «To state the obvious, you don’t need to give away massive amounts of cash or a Cybertruck to make your marketing more compelling—» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III Ad Framework #5: Challenge With Prize

## Ad frameworks, Section IV

#### 07.22. Ad Framework #1: Flying Prop
- **Rule:** For a registration or event ad, have the spokesperson catch a real object thrown into frame while looking at the camera, tie the prop to the offer with a one-liner joke, pile on the tangible investment behind it (time, money, relationship capital), tease an unrevealed bonus only those who take the next step get, and leave every loop open so the next step is the only way to close them.
- Why it works: Real motion often beats computer motion; a daisy chain of open loops with one closing action serves both registering and showing up; naming what it cost you approximates its value for them, and withheld details keep tension high until they act.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: a banana thrown at the author in his kitchen, caught and mashed → the book will make you money unlike bananas, but the launch event will be bananas → over a million dollars spent on the event → a secret project four years in the making given away live → the day after his birthday → register.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «The entire ad is curiosity-driven. The entire ad is a daisy chain of open loops with only one action to close them—taking the next step.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section IV Ad Framework #1: Flying Prop

#### 07.23. Ad Framework #2: Bribe (book launch)
- **Rule:** When the free thing cannot be named without killing curiosity, reuse the Bribe hook (ask for contact details, promise outrageously valuable free stuff), justify its value by what it cost you to make, stress that it is a big deal, and describe the unnamed gift as a range between one known low-value and one known high-value item, refusing further details.
- Why it works: People automatically abstract what they will get toward the bigger item, and vagueness lets each viewer's imagination write the perfect copy, which no advertiser can out-write; the loop then closes only by acting.
- Bad → Good: Naming the giveaway (a free t-shirt) → bracketing it as more than a gift card, less than a Tesla, with everyone who shows up getting one; the launch ad's meat was stakes rather than specifics (the season's biggest entrepreneur event, a book two years in the making released live, a secret four-year project).
- When not to apply: Name directly whatever parts can be named; the range and the investment stand in only for what must stay hidden.
- Anchor: «Give a range of values between two known items: one low-value and one high-value.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section IV, Ad Framework #2: Bribe

#### 07.24. Ad Framework #3: The Rumors Are True
- **Rule:** In any market, open by telling them other people already want what you have and it is finally coming out, say what they get for taking the next step, justify its value with receipts that prove your personal investment (hours, iterations, money), add curiosity, close on a purely visual CTA, and once the hook wins, cut permutations of it into static and other formats.
- Why it works: Presenting the product as something others already talk about makes the viewer feel left out of something important, which motivates action; proof that you really spent the investment sharply raises belief in the value; and the static permutations won too, because simple does not mean ineffective.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: the rumors are true → the book is finally coming out → two years, 2,000 hours logged by the author and 1,500 by the editor, over a million dollars into the event → the free book framed as only part of what is coming → more teased → an end card to register, never spoken; statics cut from it (IT'S TRUE., THE RUMORS ARE TRUE...) also won.
- When not to apply: The visual-only CTA is an experiment, especially suited to remixes.
- Anchor: «I position my product as important because  _other people talk about it_. It leverages curiosity.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section IV Ad Framework #3: The Rumors Are True

#### 07.25. Ad Framework #4: You’re Being Lied To
- **Rule:** For an old-way-versus-new-way ad, walk fast toward the camera (round a corner, look into the lens) and tell them they are being lied to, attack the old way as the lie and present the new way as the truth, show proof the new way works, share your selfish reason why, promise speed by reframing how long solving it alone would take, and flash one on-screen next step.
- Why it works: Old versus new is a classic that works, the confrontational walk pairs with the confrontational hook and ads where the author moves almost always outperform, and people trust someone who states his bias openly, even when it runs against their own interest.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: the camera rounds a corner onto the talent walking at it → you're being lied to → the lie that building it brings customers, the truth that telling them lets them find you, which only advertising does → proof (companies built, the last sold for $46 million; over $10 million a month now) → selfish reason: companies that reach $10 million come back for help to $100 million → a decade compressed into hours → a tear-away on-screen CTA.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «It’s another spin on a classic: old way vs. new way. Problem vs. solution. Us vs. them.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section IV Ad Framework #4: You’re Being Lied To

#### 07.26. Ad Framework #5: Data Stack
- **Rule:** Before the shoot, list every wild stat about your product or service on one sheet; then, in front of a half-written whiteboard, promise to blow their mind, rapid-fire the most impressive stats, say how many people have already shown interest, state the true limits of what you can supply, and tell them what to do next.
- Why it works: Big numbers at the start of an ad have seemed to work every time the author used them; a half-filled board makes viewers want to see it finished and is itself a flex of the work; owning a real limit raises demand by cutting perceived supply; and saying a winning thing in several ways gives several winners.
- Bad → Good: Skeleton of the original: a board already half-filled with a column of labels (words, hours, pages, drawings, pro tips, tweets) → this is going to blow your mind → the book's production figures read as one stack (2,000 hours, 107 drawings, two years for one book) → release date and giveaway → 200,000 already registered → a limited number of books → register.
- When not to apply: Collect the stats before recording, and state only the real limits of your supply.
- Anchor: «Numbers at the beginning of ads, especially big numbers, seem to work every time I use them.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section IV Ad Framework #5: Data Stack

## Rules that cut across frameworks

#### 07.27. Reuse proven hooks and formats in other industries
- **Rule:** Before inventing a hook or format for a niche, take one already proven in another industry, on another platform or in your own free content, and swap only the blocks that describe your product, service and benefits; a framework counts as understood only when you can recast it for B2C, B2B, services, SaaS and physical products.
- Why it works: The structures work agnostic of industry and only the blocks change; pros lean on proven playbooks and checklists and cover the rest with volume, and hooks that won on one platform or method are the likeliest to convert when moved to another (GOATed Ads playbook).
- Bad → Good: Dismissing a framework because its example comes from another industry → the same what's-your-email hook run for a community software platform and again for an event launching a book (example — ACQ Advertising Handbook, Part II, Section IV, Ad Framework #2).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «I recycle hooks and formats across industries. You should too. Only amateurs reinvent the wheel.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III, Ad Framework #3: Bribe

#### 07.28. The ADucational setting for complex offers
- **Rule:** Film complex, novel or higher-ticket offers in an educational setting (whiteboard, chalkboard or screen) so the ad teaches while it sells, above all when you have no brand yet.
- Why it works: A teaching visual reads as authority and value, ADucational ads build brand and drive sales at once, and a newcomer is rated on how good the information is rather than on a brand he does not have.
- Bad → Good: In the author's shorthand, man in front of whiteboard = educational = value; the Section II ads 07.12–07.14 put the speaker at a whiteboard to sell agency offers (example — ACQ Advertising Handbook, Part II, Section II, Ad Framework #2: New vs. Old).
- When not to apply: Suits advanced or complex products better than simple ones; it works whether you are brand new or established, even better once established.
- Anchor: «The “teacher” visual signals authority and works best for complex, novel, or higher ticket offers.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II, Ad Framework #1 Looking For 5 Avatars...

#### 07.29. Specificity is the proof of expertise
- **Rule:** In pain- and problem-based ads, describe the prospect's problem at the level of numbers, thresholds and moments that only an insider to that business would know, never in general terms, and make every pain one the intended avatar lives with.
- Why it works: The prospect judges expertise by how precisely and relevantly the problem is described: specific enough, and he concludes the advertiser must be an insider or is describing his life right now; pain always works, but it has to be his pain.
- Bad → Good: The problem named broadly → for an advanced gym owner, long hours, money burning and strain at home, details only that avatar would recognise (example — ACQ Advertising Handbook, Part II, Section I, Ad Framework #5 Long Term Result).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «You demonstrate your expertise by how specific or relevant you can describe the problem.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II, Ad Framework #3: Why You Will Never

#### 07.30. Name the mechanism, don't explain it
- **Rule:** When an ad introduces a new solution the prospect must opt in to learn about, give it a non-obvious name and at most one sentence on what it is and how it inverts the problems just listed, leaving the explanation to the lead magnet or next step.
- Why it works: An obvious name kills the curiosity that makes the prospect take the next step, and in a short ad the lead magnet does the explaining.
- Bad → Good: Pay Per Show → reverse royalty; the author renamed his model because the plain name gave it away and drained the curiosity.
- When not to apply: Meant for ads whose job is the click, with the explaining done later in the funnel.
- Anchor: «Name it something non-obvious. Explain what it is in one sentence and how it inverts all their problems.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section II, Ad Framework #3: Why You Will Never

#### 07.31. CTA congruent with the hook
- **Rule:** Make the CTA repeat the hook, the speaker or customer saying the hook again and linking the promised outcome to the next step, so the reason they kept watching becomes the reason they act.
- Why it works: If the hook made them stay to watch, the same hook will make them take the next step.
- Bad → Good: A Long Term Result testimonial ends with the customer repeating the opening claim and linking the outcome to the invitation (example — ACQ Advertising Handbook, Part II, Section I, Ad Framework #5 Long Term Result).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Make your CTA the same as your hook.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III, Ad Framework #3: Bribe

#### 07.32. The reason why
- **Rule:** Give every bonus, limited intake or big promotion a stated motive that is believable and as big as the offer, and say outright that the promotion is a big deal.
- Why it works: A motive makes a bonus read as authentic and so raises urgency; prospects believe a big, crazy move when it comes with a big, crazy reason, and putting your money where your mouth is dissolves skepticism and adds credibility.
- Bad → Good: An insane bonus with no motive → it's my birthday, so I'm giving an insane bonus to the first 100 clients.
- When not to apply: Whatever reason you choose has to be believable.
- Anchor: «A motive behind a bonus makes the bonus feel more authentic, and by extension increases urgency.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III Ad Framework #3 Bribe

#### 07.33. Movement, and real movement first
- **Rule:** Put movement in every ad, prefer real physical movement to motion added on a computer, and where several people can be shown, show many moving at once, in unison if it can be arranged.
- Why it works: Moving things generally beat still ones and real motion often beats digital motion; ads where the author moves almost always outperform, and lots of people doing the same thing at the same time stops the scroll far better than narration, a tip the author says people skip.
- Bad → Good: Motion added only in editing → a real object caught on camera (07.22), the talent walking fast around a corner at the lens (07.25), a packed class moving together (07.10).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Stuff that moves generally beats stuff that doesn’t. And real stuff that moves often beats computer stuff that moves.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section IV, Ad Framework #1: Flying Prop

#### 07.34. Big outcomes and average outcomes together
- **Rule:** Put both the big outcomes and the average, everyday outcomes in every ad; never stack only the outsized results.
- Why it works: People want to know the big thing is possible, but most of all they want to understand what is likely.
- Bad → Good: Only the top results → the top results next to the everyday and lower-tier ones, as in the Trending Format and Underdog ads (07.17, 07.20).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «I always try to include both big and average outcomes in my ads.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III, Ad Framework #1: Trending Format

#### 07.35. State the cash value of the free stuff
- **Rule:** When the ad gives something away, state its cash value only if the figure is realistic, and anchor it against what comparable products really sell for, escalating the numbers, only where the comparison is true.
- Why it works: A dollar figure makes a vague lead magnet a tangible benefit, and a true market comparison proves the free offer does not suck, so free reads as generosity rather than a gimmick.
- Bad → Good: Some free stuff → a thousand dollars of free stuff, then what comparable products sell for, escalating, as in the Bribe ad (07.19).
- When not to apply: An unrealistic figure or an untrue comparison defeats it: the market anchor persuades because it is true.
- Anchor: «Stating cash value (if realistic!) turns a vague lead magnet into a tangible benefit.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Section III, Ad Framework #3: Bribe

## The checklist

#### 07.36. Best Ad Framework Checklist
- **Rule:** Check every ad against the four parts and their must-haves (visual elements: paired verbal and visual hooks, movement, similar avatar, show what you tell, captions, platform native; verbal delivery: simple words, enunciation; ad script: call out prospects, a compelling reason why, comparisons, as many proof points as possible; CTA: one clear verbal and visual next step), then add the nice-to-haves that fit (aspirational setting or trending format, real-world demonstrations and settings, props; details and numbers, continuous open-looped curiosity, benefits and pains through the prospect's real life; damaging admissions, stakes, struggles and investment; reasons to act: CTA congruent with the hook, scarcity and urgency, bonus or incentives).
- Why it works: The four parts map onto what the viewer sees, what is said, what the viewer gets and how to get it, and the list is the author's real-world checklist for his own ads.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The must-haves are the author's own: ads can be made without some of them, he just does not make them that way, so treat a missing must-have as a flag, not an automatic fail.
- Anchor: «I divide the checklist into four parts: visual, verbal delivery, script, and CTA. They correspond roughly to: what they see, what you say, what they get, how to get it.» — ACQ Advertising Handbook (2025), Part III Best Ad Framework Checklist
