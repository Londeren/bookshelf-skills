## The Money Model

#### 10.1. Offer at the moment of realization
- **Rule:** Place each offer of the sequence at the moment the customer realizes they have the problem it solves: build the next offer from the problem the previous purchase creates, and never make it before the customer has experienced that problem.
- Why it works: A Money Model finds every opportunity to solve a customer's problem and then offers to solve it; once one problem is solved another appears, so timed right the number of offers is unlimited, while an offer made before the problem shows up has nothing to solve.
- Bad → Good: A $19/day rental car leaves the counter at $100/day (5x) because the agent tells him about each problem and then offers its fix: a roomier pick-up truck ("big man in a small car"), a late return (no late fees), premium insurance (worry about dinging the car) with a minimum-insurance downsell right after the no, and prepaid gas at $3.75 a gallon against $3.50 locally (the risk of missing the flight); a storage owner's free first month averages $127 the same way, through a $47 heavy-duty lock, boxes, a mover's affiliate kickback, an insurance upgrade and one size up on the unit (storage example — $100M Money Models, Start Here).
- When not to apply: Never talk anyone into buying against their will: hard selling is for weak products, so make the offer available when the problem appears and, if they don't want it, find somebody who does, since it is a numbers game.
- Anchor: «order. If you offer the right thing when customers realize they need it,» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section I: What's A Money Model?

#### 10.2. An Attraction Offer both generates and converts
- **Rule:** Judge a front-end offer by whether it turns strangers into customers in one move, generating the lead and converting it by offering something free or at a discount; an offer that only collects leads is not yet an Attraction Offer.
- Why it works: Attraction Offers turn advertising into money by offering something free or at a discount, and often also make money by offering a better deal at a higher price.
- Bad → Good: One gym's figures from the story, not a benchmark: $3,000 down on a lease, ads for a Free 6 Week Challenge bringing about 20 leads a day at $5 a lead, about half showing up and half of those buying a $600 program, plus about $80 of supplement profit, so about $680 per customer before the doors open and one advertising dollar back as thirty-four within forty-eight hours (example — $100M Money Models, Start Here).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.

## Customer financed acquisition

#### 10.3. The Three Levers of CFA
- **Rule:** To make customers finance their own acquisition, work all three levers: lower the cost of acquiring customers (↓CAC), increase lifetime gross profit (↑LTGP) and decrease the payback period (↓PPD); the thresholds for 30-day gross profit against CAC are in sheet 01.
- Why it works: A business grows by getting more customers or making them worth more, and the author adds speed as the third variable: a customer who pays for themselves today lets you get another one tomorrow instead of in thirty days.
- Bad → Good: Normal Lemonade Business, $20 CAC, a two-month payback and $40 of LTGP: −$20 on day 0 and the first money made only on day 90, while rent, utilities, software and the owners still need paying → Wonderful Lemonade Business, same prices and margins but $1 CAC and a seven-day payback: on day 7 a customer's $8 of gross profit covers CAC with $7 left, which goes to marketing on day 8 and brings seven more customers, and the whole run takes $1 out of pocket for marketing (example — the Lost Chapters, Payback Period = PPD).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «customers, make them worth more, and do it fast And those three elements create the three» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), The Three Levers of CFA

#### 10.4. LTGP on the back of a napkin
- **Rule:** When the CRM cannot give reliable lifetime transactions, estimate LTGP in steps: gross profit per customer first, then multiply it by average transactions for a product business, or divide it by churn for a recurring one.
- Why it works: A CRM often doesn't report lifetime transactions and, when it does, the data is frequently wrong, so the author works from gross profit and churn or transactions on the back of a napkin.
- Bad → Good: A service business with one account rep per 10 clients paying $3,000 a month, reps costing $6,000 a month: $30,000 of monthly revenue per rep minus $6,000 leaves $24,000 of gross profit, an 80% margin, so $2,400 of gross profit per customer; divided by 5% churn that gives an LTGP of $48,000, while a physical product multiplies instead, as in gross profit of $80 times four average transactions.
- When not to apply: Lifetime transactions are always an estimate: customers keep buying, and the figure rises as the business gets older.

## The back end: value grid and offer stacking

#### 10.5. Back End: The Value Grid
- **Rule:** Once you have metrics, lay the offers out as a grid rather than a stair step (prospects coming in, each offer with the percentage who take it, revenue per client on the right, totals and averages at the bottom), read lifetime value as all the revenue in the grid divided by the people who came in, read 30D Cash off it, and set what you can pay per lead only after the labour cost of working and selling those leads.
- Why it works: A stair step makes your brain think customers must buy the first offer to buy the second, yet many buy offer #1 and skip to #4; the grid puts the numbers in front of you, and revenue per prospect alone overstates what a lead is worth when working the leads eats that revenue.
- Bad → Good: Ten prospects, eight on a simple trial, three converting to a paid back-end offer, $600 in total: $75 collected per trial in the first 30 days, and $12 a lead to break even on 50 leads bought with that $600 → but working and selling those leads realistically costs $600 to $1,000 of labour, and at $600 of labour the business can now pay $0 a lead.
- When not to apply: The stair step is still a great place to start getting ideas down; the grid earns its place once you have metrics.
- Anchor: «point…not all customers follow it So, I think of it more as a grid For two primary reasons:» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Back End: The Value Grid

#### 10.6. The back end informs the front end
- **Rule:** When acquisition looks too expensive or a business cannot scale, raise what each client is worth by stacking Grand Slam Offers one after another (the back end) instead of concluding that customers cost too much to acquire: what you make per customer sets what you can spend to get one.
- Why it works: The more money you make per customer, the more you can spend to acquire customers; LTGP is the arms race of business, and however good your product, a competitor who can outspend you takes your product and your potential customers; acquisition is never too expensive or too cheap, it is simply what it is, and the job is to design the business to make more from the same customers.
- Bad → Good: A client on pace for about $3M a year, selling twice as many people a month yet making 56x less profit, had fine marketing and sales but one offer that wasn't irresistible, no upsell and no continuity, so a customer cost him about 50% more to acquire → the same type of business with a high-ticket front end, downsells and a series of upsells over the first 30 days takes 30D Cash from $75 to $1,763, so a competitor paying $100 a lead faces one that could pay $1,500 a lead and stay just as profitable (example — the Lost Chapters, Back End: The Value Grid).
- When not to apply: The author reached this diagnosis after reviewing the client's numbers and finding marketing and sales fine; check them before calling it a back-end problem.
- Anchor: «higher lifetime value, aka “the back end” So this is making a series of Grand Slam Offers in» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), SECTION C: ADVANCED OFFER STACKING

#### 10.7. Ultimate Offer Stacking Process
- **Rule:** After listing every need you can monetize, choreograph the sale so it weaves one of each core offer type in this order: Attract, Up Front Cash, Upsell/Downsell, Continuity, then Continuity #2 and Up Front Cash #2; build it one conversion opportunity at a time, starting with whichever brings in the most money for the least cost.
- Why it works: The up-front model acquires customers profitably, upsells and downsells get the whales to buy big and the minnows into your world, and continuity creates consistent cash flow; each pass raises the customer's LTV and so what you can spend to acquire them, and a flow that is overwhelming to build at once is not when built one step at a time.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Which up-front model, upsell and continuity to use depends on the business and its typical customer-buying journey, and anything that adds complexity had better be worth it.
- Anchor: «Next, we decide how we are going to choreograph the sales process I use this framework» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Advanced Offer Stacking: How To

#### 10.8. The four-sale offer flow
- **Rule:** When selling one-on-one over the customer's first weeks, run the stack as four sales (Sale #1 the service sale, Up Front Cash; Sale #2 the physical products sale, Upsell; Sale #3 the more-services sale, Continuity, positioned as a "Feedback" meeting; Sale #4 the prepay or last-chance sale), put a downsell ladder inside each, and move every prospect on to the following stage even after a no.
- Why it works: The big offer got them in the door but not everyone says yes, so another Grand Slam Offer in your back pocket dramatically increases the closing percentage; moving a prospect on after a no gives another chance to add value and monetize them, and in the author's flow people who said no to services still generated revenue at a complementary review.
- Bad → Good: Sale #1 goes high-ticket first, then half down on a different payment plan, a quarter down, a shorter program with no payment plan, a free trial with the card on file, and after a final no a complimentary nutrition orientation 24 to 72 hours later where the next offers begin; at the Sale #3 feedback meeting a client who feels under-supported is sold the higher-support VIP program, starting free today with the first program's cost credited, because every problem is an upsell opportunity; and where the operator down the street cuts costs and fears making more offers, the author's product sales at orientation paid for one-on-one onboarding and, most times, the entire cost of acquisition.
- When not to apply: The ladders need a one-on-one setting in person or over the phone, and a sale made off a web page doesn't allow them; a no after the whole ladder signals a trust problem, a sales matter the author marks as beyond the scope of the book.
- Anchor: «In the example above, we are selling services, then products Then we are selling them» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Advanced Offer Stacking: How To, Sample Weight Loss Offer Flow

#### 10.9. Monetize what you refer out
- **Rule:** Monetize the complementary products and services you already recommend through affiliate relationships, and when a stream you refer out makes more money than your own, consider buying or incorporating it instead of referring it.
- Why it works: Most businesses refer out lots of revenue; affiliate commissions are pure profit that goes straight to the bottom line, and a bigger stream is worth owning.
- Bad → Good: A small business owner taking home $35,280 a year off $282,240 of revenue adds $2,000 a month of retail sales commissions, which looks small next to the $23,520 the business makes selling services, yet moves take-home income from $35,280 to $59,280; the author reports more than $3,000,000 in affiliate commissions made directly.
- When not to apply: The author hedges the purchase as worth it only sometimes; the affiliate move is for revenue that adds little or no cost in time, money or complexity.

## Attraction offers

### Win Your Money Back

#### 10.10. Win Your Money Back for things people start and quit
- **Rule:** Use Win Your Money Back (you set the customer's goal and how to reach it, and if they reach it they get their money back as cash or store credit) for things people start and quit and that need continuous effort, such as starting businesses, learning skills, losing weight, fitness, beauty regimes, self-care, time or mental-health management, and plan to make the money from those who succeed, not from those who fail.
- Why it works: It keeps motivation through the early pains of learning, and the real money comes from people who succeed and to whom you have something else to offer, so the more results you deliver, the more money you make.
- Bad → Good: Expecting the profit to come from the customers who fail → Danny's client, a prospect who refused to buy, proposed paying $500 for eight weeks of training with the money back if he hit his goal, in exchange for the gym using his results in marketing; he hit it, spent the winnings on more training, and his before-and-after pictures brought thirteen referrals.
- When not to apply: Not for one-shot purchases; and don't confuse it with Trial With Penalty, where customers pay only if they miss the terms, while here they get their money back if they meet them.
- Anchor: «A Win Your Money Back Offer works like this. *You* set a goal for the» — $100M Money Models (2025), Win Your Money Back, Description

#### 10.11. Good Win Your Money Back criteria
- **Rule:** Set the terms on results, on actions, or on both, and make every criterion 1) easy to track, with the customer trained on exactly what to do, 2) likely to get the result, realistic, what the best customers already do, and 3) a way to advertise the business, such as posting, tagging, referring or leaving a review.
- Why it works: On results customers bet on their ability to reach the goal, on actions on their ability to follow directions, and on both you set a good goal and show the way, so people with too few skills still get a fighting chance; people mess up what they aren't trained on, realistic criteria are what produce results, and qualifiers who post and refer do free advertising that brings in friends and family.
- Bad → Good: A stretch target the customer can't measure → criteria people already track, as phones track steps and cameras date photos; criteria that seem too easy are probably close to realistic.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «or break this offer. Good criteria have three characteristics:» — $100M Money Models (2025), Win Your Money Back, Important Notes

#### 10.12. Refund rate below 5%
- **Rule:** Run Win Your Money Back only if your current refund rate is under 5% and you can stomach about 10% of your customers asking for their money back, and then give the money back to anyone who asks, whether or not they're entitled.
- Why it works: Above 5% you risk too many refunds and the product needs fixing first; data from thousands of gyms puts requests at about 10% of customers (2025), which the extra customers and the follow-up offer more than outweigh when the offer is advertised well; and if someone doesn't want you to have their money, you want it less than they do, so the effort belongs on the next customer.
- Bad → Good: Refusing or arguing over a refund → give it back, fix the mistake if you made one, and spend the time and resources on getting better customers next time (example — $100M Money Models, The Four Types of Offers That Make Money Models).
- When not to apply: The 5% is a precondition on the business, not on the offer: check the existing refund rate before the offer runs at all.
- Anchor: «● Only use a Win Your Money Back Offer if your refund rate is below 5%.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Win Your Money Back, Summary Points

#### 10.13. How You Apply Store Credit
- **Rule:** Apply won store credit toward a purchase priced above the winnings and spread over a longer period, never as free time up front; and if you pay in store credit while still advertising the offer as free, add a satisfaction guarantee that is unconditional.
- Why it works: People fall off if they don't pay something, so a discount over the long haul keeps them engaged over the long haul; in testing, store credit and cash back pulled the same number of customers, and adding the unconditional guarantee never materially changed how many asked for their money back.
- Bad → Good: A winner's $600 of credit handed over as three free months, after which the author's gym members churned before their first out-of-pocket payment, in effect "buy six weeks get three months free" → the $600 put toward something that costs more, over a longer term.
- When not to apply: Customers can use the credit however they want, this is only what you present first; and check the unconditional guarantee with legal counsel in your area.
- Anchor: «Just offer to apply it to something that costs more than their winnings.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Win Your Money Back, Important Notes

#### 10.14. All Meetings And Calls Provide Opportunities To Make More Offers
- **Rule:** Write check-in meetings into the money-back criteria, require attendance at all of them to win, and make an offer at each.
- Why it works: Beyond helping the customer succeed, the meetings are the best opportunities to make upsell offers based on the customer's feedback.
- Bad → Good: A gym's three required appointments: a Nutrition Orientation with before pictures and a supplement offer, a Progress Check-in with the membership offer, and a Transformation Feedback session with after pictures and the membership offer again, plus a prepay-for-a-year discount for anyone who already bought.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Make check-in meetings part of your money-back criteria whenever you» — $100M Money Models (2025), Win Your Money Back, Important Notes

#### 10.15. Make Everyone A Winner
- **Rule:** Sell the program as if the money comes back only on meeting the criteria, but about halfway through make the next offer as if the customer has already won; at the end, credit the entire deposit of anyone who turned down the first upsell and then failed the challenge toward staying long term.
- Why it works: It lowers the customer's anxiety about failing, keeps them longer and makes them like you more, and the next offer arrives as a surprise rather than a verdict; we don't get customers to make a sale, we make sales to get customers.
- Bad → Good: Halfway through, the seller asks for the customer's long-term goal, agrees the program isn't the point, and offers to credit this program toward the next one whether or not the short-term goal is hit; for someone who failed, the seller acts as if they won, since the goal was finishing what they started, and credits the deposit toward staying.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.

### Giveaways

#### 10.16. The six steps of a Giveaway Offer
- **Rule:** Run a giveaway (scholarship, sweepstake, raffle) in six steps: pick a Grand Prize, pick the promotional offer, ask for contact information and eligibility, pick the qualifying actions, put it on a deadline, then announce the winner and contact everyone else with the promotional offer; for more referrals, give two Grand Prizes and tell entrants that if someone they refer wins, they win one too.
- Why it works: Everyone who entered showed interest in what you sell, so after one person wins the rest qualify for the promotional offer, priced against the Grand Prize's assigned value; two prizes give endless entries through referrals and make referrers invested in the success of the people they bring, which keeps lead quality high.
- Bad → Good: A fitness certification business advertises a full-ride scholarship to its whole program, collects contact details and answers to questions like why should we pick you, announces one full-ride winner publicly, then calls everyone else privately to tell them they got a partial scholarship; most join on the call, never having heard the list price and knowing only the value of the full ride.
- When not to apply: Keep eligibility (is the entrant a fit: do you own a vet clinic, why should you be selected?) apart from qualifying actions (things they do: attend a call or event, make a post, enter a group); and consult legal counsel on the structure, since somebody actually has to win and the rules must make the qualifications clear.
- Anchor: «Giveaway Offers advertise a chance to win a big prize in exchange for» — $100M Money Models (2025), Giveaways, Description

#### 10.17. Pick A Grand Prize
- **Rule:** Choose as Grand Prize the very thing you want everyone to buy, give it a stated monetary value as the price anchor, and make it grand for this audience; when few enter, offer a better prize rather than rework the advertising.
- Why it works: Leads enter because the Grand Prize interests them, so the bigger its assigned value, the more compelling the discounted offer to everyone else; a giveaway explains itself, so weak interest means the prize wasn't grand.
- Bad → Good: A portfolio company gave away tickets to its own event and nobody bit → re-run with a $50,000 bundle of equipment plus its core product for a year, it crushed; working pairs: a dentist's free invisible braces at $6,000 retail against a $2,000 gift card for braces, a free year of organic dog food at $1,000 retail against a $300 gift card usable only with a one-year subscription.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**Pick A Grand Prize.** Make your Grand Prize *the thing you want» — $100M Money Models (2025), Giveaways, Description

#### 10.18. Explain The Cost-To-Value Using Their Discount
- **Rule:** Offer every non-winner the core offer sweetened with a bonus, a discount, or a small variation on the Grand Prize that ethically justifies the lower price, set the discount at 10%–30% of gross margins (2025), and present it as the gap between the Grand Prize's value and what they pay; in a recurring business, spread the discount over as long a term as they'll accept, then bill at normal rates.
- Why it works: The Grand Prize serves as the price anchor, so the bigger the discount against it the more compelling the offer, and measured against the thing's value a small discount off retail reads as a large one.
- Bad → Good: A Grand Prize advertised at $5,000 of value and $2,000 retail is offered to everyone else at $1,800, 10% off retail; presented as $5,000 of value for $1,800, it becomes a 64% cost-to-value difference.
- When not to apply: Keep it distinct from the Grand Prize: one winner gets the Grand Prize publicly, while everyone else qualifies for this offer, which the author calls a participation trophy.
- Anchor: «thumb: make your core offer discount equal to 10%--30% of your gross» — $100M Money Models (2025), Giveaways, Description

#### 10.19. Cap entries at what you can call
- **Rule:** Set a deadline at each of three points (entering, claiming, using): advertise the giveaway for three to seven days, stopping at seven days or once entries pass the number you can call within seven days, whichever comes first, with a daily update to entrants on every platform; then give non-winners seven days to claim, and tell winners how long they have to use the prize.
- Why it works: Deadlines make people act, and an expiration date on claiming makes them more likely to claim; the daily countdown and social proof keep the hype alive, and entries beyond what you can reach in seven days are wasted.
- Bad → Good: Letting in more entrants than you have the time and resources to reach within a week → a seven-day advertising window that closes early once leads reach that number, with calls scheduled the same or next day and a prize with hours to days of usable life.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «● Advertise your Giveaway for seven days, or until the number of leads» — $100M Money Models (2025), Giveaways, Summary Points

### Decoy Offer

#### 10.20. The two steps of a Decoy Offer
- **Rule:** Advertise a free or discounted lesser, smaller or simpler version of your premium offer, built by cutting components, using older models, dropping personalization and removing every guarantee, and when leads engage, present it side by side with the premium offer while emphasizing the premium.
- Why it works: Side by side, leads see how much more valuable the premium is, and either way you close everyone, which makes new customers cheap and profitable; the Attraction Offer only has to get leads to engage, and the premium's value comes from the size of its difference with the decoy.
- Bad → Good: A gym advertises a Free or $21 21-Day Transformation: the decoy is daily workouts in a Skool.com group (2025), a general nutrition plan and recordings, with no support and no guarantee; the premium is unlimited workouts, a personalised nutrition plan, 1-1 accountability and results guaranteed or another 21 days free; elsewhere, a free once-a-week version beside a $399 Ultimate version with unlimited sessions, 1-1 coaching and a results-or-repeat-free guarantee drew about eight in ten to the $399 option.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Decoy Offers advertise something free or discounted. Then, when leads» — $100M Money Models (2025), Decoy Offer, Description

#### 10.21. Get Them To Give You Permission To Sell Them
- **Rule:** Lead with the premium offer and keep the decoy in your back pocket; when the lead asks for the decoy, present it, contrast it at once with the premium, and only after both are on the table ask which will get them to their goal faster.
- Why it works: In a perfect world they take the premium right away; when they don't, they have to name the premium themselves, and you move forward in the sale mutually agreeing it is the best thing for them.
- Bad → Good: A lead asks about the advertised free thing → ask whether they came for free stuff or for lasting results; on results, skip straight to the premium, and on free stuff, present the decoy, contrast it with the premium, then ask which they think gets them to their goal faster.
- When not to apply: Where a legal obligation requires showing the advertised decoy, the premium-first order gives way: present the decoy, and the immediate contrast has to do the work.
- Anchor: «contrast it with your premium offer. Then only after presenting both,» — $100M Money Models (2025), Decoy Offer, Important Notes

#### 10.22. Discount Offers Have Higher Show-Up Rates Than Free Offers
- **Rule:** When appointment show-up rates are low, run a Discount Attraction Offer instead of a free one, especially where a no-show is expensive, as for doctors, lawyers and dentists.
- Why it works: A free offer gets more leads; a discount gets fewer leads but a higher percentage who show up, so the trade is lead volume against show rate.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «appointments, try a Discount Offer instead. This is especially important» — $100M Money Models (2025), Decoy Offer, Important Notes

### Buy X Get Y Free

#### 10.23. Raise Prices Before Giving Stuff Away To Preserve Profits
- **Rule:** Before running Buy X Get Y Free, raise prices permanently and for real to cover what you give away, never as a pretend rise.
- Why it works: The offer will work, and since it will work you need to make money on it; every new customer comes in on this price, so the change makes sense for at least a season, and a discount becomes a free offer only because you sell several things at once, so the discount's value covers the price of more stuff.
- Bad → Good: The Boot Factory: one pair of boots at $200 → $600 for one pair with two more pairs free, so the tourist customer who buys once ever still buys three $200 pairs for $600 while the store runs a free offer instead of a fair-price one; likewise three t-shirts at $10 each become one $30 shirt with two free.
- When not to apply: With a single product to sell, giving it away leaves you hungry: the offer is for stuff that makes sense to buy more of or get longer access to.
- Anchor: «need to make money. So, *permanently* raise prices to accommodate the» — $100M Money Models (2025), Buy X Get Y Free, Important Notes

#### 10.24. Give more free than paid
- **Rule:** Structure Buy X Get Y Free so the customer gets more free items than paid ones (two paid and ten free beats ten paid and two free), and mix different free items with the paid ones when more cheaper free things make the offer more compelling.
- Why it works: It seems obvious, but people don't do it; the free things can differ from the paid ones as long as the value keeps the offer compelling, and people still see one thing bought and several things free.
- Bad → Good: Eighteen months of service at $1,800: Buy 12 Months Get 6 Months Free (good) → Buy 9 Get 9 Free (better) → Buy 6 Get 12 Free (best, the most free stuff); and for the cost of one free shirt, test one shirt plus one free shirt against one shirt plus three free pairs of socks.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «● Always try to give more free things than paid things.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Buy X Get Y Free, Summary Points

#### 10.25. Make This Offer To Existing Customers For Fast Cash
- **Rule:** When you make Buy X Get Y Free to existing recurring customers for a cash pop, cap it at 10% of the customer base (2025).
- Why it works: It gives a good cash pop while keeping recurring cash flow healthy; selling it to everyone would trade away the recurring revenue.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: It applies to a business that already has recurring revenue and needs cash fast.
- Anchor: «at their current price. Just limit the offer to 10% of your customers.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Buy X Get Y Free, Important Notes

### Pay Less Now or Pay More Later

#### 10.26. The structure of Pay Less Now or Pay More Later
- **Rule:** When you can promise a clear yes/no result inside a short time frame, offer pay later first (full price billed later, cancellable only by customers who met tracked conditions that are the actions getting the most value from the product, such as attendance, showing up to appointments, turning in data), then, only after they accept it, offer pay now at a 20–50% discount with bonuses (2025), and have something more, better or newer to sell them when the time is right.
- Why it works: Pay later removes all risk, lets you advertise free and puts their card on file; once they have agreed to pay later, a hefty discount and bonuses move many to pay now; and a customer who never tried it can't say you suck, so conditions aligned with value delivery make it win-win.
- Bad → Good: A speed reading training promises double your reading speed in 3 hours or it's free; pay later takes the card at $0 and bills $297 the next day unless they email to cancel because their speed didn't double, attendance required to qualify; pay now is $97 with the recordings, sold nowhere else, as a free bonus; an eight-week program was upsold at the end; a lawn service offers $0 for a lawn cut and hedge trim with $599 billed afterwards, against $369 now with a lawn treatment added, then a $199-a-month lawncare upsell.
- When not to apply: The author uses it as a downsell for physical products or one-time services; for recurring products or services he uses Trial With Penalty instead.
- Anchor: «● Pay Less Now Or Pay More Later Offers give people a choice to pay» — $100M Money Models (2025), Pay Less Now or Pay More Later, Description and Summary Points

#### 10.27. When pay-later cancellations pass 10%
- **Rule:** When more than 10% of pay-later customers cancel their payment (2025), treat it as a fault in the offer and change one of three things: a promise that is too big, guarantee conditions that are too low, or a price that is too high.
- Why it works: However well you deliver, some people will cancel; above 10% the cause is in the offer, not in the customers.
- Bad → Good: Where the promise is to decrease shoulder pain, the customer rates the pain from 1 to 10 before the treatment and again after; if it went down, the promise was kept and something else can be sold, and a promise that simple, clear and measurable avoids unnecessary cancellations.
- When not to apply: Some cancellation is normal: up to 10% it is part of the cost of doing business rather than a reason to rework the offer.
- Anchor: «**If More Than 10% Of 'Pay Later' People Cancel Their Payment**. You» — $100M Money Models (2025), Pay Less Now or Pay More Later, Important Notes

### Free presentations

#### 10.28. Free Education with Offer: pick the length, check the benchmark
- **Rule:** Pick the free education-with-offer format by price and audience temperature, longer as the thing gets more expensive and complicated and the audience colder, and hold it to its benchmark (2025): Free XYZ Dinner, a quarter of the diners to a lower offer that covers the cost of getting everyone there, and at least a third of those buyers, booked into individual appointments, to a much higher-ticket item; Free 90-minute Masterclass, 10% of the people still on the call at the offer; Free 5 Day Challenge, 2 to 5% of sign-ups; Free 3 Day virtual Summit, 10% of final-day attendees; Free Strategy Call, 25% of people who get to the second call.
- Why it works: The more expensive the offer, the more audiences want to learn about it before buying, and the colder they are, the more exposure it takes to build trust (the bigger the plane, the longer the runway), so the format forces as much exposure as it can into as short a window as reasonable.
- Bad → Good: A 100-person dinner audience should give about 25 buyers of the lower offer and about eight of the higher one; that is the benchmark telling you whether the format is working.
- When not to apply: The benchmarks are the author's as of 2025.
- Anchor: «Presentations can last anywhere from 15 seconds to 15 days The exact length of the» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Free Presentations, Free Education with Offer Examples

#### 10.29. Three core limiting beliefs
- **Rule:** Build every presentation-based offer around three core limiting beliefs (results must come easy, I must get the best results possible, results must lead to attention and approval, aka status), attacking one per session or day: a Free 5 Day Challenge gives four 60–90 minute live sessions Monday through Thursday, each breaking a belief that stops them buying and giving content they can apply today, then makes the offer on Friday; a Free 3 Day virtual Summit builds each day of 4+ presentations around one belief, pitches at the end of day two, repitches on day three, and ends with inspiration to take action.
- Why it works: Conversion rises with how well the messaging plays to the three beliefs, and more or longer presentations only go deeper into the same three; even those who don't buy get real value, so the worst case is goodwill with the marketplace.
- Bad → Good: The same challenge named after the prospect's outcome in each industry: Free Find Your Niche Entrepreneurship Challenge, Free 5 Day Plateau Buster for weight loss, Free 5 Day Pain Release Challenge, Free 5 Days to Get Unstuck Challenge for life coaching.
- When not to apply: For the summit the days of the week don't matter, a finding the author calls personally surprising: run it whenever suits you and your team.
- Anchor: «minutes (like the summit) all serve to play on the three core limiting beliefs. The» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Free Presentations, Pro Tips

### Freemium

#### 10.30. No freemium without capital
- **Rule:** Don't use Freemium unless you have investors and a lot of capital, a software or media business with incremental margins near 100%, and something so valuable that lots of people come to you without marketing; and treat it as an acquisition strategy, never as the business model.
- Why it works: Almost every company in the author's freemium examples has funding; the free thing has to spread by word of mouth at $0 of ad spend, or the model just loses money servicing free customers, and the author has seen smart people do it wrong more often than right.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Not for a first rodeo either: only a seasoned pro who knows the numbers like their children's names should try it; the author calls it one of the most dangerous acquisition strategies and cut the chapter because it applied to too few businesses.
- Anchor: «do not have investors and large amounts of capital, I would not recommend this structure» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Freemium

#### 10.31. The three requirements of a freemium give-away
- **Rule:** Give away only something that costs you next to nothing to fulfill, provides continuous rather than one-time value, and leaves enough ungiven that people still want to buy something else; all three must hold at once, and the offer must be entirely free, since Freemium has no discount version.
- Why it works: The free thing must be good enough that people come by word of mouth and keep using it, yet leave a paid thing worth upgrading to, a very difficult balance; software fits because each extra user is virtually free and it is meant to give continuous value, leaving only how much and exactly what to give away.
- Bad → Good: Your core product goes out free, users are satisfied with it and never want the next thing, and you run a business that loses money servicing customers for free → in B2B, give away something that exposes a hole in their business that your next paid product solves.
- When not to apply: Without investors and large capital the author would not recommend it at all (see 10.30).
- Anchor: «challenging You must give something away that 1) costs you almost nothing to fulfill, 2)» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Freemium

#### 10.32. The true cost of acquisition under freemium
- **Rule:** Before running freemium, compute the true cost of acquisition as the monthly cost of servicing one free customer divided by the percentage of free customers who upgrade, and go ahead only if average revenue per paid user clears it by enough to be profitable and grow.
- Why it works: You still carry the costs of doing business before you profit, so the upgrade percentage and the servicing cost decide whether the free pool pays; conversion too low, a free thing not worth telling people about, or fulfilment cost too high against paid revenue each break it.
- Bad → Good: At a servicing cost of $0.05 a month per free customer and a 1% upgrade rate, CAC is $5 a month, so average revenue per paid user of $15 a month or more leaves a profitable business that can grow (2025).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Your true cost of acquisition when using this model is understanding what percentage» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Freemium, Details

### Free Pick Your Price

#### 10.33. Attraction Offer: Free Pick Your Price
- **Rule:** In a low-trust environment, and only where the free thing has low operational costs, market the offer as free, pre-frame at the start of the sale that there is a pick-your-price setup with bonuses at different levels and no obligation to pay anything, ask hard commitment questions, then at checkout outline the bonuses at three payment levels (small, medium, large), ask which payment method they prefer, and stay silent; anyone who won't pay still gets the basic level free.
- Why it works: People feel in control of their own destiny and it pulls on their generosity, which builds goodwill; the pre-frame avoids awkwardness at the end, the hard questions work as a mini-interview for commitment, the rungs move people off $0 on a scale with no maximum, and the more they pay, the more attention they pay to the results.
- Bad → Good: A roadside Free Car Wash: at the window the attendant points at the pricing chart, says the basic wash costs nothing but the staff are taking donations to help feed their families, names cash, credit and Venmo, then says nothing; the author paid $20, more than the top automated wash costs in normal conditions, and his gyms using the scripting during COVID got an average ticket of about $99 even from people who couldn't normally close; a free 21-day weight loss adds a 1-1 call at $99, the supplement handbook over $199 and a guarantee of 10lbs lost or credit at $499.
- When not to apply: Don't run it unless you can reliably sell the upsell that turns it profitable, the reason the author cut the chapter; conversion is very high but the average ticket typically lower, and those who pay nothing can and should still be upsold other products and services.
- Anchor: «You market the promotional offer as free When the person gets to the checkout, you» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Free Pick Your Price

#### 10.34. Pick Your Price is free only
- **Rule:** Run Free Pick Your Price as a free offer, never inside a discount wrapper.
- Why it works: The play rests on the offer being free and the payment being the prospect's own choice.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The author's own PAID VERSION pro tip sets a floor above zero instead: an art gallery gave every piece a price range and let buyers choose how much to support the artist, a painting anywhere from $149 to $299, and the owner reported most people pay more than the halfway point so as not to seem cheap or unsupportive; the author calls it half goodwill, half capitalism.
- Anchor: «This offer does not work with a discount wrapper» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attraction Offer: Free Pick Your Price, Free Versus Discount Note

## Making your money model

#### 10.35. Make Your Own Money Model: the four steps
- **Rule:** Build a money model from scratch in four steps, each with its own goal: Step 1, start with an Attraction Offer, to turn strangers into customers and cover the cost of getting them; Step 2, pick an Upsell Offer, sized so that 30-day profit lands well above the cost of getting a new customer plus the cost of delivering to them; Step 3, pick a Downsell Offer, to get the customers who said no to the last offer to say yes to another; Step 4, pick a Continuity Offer, to take one last sale inside the 30-day window and stack recurring cash.
- Why it works: A Money Model is a deliberate sequence of offers — what you offer, when you offer it and how — and each step has a defined goal, so the first offer does not have to make the profit: every opportunity to solve the customer's problem is found and then offered.
- Bad → Good: A single offer and nothing after it → a local gym's model with all four types in sequence: Stage I, Win Your Money Back, a pay-to-enter fitness challenge with the money back on hitting goals, with a Payment Plan Downsell running split pay, then three-pay, then a free Trial With Penalty; Stage II, a Menu Upsell of supplement bundles personalised to the goal, with a Feature Downsell from big bundle to small bundle to monthly subscription; Stage III, a Rollover Upsell plus a lifetime discount of $50 off per month for a 12-month commitment (example — $100M Money Models, Section VI Example Money Models).
- When not to apply: Do not try to implement a full Money Model at once, it will break the business: perfect one offer at a time, and extend a stage only once it works reliably, financially and operationally, and pays for the stage after it (12.4). Sometimes the best timing for a Continuity Offer comes after the first thirty days, and that is fine.
- Anchor: «**Step 1) Start With An Attraction Offer**. The goal is to turn» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section VI, Make Your Own Money Model

#### 10.36. Raise Price In Stages
- **Rule:** Start a new offer cheap and raise the price as the yeses come in; keep raising it until the extra cash from the yeses no longer makes up for the nos. The Lifetime Value playbook's cadence for the same move: nudge the price up about 20% every ten sales until sales drop dramatically, then move back to the sweet spot (2025).
- Why it works: Lots of early yeses generate the customer feedback that improves the product, and sales are what confirm that people want the thing and run water through the pipes to find the leaks; testing price is expensive for a few months, being under-monetized for life is more expensive.
- Bad → Good: Launching an unproven offer at its target price → a low price first, nudged up about 20% every ten sales while things are humming (example — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value, #1 Increase Prices, Pro Tip: Start Low Then Go Up).
- When not to apply: The cadence is for a new or unproven offer; if you find you sold someone at too high a price, add bonuses or refund the difference.
- Anchor: «raising the price. And keep raising the price until you cannot make up» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section VI: Make Your Money Model, Important Notes

#### 10.37. Simple Scales. Fancy Fails.
- **Rule:** Make new offers out of new ways to sell the existing product, not out of new products or new businesses.
- Why it works: It is less about having 100 products to offer and more about having 100 ways to offer your product; one personal training product becomes many offers by varying the sessions per week.
- Bad → Good: Starting more businesses or building more products in order to have more offers → one personal training service sold as one, two, three or four sessions per week, and affiliate products filling the gaps of the money model without the operational headache.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «about having 100 ways to offer your product. Think more ways to sell the» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section VI: Make Your Money Model, Important Notes
