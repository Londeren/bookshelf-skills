# Diagnose the business: where it is broken and what to fix first

Open this sheet first in advisor mode: the user describes a situation — few leads, ads that do not pay back, no cash for advertising, flat growth, a team that blames the wrong department — and asks what to do. It holds the method's order of diagnosis and its thresholds; the fixes live in sheets 02–15 and are named at each rule. Work top down: a failure at a higher level makes work on the levels below it pointless. The advisor's answer is a diagnosis plus at most three steps, each pointing at a rule.

## The order: market, offer, persuasion

#### 01.1. Diagnose in the order market > offer > persuasion
- **Rule:** When a business is not working, check the market first, the strength of the offer second and persuasion skills last — the author's order is Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills — and fix the highest level that fails before touching anything below it.
- Why it works: a great rating on a higher-order piece overpowers anything lower on the scale, a normal rating passes the buck to the next part, and a bad rating stops the equation unless a great higher-priority component nullifies it.
- Bad → Good: working the product, the offer, the sales and the team while the market itself is shrinking → a software business for newspapers diagnosed by elimination: the product was great, the offer a zero-risk revshare, the founder a natural salesman, so what was left was the market, shrinking 25 percent a year; the same founder in mask manufacturing was doing millions per month within five months (example — $100M Offers, ch. 4 Finding The Right Market).
- When not to apply: the whole order sits atop the assumption of at least a normal market (01.2); in a bad market nothing that follows works.
- Anchor: «Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Pricing: Finding The Right Market -- A Starving Crowd

#### 01.2. The method assumes a normal market
- **Rule:** Before applying any rule of this skill, confirm the market is at least normal: growing at the rate of the marketplace, with common unmet needs in health, wealth or relationships; with no market for the offer, nothing that follows works.
- Why it works: a Grand Slam Offer given to the wrong audience falls on deaf ears; the author's friend could have gone through the entire book and nothing would have worked, because he was targeting newspapers, a dying market.
- Bad → Good: a struggling owner keeps working the product, the offer, the sales and the team while the marketplace fights against him → the market is checked first, and a shrinking one is left rather than fixed.
- When not to apply: this is a boundary of the whole method: it presupposes a normal or better market and does not repair a bad or dying one.
- Anchor: «If you don’t have a market for your offer, nothing that follows will work. This entire book sits atop the assumption that you have at least a “normal” market» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Pricing: Finding The Right Market

#### 01.3. Commit to the niche: the niche slap
- **Rule:** One market is picked and kept long enough for trial and error; an offer that fails once in a market is not read as a bad market, and the business does not hop to the next niche.
- Why it works: most times it is not a bad market, they just have not found a Grand Slam Offer for it; all markets have unpleasant characteristics, and hopping means starting over from the beginning each time, so you fail far longer.
- Bad → Good: half-heartedly trying one offer in one market, not making a million dollars, concluding the market is bad and hopping to the next → picking one and sticking with it long enough to have trial and error; both abandoned markets are usually normal markets worth billions.
- When not to apply: the author's fix is absolute: pick one and commit long enough to have trial and error, since no one can serve two masters.
- Anchor: «If you keep hopping from niche to niche, hoping that the market will solve your problems, you deserve to be *niche slapped.*» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Pricing: Finding The Right Market

#### 01.4. Under $10M a year, niche down; above it, TAM decides
- **Rule:** A business under $10M per year narrows its audience rather than broadening it; above $10M, whether to broaden depends on how narrow the niche is and on the total addressable market (TAM).
- Why it works: a business can only grow to meet its TAM, so beyond that point it may have to go up market, down market or into an adjacent market; but many companies expanded past $30M per year serving a single niche, and an owner at $1M or $3M who thinks he has capped is wrong.
- Bad → Good: an owner at $1M or $3M a year broadening because the niche feels capped → niching down further, since companies passed $30M a year serving a single niche (2021 figures).
- When not to apply: the niching advice is bounded by revenue: it holds for the 99.6 percent of readers under $10M per year; above that, TAM decides.
- Anchor: «For most, if you are under $10M per year, niching down will make you more money. After that, it will depend on how narrow the niche is, or, what is called TAM (total addressable market).» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 4 Pricing: Finding The Right Market

#### 01.5. Nine percent a year is maintenance
- **Rule:** A business growing slower than 9 percent year over year is treated as falling behind, not as holding steady; in a growing marketplace the bar is 20–30 percent per year just to keep up.
- Why it works: the market is continuously growing and the stock market grows at 9 percent per year, so maintenance in the most generic sense is 9 percent growth year over year; every person, company and organism is either growing or dying, and maintenance is a myth.
- Bad → Good: aiming to maintain the current size → treating flat as falling behind and growth as the target (2021 figures).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «If we aren't growing at 9 percent per year, we are falling behind.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 3 Pricing: The Commodity Problem

## Leads: skill, volume, constraint

#### 01.6. A lead shortage is skill or volume, never a missing fifth method
- **Rule:** When there are not enough leads, diagnose the cause as not enough skill or not enough volume in the Core Four, and never as a missing fifth method.
- Why it works: the Core Four are the only four things you can do to let anyone know about anything, and you are not getting as many leads as you want because you are not advertising enough.
- Bad → Good: explaining a shortage of leads by anything other than skill and volume in the Core Four → more reps in the channel already chosen, done better (sheets 04–06), and only then something new (sheet 05).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «[So if you aren't getting as many leads as you want, you're not doing» — $100M Leads (2023), Section III: Get Leads

#### 01.7. Test at the constraint: the step where the most leads drop off
- **Rule:** Before improving anything in a lead flow, find the step where the most leads drop off — the author calls these steps constraints — and concentrate the testing there.
- Why it works: constraints are the points where the smallest improvements create the biggest boost in results: on a flow of 30% opt-in, 5% apply and 50% schedule, five extra points on the apply step double the leads (2x), while the same five points on the opt-in add 16% and on the schedule step 10%.
- Bad → Good: improving every step of the funnel by a little → five points on the 5% apply step, the biggest drop-off, for a 2x result.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «"drop-off" point. So ]{.calibre3}[I do the most testing at whatever step» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids, Better

#### 01.8. Four tries or one month, then the next constraint
- **Rule:** If the current best version of a step cannot be beaten in four tries, or in one month, the work moves to the next constraint.
- Why it works: beyond that point the effort put into making the step better brings lower and lower returns than the same effort spent elsewhere.
- Bad → Good: abandoning a constraint on a feeling that it is exhausted → abandoning it on a count of tries and a calendar limit.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «running in ]{.calibre3}[four tries (or one month)]{.calibre24}[, we move» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids, Better

#### 01.9. Place the business on the Roadmap: seven levels of advertisers
- **Rule:** Diagnose which advertising problem to work on next by the level the business is at, each with one primary action: (1) your friends know what you sell — warm outreach; (2) you consistently let everyone you know — as much warm outreach and content as you can, consistently; (3) employees help you advertise — hire people to advertise profitably on your behalf; (4) the product is good enough for consistent referrals — work on the product until 25% or more of customers come from referrals; (5) more places, more ways, more people — at least two methods on multiple platforms; (6) killers — battle-hardened executives and department heads take over new channels; (7) unwritten.
- Why it works: the levels let you identify where you are on the advertising totem pole so you know what to do to get to the next one; Acquisition.com uses this roadmap to scale portfolio companies from a few million a year to $100,000,000+ (2023).
- Bad → Good: hiring people to advertise (level 3) while your own advertising inputs are not yet maxed → people are hired only when your personal inputs are maxed out and the platform is not; the author hired a videographer and a media buyer to take the paid-ads work off his plate.
- When not to apply: the author's own limit: the roadmap looks clean and it never is, real business is messy; it orders what tends to happen and does not predict a particular business. Level 7 is not written.
- Anchor: «this chapter, I describe the phases you will go through as you scale» — $100M Leads (2023), The Roadmap - Putting it All Together

#### 01.10. Level 4: hold the product until 25% of customers come from referrals
- **Rule:** Before ramping advertising again with a bigger team, work on the product until at least 25% of customers come from referrals.
- Why it works: this is where most people mess up: they let their product slip and never recover; at the $100M machine level the product is so good that a third of customers bring more customers.
- Bad → Good: going back to scaling advertising with a bigger team while the product slides → the author doubled down on referrals after noticing his ads were off and referrals kept coming, updating the product from customer feedback every two weeks and running a strong referral programme (sheet 14).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «shoot for getting 25% or more of your customers from referrals. Now,» — $100M Leads (2023), The Roadmap

## Advertising economics: LTGP, CAC and the one question

#### 01.11. LTGP:CAC above 3:1
- **Rule:** Measure advertising as lifetime gross profit per customer divided by the cost to acquire a customer, and require the ratio to be above 3 to 1; put the other way round, all the costs of getting a customer together stay at or below one third of the profit that customer makes over their lifetime.
- Why it works: every business the author invests in that struggles to scale has an LTGP to CAC below 3 to 1, and as soon as it goes above 3 to 1, by lowering CAC or raising LTGP, they take off (2023).
- Bad → Good: treating 3:1 as the goal → treating it as the bare minimum: the author says you can do WAY better, and the portfolio company he cites gets over 30:1 from its outreach.
- When not to apply: the author marks the ratio himself: "This is a pattern I personally observed, not a rule."
- Anchor: «to CAC ratio was ]{.calibre3}[less than]{.calibre24}[ 3 to 1. As soon as» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II; #2 Employees; #4 Affiliates

#### 01.12. Lifetime value is gross profit, not revenue
- **Rule:** Compute lifetime value — LTV, which the author also calls LTGP, lifetime gross profit, and in the playbooks CLV — as gross profit over the customer's lifetime: revenue minus the direct cost of delivering, multiplied by the number of purchases an average customer makes; indirect costs such as admin, software and rent stay out.
- Why it works: gross profit is the actual money you use to acquire customers, pay rent, cover payroll and everything else; definitions differ by source, and some count only total revenue.
- Bad → Good: counting lifetime revenue as LTV → a customer who buys ten things at $15 with $5 of delivery cost each: $150 of revenue, $50 of cost, $100 of lifetime gross profit (example — $100M Leads, #4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff).
- When not to apply: gross profit is not net profit: net profit is what is left after all expenses are paid, gross profit only after the direct costs of fulfilment.
- Anchor: «The gross profit accrued over the entire lifetime of a customer.This is gross profit multiplied by the number of purchases an average customer will make over their lifetime.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 3 Pricing: The Commodity Problem

#### 01.13. CAC counts every acquisition cost
- **Rule:** Count CAC as all the money it takes to get a customer — advertising dollars, marketing and sales payroll, creative, software, sales commissions and salaries — and never as ad spend alone.
- Why it works: content leads counted as free and outbound teams counted only by commission turn a $1,000 sale that seemed to cost $200 into one that really costs $500, and in some businesses that difference is the difference between $1,000,000 and $10,000,000 per month (2025).
- Bad → Good: reporting ad spend per customer as CAC while treating content leads as free and leaving the outbound team, salaries and software out → CAC known exactly, each month, by channel.
- When not to apply: unlike LTGP, CAC is a hard science: it can and should be known exactly; the source softens nothing here.
- Anchor: «payroll to a media buyer, creative team, software, sales commissions and salaries, etc» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Cost To Acquire a Customer = CAC

#### 01.14. Two levers on LTGP:CAC, chosen by 3x the industry average
- **Rule:** Fix the ratio with one of two levers, chosen by comparing your CAC with the industry average: within 3x the average, the advertising is good enough and the work goes to raising LTGP through a better business model (sheets 10–13); above 3x, the work goes to the advertising, to get cheaper customers through more efficient ads (sheets 04–07).
- Why it works: entrepreneurs often think they have crappy ads (high CAC) when in reality they have a crappy business model (low LTGP); the cost to acquire customers between competitors in the same industry is much closer than you would think, and the difference between winners and losers is how much they make off each customer.
- Bad → Good: concluding you have bad ads because customers are expensive → researching the industry average CAC first; below 3x, the ads stay and the money model changes.
- When not to apply: the switch cannot be read without research into the industry average CAC.
- Anchor: «You have two big levers to improving LTGP:CAC:» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II, Cost & Returns

#### 01.15. Costs can only approach zero
- **Rule:** Stop pushing advertising efficiency at the point where cutting the cost of a customer by $100 takes more work than making an extra $100 from that customer; from there the work is the business model.
- Why it works: costs can only approach zero while how much you make can go up to infinity; efficiency beyond that point is like trying to save your way to a billion dollars — you feel like you are making progress and you are never going to get there.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «infinity. Increasing advertising efficiency beyond a certain point is» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff

#### 01.16. Advertising problem or sales problem: one question
- **Rule:** When CAC is above 3x the industry average, ask one question — do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to spend? — and branch: not qualified is an advertising problem; qualified and buying but too few is an advertising problem; qualified and not buying is a sales problem. Fix the side the answer names, and do not fire the other one.
- Why it works: a company the author invested in spent twelve weeks and $150,000 on ads that got the right leads on the phone, blamed the advertising and quit; the misdiagnosis cost an estimated ~$30M in enterprise value (2023).
- Bad → Good: declaring that advertising does not work while the right leads are getting on the phone and not buying → the ads work fine and the sales do not: a sales problem (sheet 09).
- When not to apply: only once CAC is more than 3x the industry average; within 3x you are doing good enough and the work moves to LTGP (01.14).
- Anchor: «Do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees, Which Employees to Focus On; Personal Lessons from Paid Ads

#### 01.17. Returns from lead-getting employees: payroll against engaged leads
- **Rule:** Measure advertising done by employees — outreach, content — by payroll, excluding paid ad spend: total payroll divided by total engaged leads is the cost per engaged lead; times the engaged leads needed per customer, it is CAC; LTGP divided by CAC is the ratio, judged by 01.11.
- Why it works: the cost of advertising with employees is almost entirely the money you pay them to do it, so payroll against engaged leads is the whole calculation.
- Bad → Good: $100,000 of payroll over 1,000 engaged leads is $100 per engaged lead; one customer per ten leads makes CAC $1,000; against an LTGP of $4,000 the ratio is 4:1 (example — $100M Series: Lost Chapters, Section D, How to Calculate Returns From Lead-Getting Employees).
- When not to apply: paid ad spend is excluded from this calculation; paid ads have their own money rules (sheet 06).
- Anchor: «this by just comparing how much money we spend on payroll to how much» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Employees, How to Calculate Returns

## Cash: the thirty-day test

#### 01.18. Client financed acquisition: the customer pays for himself in thirty days
- **Rule:** Judge the money model by its first thirty days: the customer has to pay back more than it costs to get and fulfil them within the first thirty days, so the cash can be recycled into the next customer — client financed acquisition in $100M Leads, Customer Financed Acquisition (CFA) in the Lost Chapters, where the thirty-day gross profit is called 30D Cash; below that, cash is the constraint on growth.
- Why it works: any business can get interest-free money for thirty days in the form of a credit card; cover the cost to get and fulfil the customer in that window and you square the balance, keep the customer and repeat, so money stops being the bottleneck.
- Bad → Good: day 0, borrow $40 to acquire a customer; days 1–30, $50 of revenue at $10 of cost leaves $40 of gross profit; day 30, repay the $40 and re-borrow it for the second customer; day 60, two customers paying $80 of gross profit a month and zero debt (example — $100M Series: Lost Chapters, Payback Period = PPD).
- When not to apply: passing the test is the bare minimum; the author's working standard is 2x (01.19). The rule is for the case where the full LTGP takes months to collect and cash flow blocks scaling.
- Anchor: «But... if your customer spends more than it costs you to get» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II

#### 01.19. 2x is the real-life minimum
- **Rule:** Set the working standard at thirty-day gross profit of at least twice CAC, not merely above it.
- Why it works: at 2x you only have to buy your first customer; that customer pays for every other one and the business can grow as fast as it can handle.
- Bad → Good: putting all extra profit back into getting customers at twice CAC runs 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and on to 2,048 new customers a month: 4,095 customers in twelve months, of which only the first was paid for out of pocket (example — $100M Series: Lost Chapters, Levels of Customer Financed Acquisition).
- When not to apply: something else will eventually bottleneck growth at that pace, and that is fine; what must never be the bottleneck is cash for getting customers (01.24).
- Anchor: «And 2x is my “real life” minimum standard In practice, I want customers to more than» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Section B: The Expensive Customer Problem

#### 01.20. The three levels of Customer Financed Acquisition
- **Rule:** Place the business on the three levels of CFA by what the first thirty days of gross profit from a customer cover: less than CAC (level 1), the same as CAC (level 2), more than double CAC (level 3); pass level 1 to stay in business, expect level 2 from a decent product and a good offer, and reach level 3 by mastering money models (sheet 10).
- Why it works: at level 2 the credit limit becomes the advertising budget and caps how many customers you can get; at level 3 all customers pay for themselves and cash stops constraining growth.
- Bad → Good: level 1, floating the business on life savings, loans and lines of credit → level 3, where each customer pays for the next ones.
- When not to apply: you can absolutely make money at level 1 over the long term, and many big businesses make all their money that way, but you have to already have lots of money to do it; the author avoids it when starting out.
- Anchor: «By connecting GP and speed with CAC, I see three levels of CFA» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Levels of Customer Financed Acquisition

#### 01.21. A good Money Model and a $100M Money Model
- **Rule:** Rate the money model on the author's three levels: a Money Model is a series of offers; a good one makes more profit from a customer than it costs to get and service them in the first 30 days, which is the bare minimum; a $100M Money Model makes more profit from one customer in the first 30 days than it costs to get and service at least two more, which removes cash as the limit on growth.
- Why it works: below the first threshold the business loses money getting customers, which forces it to cut advertising and eventually fail; with so much money made in the first thirty days, the cost of getting more customers is never a problem again, and the business is forced to fix everything else just to keep up.
- Bad → Good: spending more to acquire a customer than that customer produces in profit and treating it as a normal cost of doing business → one customer producing enough money in less than 30 days to get and service at least two more.
- When not to apply: the good-model threshold is the bare minimum, not a target.
- Anchor: «**A good Money Model** *makes more profit from a customer than it» — $100M Money Models (2025), Ten Years In Ten Minutes

#### 01.22. The slow drip
- **Rule:** Do not rely on a slow drip of profit from many customers to eventually pay for a single customer; a profitable-but-slow deal is judged by how long the cash takes to come back, not by the profit alone.
- Why it works: the drip starves the business of cash: it can only get lots of customers through advertising if it already has lots of customers.
- Bad → Good: spend $100, make $500 in profit over two years, accepted without asking how long the cash takes to come back → a great business only if you already have tons of cash in the bank; otherwise you run out of money while waiting.
- When not to apply: stated for any business without investors or a large cash reserve; with tons of cash in the bank the slow deal can be afforded.
- Anchor: «*eventually* pays for a *single* customer. This 'drip' starves the» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section I, Beware: Bad Money Models Kill Businesses

#### 01.23. A 3.3:1 ratio can still have a cash-flow problem: sell more immediately
- **Rule:** When LTGP exceeds CAC but the first purchase does not cover CAC inside thirty days, sell the customer more immediately rather than waiting for the lifetime profit to accumulate, and rather than lowering CAC.
- Why it works: any business can get interest-free money for thirty days on a credit card, so covering the cost to get and fulfil a customer within thirty days squares the balance, removes money as the bottleneck and lets the cash be recycled into the next customer; everything after that is gravy.
- Bad → Good: a $15-a-month membership costing $5 to deliver leaves $10 of gross profit a month; at ten months of tenure that is $100 of LTGP against a $30 CAC, 3.3:1, yet only $10 comes back in month one → a $100 upsell at 100% margins taken by one in five customers adds $20 per customer on average, $10 + $20 = $30 inside thirty days, and the customer is free (2023).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Here's the way I fix it- ]{.calibre3}[I immediately sell them more» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II

#### 01.24. Something else will bottleneck growth; when the model works, the business breaks
- **Rule:** Once cash stops constraining growth, expect the next bottleneck — usually servicing customers rather than acquiring them — and treat it as normal; what must never be the bottleneck is cash for getting customers.
- Why it works: reliability has to be financial and operational both; when the Money Model starts working, the business starts breaking, and the author calls that part of the game.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: this is a stated limit of the method: it solves cash and hands you an operational problem the books do not solve; the author calls keeping up with the volume a problem for another book and suggests finding someone who can build and lead the team.
- Anchor: «will eventually bottleneck your growth And that’s OK. That’s life But you never want that» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Levels of Customer Financed Acquisition

#### 01.25. How money models evolve: the order of work over quarters
- **Rule:** Sequence the work in this order: get customers reliably; then make sure they pay for themselves reliably; then make sure they pay for other customers reliably; then maximize each customer's long-term value; then spend as many advertising dollars as you can. Each stage is improved until it is reliable financially and operationally, and pays for the next.
- Why it works: the author makes sure each stage pays for the next, and each stage is improved until it becomes reliable both financially and operationally.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: when your Money Model starts working, your business starts breaking; that is part of the game (01.24).
- Anchor: «● I make sure they pay for other customers reliably *then*» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section VI: Make Your Money Model, Description

## Offers and price: where to look first

#### 01.26. Not enough response: make the front end more appealing first
- **Rule:** When an offer does not get enough response, whether on a first acquisition channel or on a new one, make the front end more appealing with a free or discount wrapper before changing anything else (sheet 03).
- Why it works: people who otherwise would not respond need a reason to do so, and enhancing the Grand Slam Offer with a free or discount front end is the fastest way to give one.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: this is the rule for an offer that never got enough response; for a working offer whose lead flow drops, the order of change is 01.27.
- Anchor: «If you are not getting enough response, you probably need to make your front end» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Attract Section Conclusion: Brass Tacks

#### 01.27. Offer fatigue: change things in this order
- **Rule:** When lead flow from a working offer starts dropping, change things in this order: the creative (the images and pictures in the ads), the body copy, the headline — the "wrapper" of the offer — the duration, the free/discount enhancer, and only last the monetization structure, the series of offers and their price points.
- Why it works: most of the time it is the first handful of items that need changing, and the lower on the list you go the more operationally heavy it is; changing the machine creates inefficiency and operational drag that costs money, and entrepreneurs do it because they love change.
- Bad → Good: changing the offer itself, or continuing to change an offer that is already monetized, when results dip → exhausting the lighter variations first: creative, then copy, then the headline and duration, again and again, without touching the bottom of the list.
- When not to apply: reaching an audience one time in no way means an offer is fatigued; and once you have monetized an offer, rarely should you change it — the whole machine only as a last resort and for a darn good reason.
- Anchor: «As you market offers, you will need to create variations over time as the tastes of the market change over time. Here’s the order in which you will change things to keep lead flow consistent.» — $100M Offers (2021), ch. 16 Naming

#### 01.28. The genie's three options: doubling the price beats doubling customers
- **Rule:** Before choosing which lever of the business to work on, compare the three ways to double it — double new customers, double the number of purchases, double the price — by their effect on profit, not on revenue: on the same stats the first two give 3.5x profit and doubling the price gives 6x.
- Why it works: all three double hypothetical maximum revenue, but doubling the price adds no cost to acquire and no cost to deliver, so the whole increase drops to the bottom line, while the other two bring twice the delivery load.
- Bad → Good: picking the lever by revenue → the walkthrough on 30 new clients a month, 33% churn, $100 a month and 20% net margins: 3.5x profit, 3.5x profit, 6x profit (2025).
- When not to apply: the model counts only the costs of advertising and delivering, to show where profit is maximized; its exact multiples do not transfer to a business with a different cost structure.
- Anchor: «Since we know the first option 3.5x our profit, the second option 3.5x our profit, and» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Pricing To Make The Most Money

#### 01.29. A close rate above 50% means the price is too low
- **Rule:** If the business consistently closes more than 50% of its sales and wants to make more money, raise the price (sheet 13).
- Why it works: a close rate that high is evidence that there is room in the price; higher prices get fewer new customers but often make the business more money.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: the condition has both halves: you want to make more money, and you close over 50% consistently.
- Anchor: «High close rate = prices too low . If want to make more money, and you close over 50%» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Rules of Pricing I Follow

## Referrals

#### 01.30. The referral growth equation
- **Rule:** Measure the referral percentage and the churn percentage first, as a baseline, and read them together: referrals greater than churn, the business grows without any other advertising; equal, other advertising is needed to grow; less, advertising only breaks even.
- Why it works: with the Core Four inputs and outputs are roughly linear; with word of mouth one customer brings two and two bring four, so growth is exponential and can be maintained no matter how big you get — which is why so few scale on word of mouth: they lose customers faster than they get them.
- Bad → Good: trying to scale on word of mouth while churn runs ahead of referrals, the hamster wheel of death → the two percentages measured, and the product worked on until referrals outrun churn (01.31, sheet 14).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «get them. Look at the referral growth equation to see it in action.» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Customer Referrals - Word of Mouth

#### 01.31. If customers are not bringing customers, the product is the problem
- **Rule:** When a business selling direct to consumers gets few or no referrals, treat the product as the problem, not the advertising; the question to ask is why customers are too embarrassed to tell everyone they know about it.
- Why it works: if the product were exceptional, people would already know about it and you would have more business than you could handle; it may be okay, but unremarkable — not worthy of remark.
