# Scale and lead getters: push a working channel further, then get other people to advertise for you

Open this sheet when a business already gets engaged leads from at least one Core Four activity and wants more: how to push that channel with More, Better, New, when and in what order to add channels, how to use agencies and build affiliates, and the daily discipline that carries it. How to run the channels themselves is in sheet 04 (warm outreach, content, cold outreach) and sheet 06 (paid ads); customer referrals are in sheet 14, employees as lead getters in sheet 15, the Roadmap of advertisers and the referral threshold before ramping ads in sheet 01. Every figure on this sheet comes from $100M Leads (2023).

## More, Better, New

#### 05.1. More, Better, New
- **Rule:** When you already run a Core Four activity and still get fewer engaged leads than you want, boost it in this order: do more of what you are doing, then do it better, then do it somewhere new.
- Why it works: Even with no improvement, doubling the inputs gets more engaged leads, and the biggest increases often come from simply advertising more; more and better feed each other, and only once they are exhausted do the real returns come from new.
- Bad → Good: A chiropractic owner doing $2,000,000 a year calls his niche saturated → the three questions show how small a slice he works: more (he spends $30,000 a month on one platform), better (he knows neither his click-to-close conversion rate nor his throughput), new (no other platforms, no content, no cold outreach), in a $15.1B industry where a second owner in the same niche spent $30k across four platforms the previous week.
- When not to apply: Applies only once a Core Four activity is running; new waits until more and better stop paying (05.4).
- Anchor: «you advertise more? Could you advertise better? Could you advertise» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids: More Better New; summary

#### 05.2. The Rule of 100
- **Rule:** In the Core Four activity you have chosen, do 100 primary actions every day for 100 days in a row: 100 warm reach outs a day; or 100 minutes a day making content, releasing at least one piece a day on a platform; or 100 cold reach outs a day, using automation because response rates are lower; or 100 minutes a day making paid ads and 100 days straight of running them.
- Why it works: Advertising is an inputs-and-outputs game, effort in and engaged leads out; people usually underestimate by far the volume it takes, and the right action in the wrong amount still fails, which is why the author's one promise is that 100 actions a day for 100 days brings more engaged leads.
- Bad → Good: A complaint that 100 reach outs over six weeks brought one customer, so outreach does not work → that was 1/42 of the work required: the rule is 100 per day, not 100 over time (example — $100M Leads, Advertising in Real Life: Open To Goal).
- When not to apply: It is the "More" of More, Better, New; once it has become normal, the next level is Open To Goal (05.24), and step #2 of the One Page Advertising Checklist (05.23) makes you choose between the two.
- Anchor: «The rule of 100 is simple. You advertise your stuff by doing 100» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids, More

#### 05.3. One test per week per platform
- **Rule:** Every Monday start one split test per platform, placed at the step where most leads drop off (the constraint); the next Monday pick the winners, write the results into a log of all tests, and design the next test to beat the current best version; if four tries (or one month) cannot beat the version you run, move on to the next constraint (01.7, 01.8).
- Why it works: Changing several things at once on one platform means you never learn what worked, and steps affect each other; one test a week forces you to prioritise, a week is long enough to see whether the change is a real improvement, and the log means the next attempt starts many improvements later instead of at square one.
- Bad → Good: Several changes on one platform in the same week → a change at step one can raise optins while applications fall, and nobody can tell which change did what → one split test per platform per week, its result logged.
- When not to apply: One week is the author's calibration for his team size and ad spend, not a universal length: too short gives too little data, too long wastes time you could spend on the next constraint.
- Anchor: «In every company I own, I set up a testing schedule. Every Monday we» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids, Better

#### 05.4. When to go new, and in what order
- **Rule:** Add something new only when the returns from doing more and better fall below what the same effort would return in a new placement or a new way of advertising, then expand roughly in this sequence: new placements on a platform you know, then new platforms, then a new Core Four activity — so a second Core Four activity comes only after the first has been maxed out with more, better and new.
- Why it works: New is much harder in practice; the order ranks options by leads per unit of work, which nine times out of ten puts placements first and a new Core Four activity last; you only need one activity to get engaged leads, the money, systems and experience earned from it help you master the next, and doing more until it breaks shows better where the next drop-off is.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The trigger is comparative returns, not boredom with the current channel.
- Anchor: «]{.calibre3}[new]{.calibre24}[. Use this rough order: new placement, new» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids, New

#### 05.5. The Size Of The Pie Fallacy
- **Rule:** Do not conclude that a market is saturated, or that a plateau means there are no more leads, while you advertise with one Core Four activity, on one platform, in one way, to one targeted audience; treat the plateau as a limit of your advertising and put the three questions of 05.1 to it.
- Why it works: A small business and its handful of competitors in the same slice mistake that slice for the whole available market; saying "I'm as big as I can get" is easier than saying "I'm not as good at advertising as I thought", and that false argument is why most businesses stay small.
- Bad → Good: An entrepreneur making about $3,000,000 a year in weight loss feared that pushing past $40,000 a month in ads would saturate a platform with over 1B daily active users, selling weight loss in America, a $60B industry.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «universe they advertise to is the entire available market! This is why» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids: More Better New

## Choosing and adding channels

#### 05.6. First pass: cold outreach only after warm reach outs and content
- **Rule:** On a first pass through the Core Four, start cold outreach only after warm reach outs have given you reps and posted content has grown your warm audience — never as the first advertising method — and pick it up when you run out of people to advertise to or simply want more leads.
- Why it works: Cold outreach is the more advanced cousin of warm outreach and sits on its foundation; warm outreach is the cheapest and easiest way to find people interested in what you sell, while cold outreach frees you from the size of your warm audience, lets you pick your targets and makes lead flow more predictable.
- Bad → Good: Skipping one-to-one contact with the people who already know you, as most businesses do → warm reach outs for reps, then content to grow the warm audience, then cold outreach (example — $100M Leads, #1 Warm Outreach).
- When not to apply: The author's own choice when building a business, after warm outreach, is 05.8; the trigger for cold outreach is an exhausted warm audience or a wish for more volume and predictability, not a liking for the channel.
- Anchor: «reach outs. Get some reps. Post some content to grow your warm audience.» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach, Three Problems Strangers Create→Solved

#### 05.7. First pass: paid ads last
- **Rule:** When learning the Core Four, take up paid ads last, after warm outreach, content and cold outreach.
- Why it works: Skills from the other three methods transfer to paid ads and shorten the learning curve, and paid ads cost money that the other three will have earned you by then.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Scoped to the order in which to learn the Core Four; the author's own choice when building a business is 05.8. A business with neither money to lose nor skills from the other channels is not the place for paid ads yet.
- Anchor: «I recommend doing paid ads ]{.calibre3}[last]{.calibre41}[ for two» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Run Paid Ads Part II, Conclusion

#### 05.8. Building a business: the next channel follows the resource you have more of
- **Rule:** When building a business and choosing where to put advertising effort after warm outreach — the author's own practice — pay with the resource you have more of: more time than money → post content; more money than time → cold outreach or paid ads.
- Why it works: Advertising is paid for with time, money or both, so the method you pick should be paid for with the resource you actually have.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The book's own order for learning the Core Four is 05.6 and 05.7 — warm reach outs, content, cold outreach, paid ads last; the source states both orders and does not choose between them.
- Anchor: «I have more time than money, I move to posting content. If I have more» — $100M Leads (2023), Core Four On Steroids, Conclusion

#### 05.9. The four lead getters
- **Rule:** To get more engaged leads than your own advertising brings, get lead getters — #1 customers, #2 employees, #3 agencies, #4 affiliates, in the order they arise naturally — by doing the Core Four to get them, so that they then do the Core Four on your behalf.
- Why it works: When other people advertise for you, you save time and get more engaged leads for less work; the Core Four stacks, once to get the lead getter and again when the lead getter gets leads for you, and lead getters can go get lead getters.
- Bad → Good: ALAN's layers: a single super-affiliate brought ten agencies a month, those agencies about fifty local businesses a month, and those businesses about 2500 leads a month worked at roughly $5 each, $12,500 a month; because each super-affiliate kept adding, one produced $12,500, $25,000 and $37,500 in its first three months, and a few of them took the company to $1,700,000 a month within six months of launch (example — $100M Leads, #4 Affiliates and Partners, Why You Want An Affiliate Army).
- When not to apply: Lead getters are not Core Four activities: you do not "do" referrals, affiliates, agencies or employees, you do the Core Four to get them; referrals, a way of doing business rather than a method, are in sheet 14, employees in sheet 15.
- Anchor: «[#1 Customers]{.calibre11}[- they buy your stuff then tell other people» — $100M Leads (2023), Section IV: Get Lead Getters; restated Section IV Conclusion

#### 05.10. Three to six months to crack a lead source
- **Rule:** Do not drop a chosen lead source after a few losses; allow three to six months to crack a new one, and if you expect it faster, check whether that expectation is reasonable.
- Why it works: Losing at the beginning is normal; chasing shortcuts can eat a decade before people see they should have picked one strategy and stuck with it, and an obsession with getting rich quick will likely ensure it never happens.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Three to six months is the yardstick of an author for whom this is not his first rodeo; losses inside that window are not evidence that the method failed.
- Anchor: «in three to six months (and this isn't my first rodeo). So if your» — $100M Leads (2023), Section IV Conclusion

## Agencies

#### 05.11. Agencies only to learn a new method or a new platform
- **Rule:** Hire an agency only to learn a new method or a new platform, and only once you have money to pay a good one; for a platform you do not understand, hire one good-enough agency to learn the ropes and a more elite one to learn how to maximise it.
- Why it works: Good agencies have already made the big mistakes, so hiring one skips the trial and error and goes straight to the make-money part, buying a skill you could otherwise learn only by spending the time that should go into scaling.
- Bad → Good: Unable to afford either agency, the author asked the second agency's owner to show him how they would run his ads; told the owner's time was not for sale, then quoted $750 an hour with four hours ($3,000) up front, he met one hour a week with homework between calls, every call recorded and rewatched: the agency drove calls one and two, he drove three and four, by five and six he understood the decisions and the data tracked, by seven and eight he needed no help — eight hours and $6000 bought a skill that made millions; learning YouTube later, he hired one agency to keep him committed and do legwork and a second at 4x the price to teach the in-depth ideas, dropping to consulting once his team's videos beat theirs.
- When not to apply: With no money there is no agency: the skill is learned by trial and error, which is no big deal because everyone starts that way.
- Anchor: «suggest using agencies for two things: learning new methods and learning» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Agencies

#### 05.12. How I Use Agencies Now: buy the skill, then leave
- **Rule:** Start each agency relationship by stating its purpose and a deadline: work with them about six months to learn how they do it, pay extra for them to explain their decisions and steps, train your team on it, run both teams until yours regularly beats theirs, then switch to discounted consulting until you feel you are teaching them, and cut them loose, putting the money into scaling.
- Why it works: You get better short-term results because the agency probably knows more than you, and better long-term results because you or your team learn the skill; you only ever hold a fraction of an agency's attention, so its results slip whenever it takes on new clients, while your own team stays focused on you full-time.
- Bad → Good: The agency cycle of insanity — excitement, onboarding, the best senior rep, some results, the senior rep moved to the newest client, a junior rep, worse results, complaints, cancellation, the next agency → an opening that says you want to do what they do but don't know how, asks for six months to learn it, offers extra pay for them to break down their decisions and steps, announces that your team will be trained and the relationship will move to lower-cost consulting, and asks whether they are opposed.
- When not to apply: Budget for a long stretch of paying the agency and your team for the same work, which is out of reach without money to spare (the author first needed a year to build a team as good as an agency, later ten months, then eight, now under six); if an agency refuses these terms, move on, but be willing to negotiate, since at some price it is worth it for both parties.
- Anchor: «start every agency relationship with a purpose and a deadline to fulfill» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Agencies; Next Steps

#### 05.13. What I look for in a good agency: ten checks
- **Rule:** Before signing, talk with several agencies — a few more even after one agrees to your terms — and score each on ten checks: someone you know got good results with them; prominent companies did; a waiting list; a clear sales process that sets realistic expectations; long-term strategy with clear timelines for setup, scaling and results instead of short-term hacks; they say exactly what they need from you, when, and how they use it; a regular meeting schedule and several ways to report progress; simple updates with tracking of costs against results; a good offer by dream outcome, perceived likelihood of achievement, time delay, and effort and sacrifice; a high price.
- Why it works: The best agencies live solely on word of mouth, so one you know only from its own paid ads or cold outreach is probably worse; and while every good agency is expensive, a high price alone proves nothing, which is why the other nine checks exist.
- Bad → Good: Taking an agency's high price, or the ads it runs for itself, as proof that it is good → shortlisting on referrals, prominent clients and a waiting list, then comparing several against the full list.
- When not to apply: The author offers the list as useful things that worked for him, not the last word on what makes a good agency.
- Anchor: «After working with tons of bad agencies, and a handful of good ones, I» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Agencies, How to Pick The Right Agency

## Affiliates

#### 05.14. Six steps to an Affiliate Army
- **Rule:** Build affiliates through six steps in order — find your ideal affiliates, make them an offer, qualify them, figure out what to pay them, get them advertising, keep them advertising — and advertise the affiliate offer only until 10–20 affiliates have joined, then use their results and feedback to fix the offer, terms, launches and integration before scaling.
- Why it works: Affiliates are among the most advanced ways to get engaged leads: you must convince them to advertise someone else's stuff, then yours, then to keep at it so they become a long-term lead source; the results of the first 10–20 become the first batch of affiliate lead magnets used to scale.
- Bad → Good: A $50M business preparing LLCs, bank accounts and articles of incorporation does not compete with Legalzoom: it partners with people who train new entrepreneurs, offers every affiliate's customer a free LLC setup (a high-cost lead magnet), launches with a big blast off seminar to the affiliates' audiences, then has affiliates build the free LLC into their own core offer while its team calls the customers they send and sells what they need next, bookkeeping and tax preparation — no paid ads, only the cost of the lead magnet and a percentage of every first sale (example — $100M Leads, #4 Affiliates and Partners, Three Case Studies You Can Model).
- When not to apply: Affiliates are not customer referrals: they are independent businesses with their own advertising and audiences, whereas a referring customer has no business or audience of their own (see sheet 14).
- Anchor: «How To Build An Affiliate Army in Six Steps» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates and Partners

#### 05.15. Find your ideal affiliates: who's got my leads?
- **Rule:** Build the affiliate hit list from businesses whose warm audience is full of people like your customers: ask of your best customers what they buy (who provides it), where they go (which businesses are nearby), what they like to do (who provides it) and, if you sell direct to consumer, what businesses they work for; sort the answers into softwares, products, equipment, services, groups they belong to and events they attended, and put first the businesses that fall into several categories.
- Why it works: Once you know which businesses already have your leads, you know exactly where to put your advertising effort, and a business that shows up in several categories very likely has many good leads for you and would make a great affiliate.
- Bad → Good: Starting ALAN, the author listed 200 products and services used by agencies, and they fit neatly into the six categories.
- When not to apply: If you struggle to answer the questions, call up your customers and ask them.
- Anchor: «The ideal affiliate has a business with a warm audience full of people» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates and Partners, Step 1

#### 05.16. Offer affiliates a new way to make money
- **Rule:** When recruiting affiliates, offer them a fast, simple and easy new way to make money, not your product, and advertise that offer to them like any other — callout, value elements, call to action — with the affiliate as the customer.
- Why it works: Affiliates are businesses, or become one by signing up, that do their own advertising and agree to offer your stuff to their engaged leads for money, free stuff or both, so they sign up only for a strong reason, and for a business that reason is new income.
- Bad → Good: Advertising your product to potential affiliates as you would to customers → calling out one target five ways, spa owners: the owners themselves (ATTENTION SPA OWNERS), their customers (Do you work with busy professionals who spend all day in meetings?), the result they promise (To the heroes who heal the stress of others...), what they sell (If you sell lotions or scented oils this is for you...), and your own customers (Do you know anyone who owns a spa?).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «offer them a new way to make money. ]{.calibre24}[We'll start with the» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates, Step 2

#### 05.17. Qualify them: make them a customer, make them an expert
- **Rule:** Qualify a potential affiliate by making them invest: they must buy, and preferably use, the product to keep affiliate status, and pay for the onboarding and training that certifies them as a product expert, priced at 10–20% of what the average active affiliate makes in the first twelve months ($100M Leads, 2023); lower the commitment if too few people start, raise it if too few follow through.
- Why it works: Nine times out of ten, if they pay, they pay attention; the more they invest in your product the more they make, someone who won't buy your stuff probably shouldn't sell it, and the fee covers some advertising cost and pays for proper onboarding; too low a fee leaves them uninvested, too high leaves you too few affiliates, and 10–20% maximised the number who became active.
- Bad → Good: An affiliate averaging $40,000 a year is charged $4000–$8000 for certification.
- When not to apply: If you are just starting with physical products, use the bulk purchasing strategy; otherwise raise the minimum investment every five sign ups until you hit the sweet spot.
- Anchor: «invested and winning: make them a customer, and make them an expert.» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates and Partners, Step 3: Qualify Them

#### 05.18. The three-tier payout from the maximum allowable CAC
- **Rule:** Set affiliate pay from the maximum allowable CAC — the gross profit per customer left after the business keeps its share at your target LTGP:CAC (at 3:1, three parts to the business and one to the affiliate) — and pay it in three tiers: 25% for anyone who agrees to your initial terms, 50% once they activate, 100% once they sustain a level of performance.
- Why it works: Affiliates differ in what they deliver, so the full payout is reserved for the ones who perform; the blended average then lands well below the maximum, and the difference funds contests, recruiting more affiliates, rewards for rising stars, or profit.
- Bad → Good: A $200 single-use product costing $40 to fulfil leaves $160, $120 to the business and a $40 maximum for the affiliate; paying every affiliate the full $40 → tiers of $10 (signs up and buys products or a certification), $20 (finishes the certification, does a set number of posts and outreach, runs a launch) and $40 (keeps five customers a month on subscription); with sales split 20/20/60 across the tiers the blended payout is $30 and LTGP:CAC moves from 3:1 to 4:1.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «I give it to. Not all affiliates are created equal. So, I suggest having» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates and Partners, Step 4

#### 05.19. Whisper-tease-shout, on a tightening cadence
- **Rule:** Launch in three phases that mirror the three chunks of an ad: whisper (think call outs: curiosity, a mysterious product, short posts that show the work) every 4–6 weeks until 60 days out, then every 2–3 weeks until 30 days out; tease (think elements of value: reveal the product, make the date public, use the What-Who-When framework) once a week until 14 days out, then twice a week until 3 days out; shout (think call to action: specific actions, bonuses, scarcity, urgency, guarantees for being first) at least twice a day from 3 days out, every few hours on launch day until 2 hours out, then every 30 minutes until launch.
- Why it works: Curiosity comes from wanting to know what happens next, so whispers plant questions and teases answer them; the longer something appears to take, the more an audience values it, which is why the whisper phase can start years out; the shout exposes as many people as possible to the offer as it goes live.
- Bad → Good: The author's own book launch: whisper — which draft he was on, photos of printing drafts, the many versions of his frameworks, videos of editing; tease — the product and launch date revealed, hard specifics, the dream outcome of limitless leads with less work and faster, dozens of examples of the book in use; shout — short, clear reminders to register and bonuses only for launch buyers.
- When not to apply: The author does not claim the name (he cannot remember where he first heard it) and uses it to launch anything, not only affiliates; when affiliates launch, do all the work for them ahead of time so they can plug and play.
- Anchor: «should, you may as well do them right. I use the whisper-tease-shout» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates and Partners, Step 5: Get Them Advertising -- Launch

#### 05.20. Integration: build your product into the affiliate's offer
- **Rule:** To keep an activated affiliate advertising, make your product part of the affiliate's own offer in one of three arrangements, easiest to hardest: they hand out your lead magnet free with every purchase of their stuff, sell your lead magnet separately to their audience, or sell your core offer directly; when you split money on your core offer, pay the affiliate for as long as the customer stays, never capped.
- Why it works: The launch starts affiliates advertising and integration keeps them at it, so one affiliate sale can bring engaged leads for life; a free lead magnet makes their own offer more valuable at no extra cost while you upsell your core offer, and uncapped payouts keep affiliates motivated to keep your customers forever.
- Bad → Good: Chiropractors with gym affiliates: long term, one to two adjustments are included with every new gym membership, which raises the membership's value against the gym down the street and signals that the gym cares about member safety, so every new member becomes a lead — repeated across thirty gyms (example — $100M Leads, #4 Affiliates and Partners, Three Case Studies You Can Model).
- When not to apply: All three arrangements work, they are just different: after testing, the author's companies run the first twice a year as a big event and the third on an ongoing basis, while many similar portfolio businesses use the second.
- Anchor: «I've got three ways you can integrate your product into their offer. I» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates and Partners, Step 6: Keep Them Advertising

#### 05.21. The affiliate keeps the lead-magnet cash
- **Rule:** When an affiliate sells a lead magnet that you fulfil, let them keep all the cash from it, and make your money by selling your core offer for more than the lead magnet cost you to deliver.
- Why it works: The sale becomes all profit and no work for the affiliate, an attractive proposition for any business, so they want to do it more and send even more leads.
- Bad → Good: Gyms sell your nutrition consult for $99 or $199 and keep all the money, which makes them send even more leads, while your supplements are upsold during the consult.
- When not to apply: This is the second integration arrangement, where nothing on the core offer is split; when the affiliate sells your core offer directly, the money is split and paid uncapped (05.20).
- Anchor: «sample product, etc. Also, giving affiliates all the cash from selling a» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates, Step 6; case study

#### 05.22. Affiliate returns: cost per affiliate against their customers' gross profit
- **Rule:** Judge an affiliate programme by setting the cost of acquiring one affiliate against the gross profit of all the customers that affiliate sends, not against money made from the affiliate; require at least 3:1 and aim for 5:1 or 10:1 and up ($100M Leads, 2023), and below 3:1 lower CAC with better ads, offer and sales process, get more affiliates to activate with a launch process, or make each affiliate worth more with a better integration process.
- Why it works: Money goes out to recruit affiliates, but little comes back from the affiliates themselves; the money returns through the customers they bring.
- Bad → Good: A widget company spends $4000 in advertising per affiliate; the average affiliate sells $10,000 a month for twelve months, and $120,000 at 75% gross margin is $90,000 of gross profit; a 40% payout of $36,000 leaves $54,000 against the $4000, which the source scores at 12.5:1.
- When not to apply: Not the customer LTGP:CAC, where the money comes back from the same person you paid to acquire; if the ratio is already there, just do more.
- Anchor: «compare how much it costs us to get an affiliate with the gross profit» — $100M Leads (2023), #4 Affiliates, Costs and Returns

## Advertising in real life

#### 05.23. One Page Advertising Checklist
- **Rule:** Keep the advertising plan to one page, filled out in about five minutes, in five steps: choose which kind of engaged lead to go after (customers, affiliates, employees or agencies); pick Rule of 100 or Open To Goal and commit to your daily advertising actions; fill out the advertising checklist for that daily action (its fields are given in the book only as images and are not carried in this skill); do that action daily until you can afford to pay someone else to do it; then return to step 1 with employees as the target lead type, repeat until you have the help you need, and scale again.
- Why it works: A single page leaves little room for excuses, distractions and delusions — you either did the work or you didn't — and the same steps that get customers get the people who then get you customers.
- Bad → Good: No planning at all, or a one-hundred-page plan that never gets used → one page of action steps, done in about five minutes.
- When not to apply: The book gives the content of steps #2 and #3 only as images, so the checklist's own fields are not in the source; do not invent them.
- Anchor: «[Step #1:]{.calibre11}[ Pick The Type Of Engaged Lead To Get: Customers,» — $100M Leads (2023), Advertising in Real Life: Open To Goal, One Page Advertising Checklist

#### 05.24. Open To Goal
- **Rule:** Once the Rule of 100 has become normal, commit each day to a specific number of outcomes rather than a number of actions or hours, and work until you hit it no matter what, doing that advertising work in one uninterrupted block at the start of the day, before fires, people and day-to-day work.
- Why it works: Committing to outcomes unlocks a level of effort you did not know you had — give up doing your best and do what is required; the magic is not in waking early but in a long stretch of uninterrupted work right after a long stretch of uninterrupted sleep, the most productive hours in a row spent on the most productive work.
- Bad → Good: A gym chain let its sales managers set their own schedules on one condition, five new members signed per day no matter what: done by lunch, they could leave early; if it took 18 hours, so be it.
- When not to apply: It is the Rule of 100 for the big kids, not a starting point: it may mean fifty attempts or five thousand every day for years, and sometimes your best just has to get better.
- Anchor: «a specific number of times... you commit to the work until you hit a» — $100M Leads (2023), Advertising in Real Life

#### 05.25. Test at a volume you can count
- **Rule:** Run a test at a volume where the expected response rate yields a countable number of responses — 5,000 flyers, not 300 — and when a winner appears, put it out at that volume every day.
- Why it works: At half a percent (decent) or one percent (a winner), 300 flyers yield one and a half people, so a winner cannot be told from a loser.
- Bad → Good: Putting out 300 flyers and trying to judge the result → the author's mentor tested 5,000 in a single day, then, with a winner found, ran 5,000 a day, every day, for a month.
- When not to apply: The half-percent and one-percent benchmarks are for flyers ($100M Leads, 2023).
- Anchor: «a small number...I test with 5000. Then when we find a winner, we put» — $100M Leads (2023), Advertising in Real Life

#### 05.26. A budget for crazy ideas
- **Rule:** With an established advertising budget, set aside a fixed share — 1%, 5% or 10% — for new campaigns, channels, pages and plain crazy ideas, and expect no return from it (advice the author credits to a speaker at a private entrepreneurs' event).
- Why it works: Every test teaches you something, so the money is an investment in your education, and when one of the tests wins, and some do, it makes far more than was spent.
- Bad → Good: After hearing this, the author tripled his ad budget the next week: June revenue of $400,000 became $780,000 in July; when CAC rose he tried new audiences, most failed, one hit and revenue passed $1M, $1.2M, $1.5M; email and phone follow-up of engaged leads did nothing, text blasts took it to $1.8M; more paid ads with higher production value took it past $2.5M, and the affiliate program stacked another $1.5M a month on top, past $4M.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
