# Pricing, LTV and Fast Cash: set and raise prices, make each customer worth more, raise cash fast from warm customers

Open this sheet when the task is to set a price, add revenue through pricing mechanics, raise prices on an existing base, make each customer worth more over time, or run a short, limited, high-ticket promotion to warm customers.
It holds the Rules of Pricing, picking a price from tested numbers, the Pricing Plays, the rules for raising prices, the RAISE letter, the Crazy Eight for lifetime value, and the Fast Cash Play.

## Rules of pricing

#### 13.1. Keep raising prices until the extra money no longer offsets the loss in sales
- **Rule:** Keep raising the price as long as the extra money from the sales you still make outweighs the sales you lose, judge each raise on total money rather than on the number of yeses, and do not change the price back when the first no arrives.
- Why it works: A higher price makes money twice, through more gross profit per customer and, with fewer customers, a lower cost to deliver; going from a 50% close rate to a 30% close rate on double the price makes more money even though you hear no about 40% more often, and in the author's experience 9 times out of 10 a raise makes more profit than it loses in sales (2025).
- Bad → Good: The owner hears more nos after a raise and caves, going back to charging less to get more yesses → the author tripled his gym's price from $99 to $299 and lost 30% of the customers: 200 × $99 became 140 × $299, so revenue more than doubled while the cost to deliver fell by about 30%.
- When not to apply: Some markets are price sensitive and others less so; the 9-out-of-10 rate is the author's experience, not a guarantee, and the rule holds only for an owner willing to stomach a higher rate of rejection.
- Anchor: «Keep raising prices until the amount of extra money you make from new sales no» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Rules of Pricing I Follow

#### 13.2. Bill less frequently; Pricing Play #5: Annual Billing
- **Rule:** Bill on the longest cycle the business can push, display the price in the smallest time increment (the two need not match), and sell the longer cycles as a descending ladder added alongside standard pricing: the full annual price first, then a prepaid-year discount of 17%, then a prepaid quarter at 8% off, then standard monthly with no discount.
- Why it works: Every billing cycle is an opportunity to cancel, and customers judge value inside the lookback window of the last billing cycle rather than over the whole relationship; the small display increment gives the lowest perceived price while the long billing cycle gives the profit and the lower churn; the first number out of your mouth anchors the conversation, so prepaying reads as a benefit rather than monthly reading as a penalty.
- Bad → Good: A client paying $5,000 a month who was made $60,000 in the first month still cancels if the next month makes them $0 → longer cycles create less churn and bring more qualified customers, who pay for longer periods up front; Profitwell data at a $100 price gives annual billing 2% monthly churn and $5,000 LTV, quarterly 5% and $2,000, monthly 10.7% and $935, that is 5.35x and 2.14x the LTV of monthly; with a 16% annual discount the author sees 10–15% choose annual on a sales page, 30% when it is the default and 35–40% over the phone, and at 30% prepaying a $100 upfront collection becomes an average first transaction of $370 (2025). (example — the Pricing playbook, Pricing Play #5: Annual Billing)
- When not to apply: The lookback-window explanation is the author's own theory, not established fact; selling annual only is an advanced move that drops conversions because the price asked is 12x the monthly rate, by an amount the author says he does not know and you would have to test, and his workaround is to sell six to twelve weeks first and upsell the prepaid year once trust is built.
- Anchor: «Bill less frequently to have lower churn . The more often you bill the more often peo-» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Rules of Pricing I Follow

#### 13.3. Bill one-time value and ongoing value separately
- **Rule:** When an offer holds both one-time value and ongoing value, bill the one-time part as a one-time fee and the ongoing part as a separate, smaller recurring fee, and never turn something with one-time value into a recurring subscription.
- Why it works: Sold as one price, the one-time part ends up underpriced and the ongoing part overpriced; the day after someone learns something, access to that information is worth close to zero, so a customer still paying a price set for not knowing it stops paying, and pricing has to follow the value actually created.
- Bad → Good: Information and accountability sold together as one price → a one-time fee for access to the information plus a smaller ongoing fee for the accountability.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Match how you bill with how you provide value . One-Time vs On-Going Value Pric-» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Rules of Pricing I Follow

#### 13.4. Customer Surplus
- **Rule:** Price against customer surplus, the gap between the value the customer receives and the price: charge the most you can while that gap stays in the customer's favour, and treat its size as the quantity that sets word of mouth and repurchase rate.
- Why it works: The amount of surplus typically dictates how good word of mouth is and how many times customers repurchase, which is why the aim is to keep customers happy at the highest price; a premium price funds the gross profit needed to make a far superior product, which leaves surplus for them and net profit for you.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Bill as much as you can while keeping customers happy . Customer Surplus. The dif-» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Rules of Pricing I Follow

## Picking the price

#### 13.5. Test, test, test: Three Metrics To Determine value-Driven Pricing
- **Rule:** Pick the price from tested numbers: for each price point record conversion rate, churn and lifetime value in one table and take the row with the highest total return, the price that makes the most money over the customer's lifetime rather than the one that wins the most customers or the biggest first purchase; if doubling the price costs fewer than 20% of deals with all else equal, double it, and run the next tests between the winner and the price that lost.
- Why it works: Price moves both conversion rate and churn, and they do not suffer proportionally: when the price of a non-luxury good goes up people buy and repurchase less often, but you often lose less in conversion than you gain in lifetime value, so only total lifetime return can judge a price.
- Bad → Good: 100 clicks at each price: $10 converts 5% for 5 sales, 10% churn, $100 LTV, $500 total; $20 converts 4% for 4 sales, 10% churn, $200 LTV, $800 total, 60% more from the same clicks, which triples profit for a business on 30% margins; $100 converts 2% for 2 sales, 33% churn, $300 LTV, $600 total → $20 wins, and the next tests run between $20 and $100, around $39 to $59.
- When not to apply: The elasticity is stated for non-luxury goods; the dollar figures of the table do not matter, only the relationship between the numbers.
- Anchor: «If you can double your price and close 20% fewer deals, with all else staying the same...» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), How To Pick Your Price

#### 13.6. Crazy Eight #1 Increase Prices: the break-even conversion rate
- **Rule:** Before a price change, work out the gross profit per unit at the new price and the break-even conversion rate against the old one; then set the new price, track conversion, keep the price if conversion stays above break-even, and test again until conversion rate × lifetime gross profit drops, repeating the test every quarter.
- Why it works: Profit peaks where conversion rate times lifetime gross profit is highest, so conversion alone cannot say whether a new price won; price moves gross profit more than any other of the eight levers and the extra drops straight to the bottom line, and a Profitwell study suggested a tight relationship between profitability and how often a company tests pricing (2025).
- Bad → Good: Skipping price tests because they cost money → testing anyway, since a few expensive months beat a business under-monetized for life, and a sale made at the wrong price can be fixed with extra bonuses or by refunding the difference; a business on 10% profit that raises prices 20% with sales held flat triples.
- When not to apply: A brand-new offer has no sales to test against: start low to confirm that people want the thing, nudge the price up about 20% every 10 sales or so until sales drop dramatically, then go back to the sweet spot.
- Anchor: «a) Sales conversion rate x lifetime gross profit. The price that gets the most people to» — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (2025), #1 Increase Prices

#### 13.8. Pricing Play #3: Sales Tax
- **Rule:** Where your state or country charges sales tax on what you sell, charge it on top of the agreed price instead of absorbing it: agree the price first, then put the tax on the invoice or at point of sale as its own line item ahead of the total, citing the tax code dry and matter of fact, and if the customer balks, get angrier about the tax than they are.
- Why it works: Sales tax is taken from the top line, so a business with 20% margins that pays 5% sales tax for its customers gives away 25% of its profit; customers are not offended by tax at a restaurant checkout, and they are not offended by it here either.
- Bad → Good: Paying the tax for the customer out of a 20% margin → an invoice line such as "State tax code [1030a.o] mandates that personal services are subject to 6% sales tax", and to a balk, taking their side: you do not even keep it, you hand it straight to Uncle Sam and it only makes your life harder.
- When not to apply: If you are not charged the tax you cannot charge it; the alternative the author gives is to incorporate in a state that does not charge sales tax, checking with lawyers first.
- Anchor: «Taxes are a huge cost in business, and sales tax comes off the top line. If you’re simply» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Pricing Play #3: Sales Tax

#### 13.9. Pricing Play #4: Annual Price Increases
- **Rule:** Write a fixed annual price increase of 5–15% into every new contract and customer agreement, and raise it while filling out the paperwork, never during the sale.
- Why it works: Costs and inflation rise whether you act or not, so a price left alone has already changed, in the wrong direction ($100 in 2024 bought what $79 bought in 2017), while companies that continually optimize and test pricing make far more money than those that don't; a 5% annual step compounds to 22% over four years.
- Bad → Good: An owner who says an annual increase won't make a difference in his business, and has not adjusted prices in five years → a CPI clause on every new contract, with the sales-team line that prices are kept standard with the CPI, so the business has no incentive to cut the quality of what the customer gets.
- When not to apply: The author's ceiling is 15% a year, below which few people balk.
- Anchor: «Pick a reasonable percentage 5 to 15 percent is a good place to start. Simply add it to» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Pricing Play #4: Annual Price Increases

#### 13.10. Pricing Play #6: Round Up
- **Rule:** On premium and ordinary goods, nudge every price up to the nearest 9, turning prices that end in 7 into 9 and putting .99 on every fee, on contracts for new customers immediately.
- Why it works: In the author's gyms the change added $104 to $155.48 per client per year, a 4.25% to 11.1% price increase, with no change in conversion, on a business type that runs 12.5% net margins (2025).
- Bad → Good: A premium product kept on a round number as if it were luxury → premium does not mean luxury: a premium good's price reflects the value of the product itself, so it takes the .99 and the 9 endings.
- When not to apply: Luxury items, which usually end on a round number because people who buy luxury want not to get a deal and the expense itself makes the thing valuable; even then, test it, since sometimes shorter numbers do better.
- Anchor: «Change 7s to 9s. Add .99 to all fees. This takes so little time, and the only thing easier» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Pricing Play #6: Round Up

#### 13.11. Pricing Play #7: Annual Renewal Fee On Top of Monthly
- **Rule:** In markets too price conscious to bill annually, keep the advertised monthly rate and add a renewal fee of 1–3x the monthly rate on each contract anniversary, give it a beneficial reason why (rate protection, or the right to go month to month after the year without a cancellation fee), have the customer initial both the rate and the fee, and carry a setup fee as well so one of the two can be waived to win the other.
- Why it works: People focus on the monthly price and rarely consider the annualized cost, so the fee lifts the effective monthly rate (the monthly price plus a twelfth of the fee) without touching conversions while the low advertised price stays for marketing; a second fee gives you something to give away when a customer needs an incentive to act.
- Bad → Good: $39 a month alone → $39 a month plus a $99 annual fee, an effective $47 a month and a 20% revenue lift; a fee of 0.5x the monthly rate adds 4.15% of revenue, 1x adds 8.3%, 2x adds 16.6%, 3x adds 24.9%.
- When not to apply: If you can bill annually, do that instead (13.2); if the customer balks at the fee, drop it, and they lose the benefit it was attached to.
- Anchor: «Step #1: Pick a renewal fee that’s 1-3x your monthly rate. This adds a big increase to» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Pricing Play #7: Annual Renewal Fee On Top of Monthly

#### 13.12. Pricing Play #8: Automatic Continuity
- **Rule:** For every front-end product or service, build a stripped-down, near-zero-work, high-margin version of it, price it at 5–20% of the main price, and attach it to the purchase so it starts automatically when the front-end term ends; for one-time sales, fix a date on the back end after which the continuity starts.
- Why it works: Against the main purchase the continuity price seems small, and sunk cost does the rest: they already spent that much, so they might as well pay a little to keep it; it also builds a pool of low-ticket customers you can remarket to and ascend instead of losing them.
- Bad → Good: In the author's worked example the continuity adds 32% to LTV and $1800 of profit per customer.
- When not to apply: Don't be a sneak: this is not undisclosed or forced continuity, so the buyer agrees to it up front and is told clearly what happens after the time period; continuity discounts are sheet 12.
- Anchor: «Whatever you sell, you create the most paired down, zero work version of your thing.» — $100M Playbook: Pricing (2025), Pricing Play #8: Automatic Continuity

## Raising prices

#### 13.13. Don't grandfather existing customers. Don't do lifetime deals.
- **Rule:** When your value goes up, raise the price for existing customers too: never lock a customer into an old price by grandfathering, and never sell lifetime access for a one-time price.
- Why it works: Value depends on price, and a locked price limits your options because you never know what you will want to deliver in the future; forever lasts a lot longer than what they paid once, so you end up with upset customers when the money has run out and the commitment remains.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Selling lifetime access for a single payment is acceptable only if fulfilment truly costs zero dollars; in theory it can work where lifetime LTV beats what you normally make, but no large company sells lifetime access for a single payment, so model success, not failure. For lifetime discounts and price protection, see sheet 12.
- Anchor: «If your value goes up, so should your price. Never lock your customers into a price if» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), My Rules For Raising Prices

#### 13.14. Test raises on new customers first
- **Rule:** Run a raise in this order: decide the increase; put incoming customers on the new price immediately and treat them as the test; only once they buy and stay at rates that make you more money, with no churn spike and no collapse in sales, segment out the customers who came before the raise and send them the RAISE letter (13.17), signed in ink if possible, personally if it goes by email.
- Why it works: New customers have no context on the old price, so there is no point delaying; the test shows whether the new price actually makes more money, gives you confidence when you present it, and shows the existing base that the marketplace has already agreed with the new value.
- Bad → Good: A prospect who heard a cheaper number from a friend grumbles about the price → the sales team answers that it feels like bad news but is good news: the value keeps improving, 96% of existing customers agreed to the new price and they are the ones already using it, and this is the cheapest it will ever be; people grumble and buy anyway. (example — the Price Raise playbook, What To Do About Incoming Customers)
- When not to apply: The softening steps, the loyalty discount and the stair step, are for the existing base only; new customers get none of them.
- Anchor: «Test price raises on new customers before rolling out to your entire customer base .» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), My Rules For Raising Prices

#### 13.15. Meet customers one on one on a raise of 50% or more
- **Rule:** When a raise is 50% or more, typically to fix an early pricing mistake such as a flat price whose cost to fulfil grows with the customer's success, talk to customers individually on top of the letter.
- Why it works: A raise that large usually follows a big mistake made early on, so the customers need a conversation rather than only an announcement.
- Bad → Good: A Shopify competitor charged $30 a month while hundreds of its customers made over $1,000,000 a year on the software, so the biggest customers cost the most to fulfil and lost it the most money, and the choice was to fire them or reprice them → the CEO phoned all 300, took prices from $30 to $3,000 on average, and lost one.
- When not to apply: Only where you can manage the call volume.
- Anchor: «Meet with people if you raise prices more than 50% . If you raise prices *a lot* - which» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), My Rules For Raising Prices

#### 13.16. Time a raise with a launch
- **Rule:** When a new product or promotion is launching, time the price raise inside the same 90 days and frame it as early adopter pricing.
- Why it works: The raise drafts off the momentum of the launch.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Only when a new product or promotion is launching; the author makes the pairing conditional on being able to do it.
- Anchor: «If you can, pair a price raise within 90 days of a launch . Every business has new prod-» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), My Rules For Raising Prices

## The RAISE letter

#### 13.17. RAISE (The Perfect Price Raise Letter)
- **Rule:** Write the raise to existing customers as a letter in five sections, in this order, the concise version the author built with Patrick Campbell of Profitwell: R, remind them of the value they have already had, personalized and quantified (calls attended, deliverables used, revenue generated); A, address the price change directly, in one sentence and without preamble; I, invest in their future (13.18); S, soften the news with a loyalty discount (13.19); E, explain away their concerns (13.20).
- Why it works: The value reminder makes the letter about them rather than you; the direct statement rips off the bandaid while the rest of the letter softens the blow; the investments keep you from looking greedy and the vanishing discount is easier to accept than a raise; Patrick Campbell oversaw 100 price increases directly and analyzed over 500.
- Bad → Good: The assembled template: the value recap; one sentence that prices have to rise; the list of investments; the loyal customer kept on the existing plan for three to six months with a thank-you credit, shown on the invoice where possible, then moved to the new price; a PS inviting anyone materially affected to write back, about groceries for B2C, about the business for B2B.
- When not to apply: Section E does not scale by design: be ready to roll up your sleeves and answer customers yourself; where members gather in a community and the platform allows it, announce by video with comments turned off and refer questions to you directly, so complaints do not compound in a public thread.
- Anchor: «My price raise letters have five sections. I use the acronym RAISE to remember it.» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), The Perfect Price Raise Letter

#### 13.18. I: Invest in their future
- **Rule:** In the Invest section, name three of the biggest investments you will make with the profit from the raise, drawn from hiring better people, training the people you have, better equipment, better technology or software, facility upgrades and a higher level of service, and pair each with more good stuff (more aligned with their desired outcome, faster, easier, risk free) or less bad stuff for the customer; name only investments you were already going to make.
- Why it works: Framed as investment rather than greed, the raise shows the customer better bang for their buck, and because the investments were planned for the next 12 months anyway they cost nothing extra.
- Bad → Good: Taking on a new, unplanned expense so the raise looks justified → naming the upgrades already planned for the next 12 months, each framed as value for the customer; the unplanned expense cancels the extra profit the raise was meant to bring.
- When not to apply: Promise only what you will actually do; naming an investment you will not make is lying.
- Anchor: «You want to make this about investments. Basically just tell them the stuff you already» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), Section #3 - I: Invest in their future

#### 13.19. S: Soften the news with a loyalty reward
- **Rule:** Raise the price now and at once give existing customers a loyalty discount or credit that expires in 3 to 6 months, shown on their invoices where possible; when the raise is too big to take in one go, either go all the way up or stair-step it, positioning the discount as falling off in one to three increments, a portion every few months.
- Why it works: A vanishing discount is easier for people to accept than a raised price, and they mind even less when another discount appears later; it reads as credit given rather than a charge added, and a staged fall-off eases customers into the new price.
- Bad → Good: One jump to the new price → a stair step of $200 a month off in months one to four, then $100 a month off, then $50 a month off for the last three months; the checklist version drops the discount at 6-month intervals for 2–3 mini jumps. (example — the Price Raise playbook, What to Do Next)
- When not to apply: New customers pay the full new price from today (13.14); going all the way up at once is the alternative, which worked for Trey when he was not profitable and needed the move.
- Anchor: «and then immediately offer a discount that expires 3 to 6 months from now. Bonus points: if» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), The Perfect Price Raise Letter, Section #4 - S

#### 13.20. E: Explain away their concerns, and the three types of reply
- **Rule:** End the letter with a PS inviting anyone the raise would materially affect to reply, route every reply to the owner, and handle the three types of reply separately: those who see the value comply; those actually affected get their discount extended another six months and a check-in then; those who were going to cancel anyway leave now.
- Why it works: The PS is why far fewer people reply negatively than you expect, and it keeps objections out of public threads and off the churn line; the affected type gives you optionality, a longer discount instead of a lost customer.
- Bad → Good: Answering concerns with arguments about what is scalable → rolling up your sleeves and replying to each customer; a sob story from someone actually affected gets an offer to extend the discount six more months and reach out again then.
- When not to apply: A churn spike in the first month is not by itself a failed raise: read it against months two and three, where churn dips below normal and then returns to baseline, because it is churn pulled forward from people who were leaving anyway and costs one month of revenue from that slice.
- Anchor: «You’ll find there are three types of people:» — $100M Playbook: Price Raise (2025), Section #5 - E: Explain away their concerns

## Lifetime value: the Crazy Eight

#### 13.21. Churn is measured on the original cohort
- **Rule:** Calculate churn as the customers who left during the period divided by the customers you started it with, and leave the period's new signups out of the calculation.
- Why it works: The count of original customers who left is the same whether you signed up zero or 1,000 new clients, so counting new signups hides the real churn.
- Bad → Good: Netting 1,000 new signups against the 5 of the original 100 who left during the month → churn is still 5%.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «it does not affect churn. The same number of original people left. You could» — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (2025), How To Calculate LTV, Step Two

#### 13.22. Cut to the core 2–3 deliverables
- **Rule:** When an ongoing offer churns, cut it to the two or three core things you deliver and make those really good, instead of answering churn with more calls, features and material.
- Why it works: Overwhelm is the number one reason for churn; retention comes down to making sure customers consume the value rather than burying them in it, which is why each version of Gym Launch got shorter and simpler.
- Bad → Good: A newsletter business moved to weekly calls and added more material, and churn rose with everything it added → one Q&A call and one physical newsletter a month, the setup that had its lowest churn.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Sometimes less is more. There is wisdom in deletion. Decide on the core 2-3 things you deliv-» — $100M Playbook: Retention (2025), Price, Value, And Churn, Provide On-Going Value

#### 13.23. The Crazy Eight
- **Rule:** Whenever lifetime value has to go up, run the product or service through all eight levers and write down an action step for each: raise prices (13.6); lower the cost of delivering the thing, circling the top two of nine tactics you could implement (increase the ratio of employees to customers, offshore talent, sell more similar customers to productize delivery, done for you to done with you, cap usage, lifetime to annual, in-person to remote, cut meeting times, buy in bulk and prepay); upsell frequency, quantity and quality (13.25); downsell quantity and quality (13.26); cross-sell a different, complementary thing on top.
- Why it works: If you make more than anyone else from the same customer, you can spend more than anyone else to get them; the eight are small enough chunks to apply to any business, unlike the two-way split into order value and purchase frequency, which the author found too broad to act on.
- Bad → Good: Buy in bulk and prepay: with the cash on hand, prepaying vendor spend locks in 10–20% discounts, a guaranteed return you would take anywhere else. (example — the Lifetime Value playbook, #2 Decrease Costs)
- When not to apply: Costs can only go down to zero while price can go infinitely high, so cutting delivery cost never substitutes for the price lever, which is why pricing comes first; pick a cross-sell that does not dramatically change who you serve or what you do every day.
- Anchor: «smaller chunks so I could apply them to any business. I actually drill my team on this stuff» — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (2025), The Crazy Eight

#### 13.24. My favorite upsell of all time
- **Rule:** When a one-and-done business chooses its back end, make the default offer more of, or more help with, what the customer just bought, with faster results and less risk, effort and hassle, for more money; when the team cannot agree on the back end, put the candidates to the customers and let them choose.
- Why it works: If customers like your stuff, they want to buy more stuff like it, and the business serves the customer, not the other way around; an offer far from the core product is a marketing, branding and sales nightmare, as if you had started another business.
- Bad → Good: The founders of a company acquired in 2020 wanted a back end they were passionate about that had nothing to do with the business → both offers went to the customers in a survey, the vast majority chose the adjacent one, and the upsell built from it 2.2x'd LTV per customer, which allowed 5x the advertising across all channels profitably and took revenue from a few million a month to a few million a week.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «or more help with - what they just bought... but with faster results, less risk, less» — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (2025), Increasing Lifetime Value: The Crazy 8

#### 13.25. Offer the upsell first, then downsell the standard offer
- **Rule:** On the sales call, present the bigger or better version first and then downsell to the standard offer; a quantity upsell comes as bulk, more often or bigger, and a quality upsell as a newer version or a premium one, built by moving a service dimension up a level (speed of response, availability, length of calls, service ratio, done for you, personalization, provider qualifications).
- Why it works: Making the bigger or better version the first thing presented routinely produces 20%+ lifts in cash collected upfront and in LTV overall (2025).
- Bad → Good: Pest control serviced once a month → offer first a prepaid year (12x), service every three weeks (1.33x) or three hours a visit instead of one (3x), then downsell to the standard monthly plan; for a burger, two burgers instead of one or a bigger burger.
- When not to apply: More often does not apply to physical products.
- Anchor: «and begin offering it first on your sales calls. Then, downsell your standard offer. You may see» — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (2025), #5 Sell More (Increase Quantity) and #6 Sell Better (Increase Quality)

#### 13.26. Downsell only the unqualified
- **Rule:** Offer a downsell only to prospects who do not qualify for the main offer, judged by money made per person who comes through the door: a quantity downsell (fewer, less often, smaller) or a quality downsell that reaches the same result with a worse experience, built by reversing the quality upsell list (slower responses, fewer locations and hours, more junior staff, more recorded, done with you or do it yourself, less personalization, a weaker or no guarantee).
- Why it works: If it means this or nothing, this beats nothing, and since you already do or make the stuff it adds little operational drag; you lose money only when someone who would have bought the $5 thing takes the $2.50 thing, so keeping downsells to the unqualified protects the main offer while still collecting the extra cash.
- Bad → Good: Four people through the door, on the phone or at the checkout page, counted before and after a downsell for those who do not qualify → the business makes 50% more per person, even though the extra sales happen at a lower price.
- When not to apply: A prospect who qualifies for the main offer: the author forbids his sales team from selling a qualified person a downsell.
- Anchor: «Downsell people who otherwise don’t qualify for your other offers. In other words, I forbid» — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (2025), #7 Downsell Fewer (Lower Quantity) and #8 Downsell Lower Quality

## The Fast Cash Play

#### 13.27. The Fast Cash Playbook design decisions
- **Rule:** Design a Fast Cash Play, a limited-time, limited-spot, high-touch, big-ticket offer to your warmest audiences, by answering six questions in this order: who to advertise to (13.29), what to sell them (13.30), how many to sell (13.31), how to sell it (a one-on-one consultation for most high price points, 13.33), what to charge (13.32), and how to promote it, for no more than seven days and stopping earlier if the spots sell out.
- Why it works: How you sell changes how you promote, so the promotion cannot be chosen before the earlier answers are fixed; any longer than seven days loses urgency and any shorter misses sales.
- Bad → Good: Gym Launch clients started cancelling because unexpected tax bills had eaten the money they made → a grand opening giveaway idea became a high-ticket offer sized from the clients' own numbers at 5–10 people at $5,000 each, with sales training bundled in because most owners had never sold anything expensive, Sunday training slots cleared to hold the buyers, the materials written that day and taught on a call the next, and recently cancelled clients invited to that call. (example — the Fast Cash playbook, Start Here)
- When not to apply: A Fast Cash Play is neither the nurture of new leads for the main offer nor the long-term value-add content that keeps lists warm; the author keeps the three separate.
- Anchor: «I’ll cover who you advertise your offer to, what you sell, what to charge, how» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), The Fast Cash Playbook, Description

#### 13.28. Run a Fast Cash Play once a quarter
- **Rule:** Run Fast Cash Plays once a quarter, four a year, not weekly and not once a year, each limited in spots and in time.
- Why it works: A quarter cleans the pipeline of qualified but on-the-fence leads, raises the value of every customer (people who buy more often are more likely to keep buying), shows something new is going on, leaves time to deliver and reset, and for a business at break-even or better drops the extra revenue from warm leads straight to the bottom line; the limits are what let you repeat it without fatiguing the warm audience.
- Bad → Good: A gym with 166 members at $150 a month, $25,000 a month on 25% margins and $75,000 a year of net income, runs four plays: 10 buyers at $6,000, 13 at $5,000, 8 at $6,000 and 14 at $4,000, $229,000 of extra cash that takes revenue from $300,000 to $529,000 and net income from $75,000 to $281,000 and adds 22 customers (2025). (example — the Fast Cash playbook, ROI Benchmarks)
- When not to apply: If you need longer than twelve weeks to cool off between promotions, run it twice a year instead; the author warns that run incorrectly it makes you seem like a shill.
- Anchor: «I’ve found that once a quarter is the sweet spot for a few reasons:» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), 5 Reasons To Run Fast Cash Offers Every 90 Days

#### 13.29. Advertise only to warm audiences
- **Rule:** Send the Fast Cash offer only to existing customers, previous customers in good standing and engaged leads, and to no one else.
- Why it works: These groups are the most likely to buy and the cheapest to reach, often free, which shortens both letting them know and getting them to buy; all of them have given explicit permission to be contacted about what you sell.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Leads you have sent nothing for six months or more are cooled off: exclude them from this play and start warming them up for the next one.
- Anchor: «You advertise your Fast Cash offer to your existing customers, pre-» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), The Fast Cash Playbook, Description: Who to advertise to

#### 13.30. One to three core components and three bonuses
- **Rule:** Build the Fast Cash offer from one to three core components plus three bonuses, drawn from the unscalable value categories (attention, personalization, convenience, status, duration, speed, experience, access, network, secrets), keep the unscalable parts in at a very high price instead of cutting them, reveal the bonuses one at a time during the promotion, and give every bonus to every buyer whenever they bought.
- Why it works: The unscalable things are what justify the crazy high price tag, and each new bonus is new information that widens the price-to-value gap and pushes more people over the edge; for someone who bought early, the later bonuses confirm they made the right choice.
- Bad → Good: Fitness at $10,000: the core is a one-on-one, year-long "Total Transformation" package of tailored supplements, meals, workouts and a photoshoot; bonus 1 is a nutrition plan designed for a year plus an introduction to a meal prep company that delivers food to that plan; bonus 2, workouts with you one on one three times per week; bonus 3, 24/7 personal cell phone access. (example — the Fast Cash playbook, Ultra-Premium Examples)
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «You will want to have one to three core components of your offer, and three bonuses.» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), The Fast Cash Playbook, Description

#### 13.31. Cap the spots at five to ten percent of customers
- **Rule:** Cap the spots at a number certain to sell out, about five to ten percent of your customers, sized on 10x The 10%: ten percent of your customers paying ten times the price doubles your revenue.
- Why it works: Selling out fast makes the next Fast Cash offer more compelling, so it sells out even faster; the customers are already paid for, so the extra revenue minus delivery drops straight to the bottom line.
- Bad → Good: The Recurring Max Cash Offer: priced at the monthly price times twelve as "1 Year to [ULTIMATE RESULT]", with three bonuses (custom work on what they struggle with, something that makes it faster, something that makes success virtually guaranteed without being a guarantee), sold to the existing base as extra value for prepaying the year, it typically needs little else for 10% of the base to buy. (example — the Fast Cash playbook, Ultra-Premium Examples)
- When not to apply: With a thousand customers or more, fulfilment constrains you well before the 5–10% window, which is fine; with only a handful of customers, appeal to all of them privately.
- Anchor: «At first, I suggest capping it to a number where you will absolutely sell out– maybe five to ten» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), The Fast Cash Playbook, Description: How many to sell

#### 13.32. Price at 10x to 50x your average transaction
- **Rule:** Price the Fast Cash offer at ten to fifty times your current average cart value or transaction size, and run the play at a gross margin of at least 90%.
- Why it works: The price takes the offer beyond comparison with your other products and selects for people with the purchasing power to buy it; ten percent of a large number still leaves plenty of cash to spoil the customer with, and the point is to make money (2025).
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: You will work more for these customers and earn that money, so the play suits owners willing to deliver unscalable work, priced high enough to be happy providing it; you can also price for volume, as long as you scale the features and bonuses to match.
- Anchor: «add a zero. And if you want to get frisky, multiply it by five. So yes, think 10x to 50x your» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), The Fast Cash Playbook, Description: What to charge

#### 13.33. PUSH TO CONSULT SEQUENCE and PUSH TO AUTOMATED CHECKOUT SEQUENCE
- **Rule:** Choose the promotion by how you sell. By consultation, make the offer buyable from day one and spread appointments inside the seven days: 7 days out a teaser that something big comes by email tomorrow; 6 days out the announcement with the core components and a booking link; 5 days out a recap and the first bonus; 3 days out a sales update (sold, left, first come first serve); 2 days out the spots left and the second bonus; 24 hours out a warning with the best bonus; 12 hours out sold out. By automated checkout, build tension and then open the cart to everyone at once: 5 days out a primer text; 4 days out the announcement; 2 days out the cart opens in 48 hours; 24 hours out the first bonus and a set-an-alarm reminder; 60 minutes out a text to have the payment method ready; at cart open the best bonus, the one with the most immediate benefit; 60 minutes after a sales update; 6 hours after the spots left and a bonus not yet mentioned; 12 hours after sold out. Without the tech but with cards on file, let buyers reply that they are in and charge the card with their permission or call them at once.
- Why it works: Without enough competent salespeople you cannot smash all the consultations together, so the offer is buyable at once while the consults are spread out; with a whole-list blast and an automated checkout, the built-up tension causes a stampede when the cart opens, first come first serve.
- Bad → Good: The announcement email, as a skeleton: a short promise to get them to their goal faster, easier and cheaper; a declared selfish reason, an investment the sender was going to make anyway, with three benefits of it for the customer; the deal framed as thanks for their support, capped at ten customers, with a named expiry day and hour; what the first ten get; the exchange asked for, prepaying the year; two ways to act, a booking link or a reply authorising the charge; a PS repeating the cap and the deadline, whichever comes first. (example — the Fast Cash playbook, Sample Email And Text Sequence)
- When not to apply: Each schedule fits only its sales method; after the sell-out the author prefers to waitlist the people who already tried to buy, with permission to charge them if a spot opens up, and to announce one spot left if someone backs out.
- Anchor: «7 DAYS OUT - Let them know something big is coming via email tomorrow. I prefer» — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash (2025), PUSH TO CONSULT SEQUENCE

#### 13.34. A countdown and a new bonus in every message
- **Rule:** Give every message of the sequence a countdown and one new bonus, sold on the good it brings and the bad it removes; hold the best bonus, worth more than the whole price, for the last push of a consult sequence (in a checkout sequence it lands at cart open, 13.33); and if the offer has not sold out or come close by day three or four, make it more compelling before sending the rest of the sequence.
- Why it works: The best bonus is what sells out the remaining spots, and a well-built offer is sold out by the third or fourth day.
- Bad → Good: The last-day email names the objection that people will wait for the next one and answers it in three beats (what if there is no next one, what if not acting now costs you the next step, maybe the habit of waiting is why you are not where you want to be), contrasts what people spend on phones and cars with what they spend on their goal, restates the hard expiry hour, and adds one more bonus for the last buyers before listing the whole stack again. (example — the Fast Cash playbook, Sample Email And Text Sequence)
