# Leads and the Core Four: getting engaged leads through your own outreach and content

Open this sheet when someone needs more engaged leads from their own work: choosing among the Core Four, building a lead magnet, running warm outreach, posting free content, running cold outreach, and checking the results against the $100M Leads benchmarks (2023).
Diagnosing a lead shortage as a skill or volume problem is sheet 01. The Value Equation and the core offer are sheet 02. Scarcity, urgency and the other enhancers are sheet 03.
Scaling a channel, picking the next channel after warm outreach, agencies and affiliates are sheet 05. Paid ads are sheet 06, and nurture after the opt-in is sheet 08.

## Leads and lead magnets

#### 04.1. The Core Four, counted in engaged leads
- **Rule:** When lead flow has to rise, count results in engaged leads — people who show interest in what you sell (leave contact information on a website, follow you where you can contact them, reply to a campaign) — not in leads, who are only people you can contact, and get them through the Core Four, the only four ways to let anyone know about anything, found by crossing audience (warm: gave you permission to contact them; cold: did not) with communication (one-to-one, private; one-to-many, public): warm outreach (1-to-1 warm), posting content (1-to-many warm), cold outreach (1-to-1 cold), paid ads (1-to-many cold).
- Why it works: These are the only four things you can do to let people know about your stuff, so too few engaged leads means the Core Four are not being done with enough skill or enough volume (see sheet 01); the methods compound, and posting content grows the warm audience that warm reach outs draw on, so a new method is added to warm reach outs, never swapped for them.
- Bad → Good: Emailing a 10,000-person list once and filing it as one-to-many because a machine sent it → it is one-to-one done really fast by a machine: automation only means machines do some of the work, and the nature of the communication stays the same; a bought email list, the numbers in your phone or people on the street reported as results → those are leads, and only the people who reply to your campaign are engaged leads (example — $100M Leads, Leads Alone Aren't Enough).
- When not to apply: You only need one of the four to get engaged leads; paid ads are sheet 06, and which method to add after warm outreach is sheet 05.
- Anchor: «the only four ways we can let anyone know about anything: the core four.» — $100M Leads (2023), Section III: Get Leads

#### 04.2. Seven Steps To Creating an Effective Lead Magnet
- **Rule:** When people want to know more before they buy, build the lead magnet in seven steps, in this order: 1) figure out the problem you want to solve and who to solve it for; 2) figure out how to solve it; 3) figure out how to deliver it; 4) test what to name it; 5) make it easy to consume; 6) make it darn good; 7) make it easy for them to tell you they want more.
- Why it works: Good lead magnets get more engaged leads and customers than a core offer alone, and they do it for less money.
- Bad → Good: Eight Sundays building a webinar plus $150 a day of ads → 80 leads and 0 sales in three days, then shut down; a plain 13-minute screen share of a real gym launch (the ad account of a newly launched gym, the ads, the spend, the page and offer, the leads, appointments, shows and sales, what the owner made, then the offer: the whole thing done free, paid only off the sales, book a call if that sounds fair; no slides, no presenting) behind the headline "FREE Case Study: How we added 213 members and $112,000 in revenue to a small gym in San Diego" → strangers booked the calendar solid for a week; they did not want the webinar, they wanted the case study.
- When not to apply: Try advertising the core offer first — it goes straight for the sale and may be enough on its own to get leads engaging; a lead magnet is for when people want to know more before they buy, which is common for businesses that sell more expensive stuff.
- Anchor: «Seven Steps To Creating an Effective Lead Magnet» — $100M Leads (2023), Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets

#### 04.3. The Problem-Solution cycle
- **Rule:** In step 1, pick a narrow, meaningful problem, make the lead magnet a complete solution to it, and check that the new problem this solution reveals is the one your core offer solves in exchange for money; reject a candidate whose solution is broad or whose next problem your core offer does not solve.
- Why it works: Every problem has a solution and every solution reveals more problems, so the free solution hands the lead straight to the paid one, while a broad solution leaves nothing for the core offer to be paid for; and a person who pays with their time on a free or low-cost offer now is more likely to pay with money for a related higher-cost offer later.
- Bad → Good: "Help homeowners sell their homes" is a broad solution → pick one of the narrow problems in front of it (what the house is worth, how to increase its value, pictures, cleaning, landscaping, minor repairs, moving services, staging) and solve it free; that makes the remaining problem obvious, they still have to sell the home, and with trust earned you charge to solve it with the core offer.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «figure this out. I call it the Problem-Solution cycle. You can see it» — $100M Leads (2023), Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets

#### 04.4. The three types of lead magnets
- **Rule:** In step 2, solve the narrow problem with one of three types, chosen by the core offer: Reveal Their Problem ("diagnosis") when the problem gets worse the longer they wait; Samples And Trials (full but brief access to the core offer) when the core offer is a recurring solution to a recurring problem; One Step Of A Multi-Step Process (one valuable step free, the rest when they buy) when the core offer solves a more complex problem.
- Why it works: All three solve one problem and reveal others, which is what moves the lead toward the core offer.
- Bad → Good: Reveal Their Problem — a termite inspection that shows what the bugs do: with termites you remove them for less than the cost of another home, without them you sell prevention, so you sell either way; Samples And Trials — a free adjustment that gives relief, with permanent benefits only if they buy more, or a consumable sample the way Costco sells food; One Step — one free coat of garage-door sealant out of the three the job needs, explained as partial coverage, with the other two offered as a bundle.
- When not to apply: One Step Of A Multi-Step Process needs a core offer that has steps; a diagnosis relies on the revealed problem getting worse with delay, which is what creates its own pressure to move.
- Anchor: «So your three types are: 1) Reveal Problems, 2) Samples and Trials, and» — $100M Leads (2023), Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets

#### 04.5. Test the headline, then the image, then the subheadline
- **Rule:** In step 4, test the lead magnet's packaging in this order — the headline, then the image(s), then the subheadline — and if you test only one thing, test the headline; keep a version when people engage in droves, and count it a mega winner when they also ask when they can get their hands on it.
- Why it works: Five times more people read the headline than any other part of the promotion, and improving the headline, name and display of a lead magnet can 2x, 3x or 10x engagement; if no one shows interest in it, no one will ever know how good it is.
- Bad → Good: Head-to-head polls on his own following for the title of $100M Leads: Leads beat Advertising (which had beaten Promotion) and Marketing, a real image beat a cartoon, and "How to get strangers to want to buy your stuff" won all four subheadline rounds — against "Get strangers to want to buy your stuff" the difference was the two words "how to", and one round was won by deleting the single word "more".
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «and the subheadline, in that order. The headline is the most important.» — $100M Leads (2023), Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets

#### 04.6. Make it darn good: more value than the core offer
- **Rule:** Make the lead magnet provide more value than the price of the core offer before anything is bought, and hold all free material — lead magnets and free content alike — to the standard of the paid product: give away the secrets, sell the implementation.
- Why it works: People buy on how much value they think they will get after they buy, and giving value before they buy is the easiest way to make them think it; 99% of people won't buy, but they create or destroy your reputation on the value of your free stuff; and paying customers consume your free content and count it in the return they got, so weak free content makes them like the paid product less.
- Bad → Good: Holding back the secrets and giving away sucky fluff → people who might have become customers conclude you suck, buy from someone else and tell others not to buy from you; give away the secrets and sell the implementation instead.
- When not to apply: A lead magnet that costs money to deliver stays only if it lowers the cost of getting a new customer: in the author's example a core offer advertised directly cost $3000 of advertising per customer, and a $25 lead magnet cut that to $1000 and tripled the business on the same budget (2023).
- Anchor: «magnet to provide so much value people feel obligated to pay you. The» — $100M Leads (2023), Engage Your Leads, Step 6: Make it darn good

#### 04.7. A reason why, and ethical scarcity
- **Rule:** In step 7, give every call to action a reason to act now; when the reason is scarcity, take it from the business's real limit — customer service, onboarding, inventory, time slots per week — and advertise it openly instead of inventing a number; when no real scarcity or urgency is available, a made-up reason (the Fraternity Party Planner) still beats giving none.
- Why it works: The fewer you have, the more valuable people think it is, and the less time they have, the faster they act; any reason tends to work better than none — in a Harvard experiment more people let someone cut in line when simply given a reason, and more still when the reason made sense; a real limit advertised rather than hidden is what makes the scarcity ethical.
- Bad → Good: A call to action with no reason → saying five people a week is all you can handle, so anyone who wants it solved soon should act now; "The most convenient class times fill up fast. Call now to get the one you want."; a July 4th promotion ending Monday at midnight; or, made up, "Because... it's my birthday, and I want you to celebrate with me."
- When not to apply: Scarcity trades lead volume for perceived value — the scarcer it is, the fewer engaged leads you can collect before it runs out — so it cannot maximise both; scarcity and urgency as offer enhancers are sheet 03.
- Anchor: «per week, etc. Don't keep it a secret - advertise it. This gives you» — $100M Leads (2023), Engage Your Leads, Step 7

## Warm outreach

#### 04.8. How To Do Warm Reach Outs in 10 Steps
- **Rule:** To get the first five clients for any new product or service, run warm outreach — one-to-one contact with the people who know you — in ten steps, in order: 1) Get your list (everyone who has given you permission to contact them); 2) Pick a platform; 3) Personalize your message; 4) Reach out; 5) Warm them up; 6) Invite their friends; 7) Make them the easiest offer in the world; 8) Start at the top; 9) Start Charging; 10) Keep Your List Warm.
- Why it works: It is the cheapest and easiest way to find people interested in what you sell, and because everything is done by hand and personally it is reliable, even though you don't get many engaged leads for your time.
- Bad → Good: The first clients of The Free Training Project: he asked people he already knew, by call, text and Facebook, whether anyone wanted to get into shape — twelve weeks of free training with a custom nutrition plan and grocery list, for a donation to a charity of their choice and a testimonial; six said yes (two high school friends, one college friend, three people they referred) and paid him after the twelve weeks without minding, their referrals (another five or six clients) were charged directly, and the business settled at about $4000 a month and replaced his job income.
- When not to apply: Warm reach outs have two limits — the time they take and the number of people who know you, which you eventually run out of; it is not the channel you scale on, and past the first clients advanced operators use it for re-engagement and new product lines (the next channel is sheet 05).
- Anchor: «How To Do Warm Reach Outs in 10 Steps» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach

#### 04.9. One hundred reach outs a day, three attempts each
- **Rule:** Send 100 personalized warm reach outs per day, contacting each person up to three times — once per day for three days, or until they respond, whichever comes first — and when starting out, give getting new customers four hours minimum a day, first thing after getting up, without stopping until the goal is reached.
- Why it works: Volume is what makes warm outreach reliable — with enough of it you will get a customer, and the numbers improve the more you do; time is its first limitation, so getting customers has to take the majority of your day while you are starting out; and you learn more in the first ten days of 100 reach outs than from everything you have ever read or watched.
- Bad → Good: A list of 1000 contacts is ten solid days at 100 a day, about a month with follow-ups, after which five or more people will have accepted the free offer (2023).
- When not to apply: With physical mail the three attempts go out once per week, not once per day.
- Anchor: «[Now, reach out to 100 of them per day with your personalized messages.» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach, Step 4

#### 04.10. The A-C-A framework, then the offer
- **Rule:** When a contact replies, answer with the A-C-A framework — Acknowledge what they said by restating it in your own words, Compliment them and tie it to a positive character trait, Ask another question that leads toward your offer — and make the offer only after a normal amount of conversation: 3–4 exchanges by phone or message, 3–4 minutes in person.
- Why it works: People love talking about themselves and love being complimented, and if they feel good talking to you they like and trust you more; the exchanges let you learn about the person and steer to a topic closer to your offer before you test whether they are interested.
- Bad → Good: A mother of two who is an accountant: acknowledge ("Two kids. And you're an accountant..."), compliment the trait (supermom, so hardworking, managing a full-time career and two kids), then ask what fits your offer — "Do you get time for yourself?" for therapy or life coaching, "Do you have time to get workouts in?" for fitness, "Do you have anyone who helps you keep the house tidy?" for cleaning.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «[ACA]{.calibre11}[ framework is great because it helps» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach

#### 04.11. The value equation in an outreach offer
- **Rule:** When you make an offer from scratch in a warm reach out or an ask to a content audience, build it on the value equation (the construct itself is sheet 02) — maximize Dream Outcome (the best results, ideally from people like the one you're talking to) and Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (results, reviews, third-party validation, guarantees), minimize Time Delay (how fast results start) and Effort and Sacrifice (the good stuff they keep doing, the bad stuff they avoid); when people refuse it even free, find which element failed — they don't want it, they don't believe you, or the hidden costs of time, effort and sacrifice are too high — and ask "What would I have to do to make it worth it for you to continue?".
- Why it works: It shows someone you have exactly what they want, that they are guaranteed to get it, insanely fast, without lifting a finger or giving up anything they love; the most expensive part of what you sell is not the price but the hidden costs at the bottom of the equation, so free stuff can still be too expensive.
- Bad → Good: The ask to an audience built on it: looking for five of a specific avatar to reach the dream outcome within a time delay, the best part being that they don't have to (effort and sacrifice), and if they don't get the dream outcome, two things — their money back and your work with them until they do — closed with "If that sounds fair, DM me/book a call", then back to providing value (example — $100M Leads, Post Free Content Part II).
- When not to apply: The ideal cannot be reached in full: get as close to it as you can without lying or exaggerating.
- Anchor: «When I make an offer from scratch, I refer to the value equation.» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach

#### 04.12. Ask who they know, not whether they'll buy
- **Rule:** Make the warm offer by asking whether they know anybody who is (struggle) looking to (dream outcome) in (time delay), not whether they want to buy; when there is little time or space — emails, texts, direct messages, calls, in person — put the value elements back to back: I help (ideal customer) get (dream outcome) in (time period) without (effort and sacrifice) and (something that raises perceived likelihood of achievement).
- Why it works: Most of the people who say yes turn out to be interested themselves; since you didn't ask anyone to buy you don't come off as pushy, and you win whether they show interest, refer someone, or both.
- Bad → Good: The script's skeleton, in order: ask whether they know anybody who fits; say you are taking on five case studies free because that is all you can handle and you want testimonials; state the dream outcome without the effort and sacrifice; add that you work with people until they get it; give two named client results, one of them sharing the contact's own struggle; close "Does anyone you like come to mind?" and, on a no, joke "does anyone you hate come to mind?" to break the awkwardness.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «to buy anything. We're asking if they know» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach, Step 6

#### 04.13. Free for the first five, charge once they refer, raise every five
- **Rule:** Each time you launch a new product or service, including inside an established business, give it free to the first five people on three promises — they use it, give feedback, and leave a review if they think it deserves one; start charging once people start referring others to you, then raise the price 20% every five customers (80% off for the next five, then 60% off, then 40% off) until you find your sweet spot.
- Why it works: Free gets you reps and comfort making offers while you probably suck, and people are far more forgiving when they haven't paid; people who get value, especially free, leave testimonials, give feedback and send friends and family, and free customers convert into, refer and bring paying ones; referrals are the litmus test for being good enough to charge, an observable event instead of a guess; and prices that really go up every five add urgency.
- Bad → Good: The free offer as he words it: he is only taking on five people, so he can give each all the attention needed for brag-worthy results, and gives it all free as long as they promise to use it, give feedback and leave a killer review if it deserves one — "Does that sound fair?"
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source; this is the warm-outreach cadence of $100M Leads (2023), and the cadence for a new offer in Money Models and the Lifetime Value playbook, about 20% every ten sales, is 10.36 and 13.6.
- Anchor: «[My recommendation - whenever you launch a new product or service -» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach, Step 7

#### 04.14. The 9-word email
- **Rule:** Once you have given a warm list value for a while, or want to see who wants value, send Dean Jackson's 9-word email — "Are you still looking to [4 word desire]?" and nothing else: no images, no frills, no links — and put the replies at the top of your warm reach outs.
- Why it works: The replies are the engaged leads — the bare question surfaces who is ready with nothing else in the message; the author calls the template money for getting leads to engage and uses it among the first things he does when he invests in a new business.
- Bad → Good: Filled in: "Are you still looking to buy your dream home?", "...to get more sales leads?", "...to tone up your arms?", "...to open an online store?", "...to start a YouTube channel?".
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «[No images. No frills. No links. Just a question. Nothing else. This» — $100M Leads (2023), #1 Warm Outreach, Step 10

#### 04.15. Warm outreach benchmarks
- **Rule:** Judge warm outreach against the $100M Leads benchmarks (2023): about one in five contacts engages, about one in five of those takes the free offer, and about one in four of those converts to a paid offer — roughly one customer per 100 reach outs — and plan the volume from that ratio.
- Why it works: The chain predicts how many customers you get per 100 reach outs, so with enough volume you will get a customer, and the numbers improve as you do more.
- Bad → Good: Assuming 1% of the list buys a $400 offer, 500 reach outs a week is 5 customers a week, $2000 a week and $104,000 a year from warm reach outs alone — about twice the median US household income at the time of writing (2023).
- When not to apply: The numbers vary with the value of your offer and how much people trust you.

## Free content

#### 04.16. The content unit: hook, retain, reward
- **Rule:** Build every piece of content, on any platform and at any length, from content units — the smallest amount of material that hooks attention (they notice it), retains it (they consume it) and rewards it (it satisfies the reason they consumed it to begin with) — check that each unit does all three, and make longer content by linking units together.
- Why it works: All audience-growing content rewards the people consuming it, and a person can only be rewarded if they have a reason to consume it, pay attention long enough, and get that reason satisfied; reversed, those three outcomes are the three things you have to do.
- Bad → Good: A Far Past topic — the story without the scar — as one unit — hook: he complained to a friend that he didn't have enough time while glued to his phone; retain: the friend yanked the phone away and read the usage, three hours a day on social media; reward: "Hey, I found you some time."
- When not to apply: The three are separated to discuss them clearly but can all happen at once — a unit can be as little as an image, a meme or a sentence, which is how a short tweet, a meme image or a jingle goes viral.
- Anchor: «The smallest amount of material it takes to hook, retain and reward» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part I

#### 04.17. Hook: format for the platform, then the headline components
- **Rule:** To hook, format the piece to match the platform first, using the best-performing content on that platform in your market as the guide, then tweak it to hook your ideal audience, and give the headline at least two of the seven components: Recency (as new as possible), Relevancy (personally meaningful), Celebrity (prominent people, authorities), Proximity (close to home geographically), Conflict (opposing ideas, people, nature), Unusual (odd, unique, rare, bizarre), Ongoing (a story still in progress).
- Why it works: People consume content because it is similar to stuff they have liked before, otherwise better-looking content hooks them before yours has a chance; and the headline is what the audience uses to weigh whether your content will reward them — a meta-analysis of news found these components drove the most interest.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The action step of the same section relaxes the minimum to one or more of the components.
- Anchor: «A meta-analysis of news revealed headline components that drove the» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part I

#### 04.18. Retain: lists, steps, and stories
- **Rule:** Retain attention by embedding unresolved questions in the audience's mind through lists (things, facts, tips, opinions, ideas one after another), steps (actions in order that accomplish a goal when completed) or stories (events, real or imaginary), alone or interwoven.
- Why it works: Curiosity is the author's favourite driver of retention: people want to know what happens next, and done correctly they will wait years to find out.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Steps must be done in a specific order to get a result, so they are less flexible but give a more explicit reward; lists can hold anything in any order, so they are more flexible but give a less explicit reward.
- Anchor: «favorite ways to embed questions are: lists, steps, and» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part I

#### 04.19. Reward: value per second, judged by audience growth
- **Rule:** Judge content by value per second — how often it rewards the audience in the time it takes to consume it — never by its length, fix what is boring instead of cutting for length, and count it as rewarding only when the audience is growing, reading that growth against a posting cadence and an ask cadence picked for the platform and held constant.
- Why it works: There is no such thing as too long, only too boring: people don't have shorter attention spans, they have higher standards; the audience decides whether you matched or beat the expectation they consumed it with; and since we can only control inputs, outputs mean something only when the inputs are consistent.
- Bad → Good: A piece cut down for being "too long" → the same person who gets bored three seconds into a ten-second video will binge a 900-page book or eight hours of television, so the fix is more reward per second, not fewer seconds.
- When not to apply: Growth comes slowly at first — the author released a podcast twice weekly for four years before it was picked up on the Top 100 list, and it became a Top 10 US business podcast only in its fifth year — so judge it over years, not months.
- Anchor: «to share it? ]{.calibre3}[How good your content is depends on how often» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part I, Reward

#### 04.20. Volume, not a secret; keep repeating yourself
- **Rule:** Grow an audience by putting out more content, not by hunting for a blueprint or secret — on one platform up to about ten short-form posts a day, or long form up to five days a week, before adding the next platform — and keep repeating your message long past the point where you are bored of it.
- Why it works: Anyone telling you there's a secret is trying to sell you something, and audiences compound faster the more you do; and we need to be reminded more than we need to be taught — though the author posts about his book every day, one in five who saw that post still didn't know he had one.
- Bad → Good: Asking an influencer for the blueprint → he went platform by platform instead (on Instagram, one post that day against his three; on LinkedIn, one post that week against his five that day) and concluded "You just gotta do more bro"; over the next six months ten times the content added 1.2M people, growing the audience ten times as fast.
- When not to apply: Ten short-form posts a day is an upper figure the author says you may sometimes reach, not a floor; scaling past one platform is sheet 05.
- Anchor: «Bro, anyone telling you there's some secret is trying to sell» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part I

#### 04.21. Puddles, Ponds, Lakes, Oceans, and "How I"
- **Rule:** When starting out, narrow your content — for a small local business, to what you do and the place you do it — then widen it over time, and make claims from your own experience ("How I", "my favorite ways") rather than instructions ("How to", "the best way").
- Why it works: On broad topics the audience listens to people with better track records than you, while on a narrow one you can become king of that puddle and expand to the pond, the lake and eventually the ocean; no one can question your experience, which makes you bulletproof, while telling a stranger what to do is hard to do without coming off preachy or arrogant.
- Bad → Good: A local plumber making general business content → plumbing in one particular town, then the general local business pond, the lake of brick-and-mortar chains, the ocean of general business; "How To Build a 7-Figure Agency" → "How I Built My 7-Figure Agency"; "you should make your oatmeal this way" → "I make my oatmeal this way".
- When not to apply: Both are lessons for the start: "How I" matters especially when starting out, and the narrow puddle is for a small local business at first, widened over time.
- Anchor: «]{.calibre11}[Narrow the focus of your content. If you have a small» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part II, 7 Lessons

#### 04.22. Avoid pre-scheduling posts
- **Rule:** Publish posts by having a person press submit, not by pre-scheduling them.
- Why it works: Manually posted content performs better for the author: knowing you will be rewarded or punished within seconds is a close feedback loop that makes you try that much harder to make it better.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The mechanism is the author's own theory, offered with "try it", not a measured finding.
- Anchor: «Posts]{.calibre11}[. The posts I manually post perform better than ones» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part II, 7 Lessons

#### 04.23. Give far more than you ask
- **Rule:** To grow an audience, keep the give : ask ratio far above the mature-platform minimum — roughly 3.5:1 on television and about 4 posts per ad on Facebook (2023) — and after every ask get back to providing value.
- Why it works: You deposit goodwill with rewarding content and withdraw it with offers; mature platforms give less and ask more because they care about monetizing rather than growing, while the more you reward your audience, the bigger it gets.
- Bad → Good: Modelling television (47 minutes of giving to 13 of asking) or Facebook's four posts per ad, the pattern for maximally monetizing an audience without shrinking it → modelling growing platforms, which show lots of content with almost no advertisements.
- When not to apply: Your very first post may carry an ask, since you have probably been providing value to people for a while; if it brings no engaged lead, give for a while before asking again.
- Anchor: «the bigger it gets. So if you want to grow an audience, give far far» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part II, Mastering the Give : Ask ratio

#### 04.24. Integrated and intermittent offers
- **Rule:** When you do ask inside content, choose by platform — on short-form platforms intermittent (many pure give pieces, then an occasional ask piece, e.g., ten give posts and a promotion on the eleventh), on long-form platforms integrated (an ask in every piece while the give : ask ratio stays high) — and place each ask after a valuable moment or at the end of the piece, one placement at a time, adding a second only after checking that audience growth has not slowed.
- Why it works: Asks are commercials that cost you trust and growth, so the method has to keep the ratio high enough for the audience to keep growing; it is a balancing act — don't kill your golden goose.
- Bad → Good: A friend whose podcast blew up started asking too frequently inside the content, and it stopped growing and then shrank → an hour-long podcast with three 30-second ads is 58.5 minutes of giving to 1.5 minutes of asking, and the audience keeps growing while the asks run.
- When not to apply: The author's own recommendation is to give until they ask — give in public, ask in private, let the audience self-select — because the moment you start asking for money is the moment you decide to slow your growth; asking is the concession for when you need money now.
- Anchor: «Now, I use two strategies to weave promotions into content:» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part II

#### 04.25. Ask what you stopped doing
- **Rule:** When a channel's results decline, first ask what you stopped doing in the months before the decline, before debating the creative, the copy, the offer, the pages, the sales process or the price.
- Why it works: The paid ads had been given all the credit while free content was nurturing the demand; free content also warms the people moving to and from the cold methods, so even where it is hard to measure it improves the return on every other advertising method.
- Bad → Good: With paid advertising declining, the department meeting debated creative, copy, offer, pages, sales process and price → Leila asked what they had stopped doing, and the unanimous answer was that Alex had stopped making gym content and started talking about general business; a survey then found 78% of gym-owner clients had consumed at least two long-form pieces before booking a call.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «78% of all clients had consumed at least TWO long form pieces of» — $100M Leads (2023), #2 Post Free Content Part II

## Cold outreach

#### 04.26. Three ways to get a targeted lead list
- **Rule:** Put your first 1000 cold names together from three sources, in order — scraping software, then list brokers asked for a list on your audience criteria, then elbow grease, joining groups and communities that hold your audience — testing each on a representative sample (a few hundred leads per software, a sample list per broker) and keeping a source only if the contact information is current and the leads respond; with more time than money, start with the third.
- Why it works: You work down from the most accessible leads; if you can search the database so can everyone else, while people on a list you assemble yourself are less likely to have had many cold reach outs, so they are the freshest.
- Bad → Good: Prospecting inside a group in a way that makes you look like someone only there to take business from it → find their contact details outside the group, and reach out inside the platform only if you have to.
- When not to apply: The self-assembled list is the freshest but takes the most time.
- Anchor: «There are three different ways I get my targeted lead lists.» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach

#### 04.27. Personalize: one to three facts a friend might know
- **Rule:** Open every cold message — the subject line, the first messages in chat, the first sentences on a call — with one to three facts a friend might know about the prospect, a compliment on them and, ideally, how it benefited you, found by a little batched research on each lead before sending, so the cold reach out looks like a warm one.
- Why it works: Strangers don't know you and don't trust you, and you have seconds to overcome both: personalization answers the first, big fast value (04.28) the second; people like people who like them, and even a stranger gives you more time if you know something about them and appreciates the time you took to research them.
- Bad → Good: Opening a call by asking whether they want to buy marketing services gets you hung up on → the dog-trainer call: their name and a pause, your name and a pause, then you watched a few of their videos and read their recent blog post, and the peanut butter trick helped with your doberman; you help dog trainers fill their books and partner with the best in the area; a daycare an hour north got 100 appointments in 30 days, heard of them?; a qualifying question, a light joke, and "Will you be around at 4?".
- When not to apply: The fewer leads on the list, the less automation and the more personalization: every one of only 1000 qualifying hedge fund managers gets a personal message, while with tens of millions of women 25–45 trying to lose weight you can get away with less, though personalizing still gets more.
- Anchor: «the sale. Basically one to three pieces of information we can find that» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach, Problem #2a: Personalize

#### 04.28. Big fast value; up the ante
- **Rule:** Lead a cold message with big fast value — something people actually pay for, not merely something so good they should pay for it — built to blow their minds in under thirty seconds rather than tickle their interest; when the offer or lead magnet isn't working, up the ante, offering more until they feel stupid saying no, rather than tweaking the message.
- Why it works: Strangers give you far less time to prove your worth and need far more incentive to move toward you, so a mediocre offer blends in with everyone else competing for their attention and gets ignored; people want what they would normally pay for when you give it away free.
- Bad → Good: Four months of cold outreach offering a "game planning session", code for a sales call, which most gyms declined → as much free service as they could possibly afford tripled take rates and made cold outreach a monster channel.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «We're not trying to tickle their interest, ]{.calibre3}[we're trying to» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach, Problem #2b: Big Fast Value

#### 04.29. Short scripts, volume before tweaking
- **Rule:** Keep phone and chat scripts to a page or two and cold emails rarely over half a page, get 100 conversations or 10,000 emails done before tweaking the script, and put 10–20% of outreach effort on brand new, untested technology, compared against your standard.
- Why it works: There are no awards for prettiest script — get testing first, then tweak as you learn; running the new technology on a slice of the effort shows how it does against what you already use.
- Bad → Good: If you make phone calls five days a week, run a new dialer or tool on one of those days and see how it does compared to your standard one.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «prettiest script. Get your first 100 conversations or 10,000 emails out» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach, Problem #2b action step

#### 04.30. Follow up across channels, then work the list again
- **Rule:** Plan a higher-ticket cold sale for two to three conversations after the appointment is booked, not one; when a lead doesn't answer on one channel, follow up on another and give the unanswered attempt as the reason; once the list has been worked multiple times in multiple ways, leave it three to six months, then work it again from the top.
- Why it works: People who don't know you need more touch points; a non-response either gets you a response on the next channel or a real reason to reach out again, and people respond to different methods; on an old list everything may be right except the timing — they didn't see the messages, couldn't respond then, or their circumstances changed.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Shoot for fewer than two to three conversations, but expect more when you start out.
- Anchor: «don't know you. So expect two to three conversations before a higher» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach, Problem #3c

#### 04.31. Cold outreach takes about a year
- **Rule:** Plan roughly a year for a cold outreach channel to become profitable at scale, not weeks, and don't pull the plug on the early months' sales.
- Why it works: People dramatically underestimate both the volume cold outreach needs and how long it takes; the work is boring and tedious, but brutally effective, and proper expectations are part of the method.
- Bad → Good: Planned for twelve weeks → September 0 sales; October 2 ($32,000) and the team asks to pull the plug; December 4 ($64,000) and it asks again; January 6, February 10, March 14, April 20, May 30 sales ($480,000); today cold outreach generates millions per month.
- When not to apply: The year is the author's own experience, a long time at least for him; two earlier attempts failed, and the third worked when run by someone who had done it before and tracked every metric.
- Anchor: «me it would take a year to scale. I figured we could do it in twelve» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach

#### 04.32. Cold outreach numbers: shoot for 3%, then solve for X
- **Rule:** Steer cold outreach by its ratios — shoot for 3% of an email list becoming engaged leads, about 30% opening and 10% of those replying with interest (2023) — work out the daily volume backward from the customers you want (solve for X), and judge the channel against the bare minimum of three times: the profit from a customer at least three times what the outreach costs to get one, counting labour and software.
- Why it works: Cold outreach is incredibly reliable — a certain input creates a certain number of responses — so once you know how much outreach engages a lead, the only thing left to do is more; nearly all its cost is labour, so the return is computed from labour and software across the steps.
- Bad → Good: One customer per 100 emails and 100 customers wanted → 10,000 emails, 333 a day; one person sends 111 a day, so three people send every day; by phone, 100 calls at a 20% pick-up with 25% taking the lead magnet is four engaged leads, one an hour over four hours; a calling team at $15 an hour plus $50 per shown appointment, ten-cent leads and a 33% close costs $360 per client against $3600 profit, a 10:1 return (2023).
- When not to apply: The numbers vary by platform, and three times is the bare minimum, not the target — the portfolio company cited gets over 30:1 from its outreach; do the outreach yourself at first and hand it to a rep once the customers it brings make more than the rep costs.
- Anchor: «vary but ]{.calibre3}[shoot for 3% of your list turning into engaged» — $100M Leads (2023), #3 Cold Outreach, Benchmarks, Email Example
