# Continuity offers: sell ongoing value once, get paid again and again, and keep customers staying

Open this sheet when the user designs, prices or repairs a subscription, membership, retainer or any other recurring offer, or asks why members start but do not stay. It holds where continuity sits in the Money Model and the three Continuity Offers of $100M Money Models (2025): Continuity Bonus, Continuity Discount and Waived Fee; then the Lost Chapters (2025) continuity offers: Lifetime Upgrades, Lifetime Discounts and Discount + One-Time Fee.
The Money Model itself and the attraction offers are in sheet 10; upsells, downsells and payment plans are in sheet 11; billing frequency, price raises and the Crazy Eight are in sheet 13; retention moves such as annual payment options are in sheet 14.

## When to offer continuity

#### 12.1. Make Continuity Offers last
- **Rule:** Make the Continuity Offer the last offer of the Money Model (the deliberate sequence of offers): after the Attraction, Upsell and Downsell Offers, as one last sale inside the 30-day window that stacks recurring cash; do not use continuity on its own as the Attraction Offer.
- Why it works: Continuity attracts more customers but crashes 30-day profits and makes profitable advertising difficult; placed last, the earlier offers bring the cash today (the Attraction Offer covers the cost of getting the customer, the Upsell lifts 30-day profit well above the costs of getting and delivering, the Downsell turns the nos into yeses) and continuity stacks recurring cash for tomorrow.
- Bad → Good: A $1,000 thing offered to 100 people gets 10 buyers, $10,000 now and nothing later → the same thing at $50 a month gets 40 of the 100, $2,000 now and $40,000 over twenty months, with four times as many customers left to upsell: more money tomorrow, strapped for cash today, which is why it goes last.
- When not to apply: The author allows Continuity Offers wherever and however you want (they can attract, upsell, downsell or re-engage), and sometimes their best timing comes after the first thirty days; see 12.3 for an Attraction Offer that turns into continuity.
- Anchor: «By making continuity offers *last,* we get the best of all worlds. We» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section V: Continuity Offers

#### 12.2. Ongoing value means ongoing payments
- **Rule:** Put something on continuity only when the customer keeps getting value from it for as long as they pay; charge a one-off deliverable over time as a payment plan, and do not sell a service provided forever for a single price.
- Why it works: A Continuity Offer is ongoing value paid for with ongoing payments until the customer cancels, so paying forever for a one-day workshop is silly, while one price, even a big one, for a service provided forever is probably a mistake.
- Bad → Good: A one-day workshop billed every month forever → billed until the customer covers its cost, which makes it a payment plan; a service delivered forever for one big price → the same service on ongoing payments.
- When not to apply: The test is the value delivered, not the billing schedule: without ongoing value the same schedule is a payment plan (payment plan downsells are in sheet 11).
- Anchor: «get ongoing value, it probably makes sense for them to make ongoing» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section V: Continuity Offers

#### 12.3. Turn Attraction Offers Into Continuity Offers With Automatic Renewal
- **Rule:** When the Attraction Offer is a bulk purchase of product or time, make it renew automatically month-to-month when the purchased period runs out.
- Why it works: One sale then earns the benefits of an Attraction Offer and of a Continuity Offer at once.
- Bad → Good: Dog food: buy four months of food and get two months free as the Attraction Offer, then automatic month-to-month renewal once the bulk purchase runs out (example — $100M Money Models, Section VI, Example Money Models).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Renewal.** This makes it a two-for-one. For example, if you do a Buy 6» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section VI: Make Your Money Model, Important Notes

#### 12.4. Perfect One Offer At A Time
- **Rule:** Build the Money Model one offer at a time: run each offer until it works reliably, then until it runs automatically, and add the next stage only when the current one works financially and operationally and pays for the stage after it.
- Why it works: Implementing a whole Money Model at once breaks the business, which starts breaking anyway once the Money Model works; a bootstrapped business started with a finished Money Model collapses on top of its owner, and none of the author's businesses started with a fully forged one.
- Bad → Good: Gym Launch ran a Decoy Offer alone and reached $476,000 a month in three months; only then it added a Classic Upsell with a community as Continuity Bonus, a Payment Plan Downsell and a Continuity Discount (about $1,500,000 a month), and after that a personalised Menu Upsell with Feature Downsells ($2,300,000 a month within 14 months).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «Money Model at once. Don't. Stick to your stage. Pick one offer. Try it.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Section VI: Make Your Money Model, Important Notes

## Continuity Bonus Offers

#### 12.5. The bonus outweighs the first continuity payment
- **Rule:** In a Continuity Bonus Offer, offer an awesome thing on condition that the customer signs up today, and make that bonus worth more than the first continuity payment.
- Why it works: The bonus is what makes people start: the more good stuff added and the more costs taken away, the more people join the continuity.
- Bad → Good: Pet food at $59 a month: sign up for monthly shipments and get every dog toy the company has ever made, an $800 value, free, plus a new dog toy every month as a member.
- When not to apply: Keep bonuses related to the core offer, or you attract the wrong customers, and price them realistically: made-up values will not anchor and lose trust.
- Anchor: «With Continuity Bonuses you give the customer an awesome thing *if* they» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Description

#### 12.6. Advertise the bonus, not the membership
- **Rule:** In advertising for a Continuity Offer, sell the free valuable thing, and explain the membership only after the lead shows interest.
- Why it works: An invitation to join a membership program is nowhere near as compelling as the offer of a free valuable thing.
- Bad → Good: A gym sold its six-week challenge and converted later: 34 of every 100 took the challenge and about 17 of them stayed → the challenge became free for anyone who joined: about 15 took the challenge alone, 40 went straight into continuity, about 8 of those 40 prepaid six months, and membership sales tripled while up-front cash still stacked.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**Focus On The Bonus, Not The Membership.** "Join my membership program"» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.7. Bonuses made of what you already have and do
- **Rule:** Build continuity bonuses out of things the business already has and already does, such as back issues or onboarding, and put a price on each.
- Why it works: Past material costs no extra time but carries high value, and onboarding has to happen anyway, so you might as well put a price on it: if you value it, they will too.
- Bad → Good: A newsletter at $399 a month after a 30-day free trial: all 40 past newsletters, valued at $15,880, free on becoming a member today; paying today also locks the rate at $299 a month with early digital access and a physical copy every month.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**Make Bonuses Things You Already Have And Do.** For instance, the two» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.8. Anchor The Bonuses
- **Rule:** Sell what the bonus is worth and what it does before revealing how the customer can get it free; when bonuses are stacked, name the dollar value of each one.
- Why it works: The high-value bonus works as the anchor: it may shock them, and that is the moment to ask whether they want to know how to get it free; naming each value anchors it, and stacked bonuses get even more people to join.
- Bad → Good: The gym still advertises its six-week challenge and still pitches its price; as soon as the lead is interested it asks whether they want it free: free if they become a member, and members also get exclusive bonuses such as better class times, the tanning booth and VIP events.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «● Sell the value of the bonus *before* telling them how they can get it» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Summary Points

#### 12.9. Pricing For Continuity vs. Up Front Cash
- **Rule:** When you want more up-front cash, also sell the bonus on its own as a single payment priced at a multiple of the first month of the continuity-plus-bonus offer, and set the multiple by the share you want on continuity: 1.33× gives 50%, 1.66× gives 60%, 2× gives 70%, 2.33× gives 80%, 2.66× gives 90% (2025).
- Why it works: The smaller the standalone price next to the continuity price, the more people buy the standalone, and the larger it is, the more choose continuity, so the ratio is the dial between cash today and recurring revenue tomorrow; people pay 33% more to avoid continuity, so even at 1.33× half buy the one-time option.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: The exact numbers matter less than the principle; the author gives them as his own tested range.
- Anchor: «To get 50% to choose continuity make the standalone offer 1.33x more.» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.10. Offer Bulk Prepaid Discounts
- **Rule:** Immediately after a customer joins continuity, offer a discounted prepaid block of months, such as buy five months and get one free.
- Why it works: Only one in eight joiners has to take it to raise 30-day profits by 50%, which can make or break the Money Model; the laws of discounting apply, and the larger the discount, the more people take it (2025).
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «"buy five months get one free." Only *one out of every eight people* has» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.11. If You Want Commitments, Prepare To Make A Trade
- **Rule:** When you want commitments of 3, 6 or 12+ months, give the continuity bonus only to customers who commit to the term.
- Why it works: The bonus buys the commitment: you lose the people who would have signed up month-to-month just for the bonus, so you net fewer sales but more committed customers.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: Commitment and volume cannot both be maximised: the author frames the choice as a trade, not an optimisation.
- Anchor: «commitments, trade them for bonuses. For example, only allow customers» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Bonus Offers, Important Notes

## Continuity Discount Offers

#### 12.12. Four ways to apply a continuity discount
- **Rule:** In a Continuity Discount Offer (products, services or time given free, now or later, for a commitment to buy more over time), decide before launch where the one-time discount goes: up front (discount first, term pushed out), at the end (bonus time earned by making every payment on time), spread evenly over the term, or after the first 1–2 payments.
- Why it works: The placement trades conversion against churn and cash: frontloaded discounts convert more customers but may churn more, backloaded ones convert fewer but churn less, spreading keeps cash flowing while giving the full discount, and taking one or two payments first covers advertising and delivery costs and proves the card works.
- Bad → Good: Three months free on a one-year commitment at $200 a month is a $600 discount; spread over twelve months it becomes $50 off every month, and customers who make every payment on time can be told they keep that discount for life once the term ends.
- When not to apply: Up front works best in industries with a history of enforcing contracts (cell phones, storage, real estate, equipment, anything with collateral); with historically high churn, skip it, and remember it gets customers but delays cash.
- Anchor: «**I discount in four ways:** Up front, at the end, an even spread, or» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, Examples and Summary Points

#### 12.13. Extend the term, don't eat into it
- **Rule:** Give the free months of a commitment discount on top of the paid term, not out of it.
- Why it works: Starting from the extended term leaves room to Feature Downsell a shorter one later.
- Bad → Good: Pay nine months, get three free, twelve in total → pay twelve months, get three free, fifteen in total.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «prefer to start with extending the term. Then, I can Feature Downsell a» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.14. Bill in weeks, not calendar months
- **Rule:** Set recurring billing on cycles counted in weeks (every 4 weeks, every 12 weeks or longer) instead of calendar months.
- Why it works: A year has 12 months but 13 four-week cycles, an 8.3% difference; the same number of people buy at $100 every four weeks as at $100 a month, so the extra cycle is pure profit, and at a 20% margin annual profit rises 41% (2025).
- Bad → Good: $100 a month, 12 charges a year → $100 every 4 weeks, 13 charges a year, with the price displayed weekly; change the contracts of new customers immediately, set a date for everyone else, call it a price increase if needed and explain it as reinvestment in the business (example — Pricing playbook, Pricing Play #1: Monthly to 28 Day Billing Cycles).
- When not to apply: Weekly and bi-weekly billing give the same benefit in theory but caused billing hassles because people wanted short pauses, so stick with 4 weeks, 12 weeks or longer (Pricing playbook, 2025); this is compatible with sheet 13's bill less frequently: the cycle is counted in weeks at whatever length the customer accepts.
- Anchor: «hate money. Bill *weekly* (weekly, every 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 12 weeks» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.15. Get Two Forms Of Payment
- **Rule:** Quote recurring prices with a processing fee (3% in $100M Money Models, 3.99% in the Pricing playbook) and waive it for every recurring customer who puts a second form of payment on file, ACH wherever you can get it.
- Why it works: Recurring businesses lose mountains of cash to expired cards and insufficient funds from customers who never cancelled: involuntary churn runs 1.2–1.7% a month, 24–34% of all churn at 5% monthly churn (Pricing playbook, 2025); the waiver is justified because chasing new payment information costs man hours; the fee alone goes straight to the bottom line, 30% more profit for a business at 10% profit, and the author has never had anyone refuse to buy over it; ACH links to the bank account and is the cheapest way to transact besides cash.
- Bad → Good: Once the price is agreed, ask how they want to pay, then name the card processing fee; if they hesitate, offer to waive it for a second form of payment, have them authorise both, and re-run failed transactions the next day on the new card (example — Pricing playbook, Pricing Play #2: Processing Fees & Second Form of Payment).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «with the same solution. I ask them if they want a 3% discount (a pretty» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.16. Gift Cards
- **Rule:** Hand the discounted time over as a physical gift card that the customer can apply after about the first three payments or give to a friend.
- Why it works: The gift becomes a lead magnet, and many people simply forget to use it, which leaves you a full-priced sign-up.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «can apply the discount whenever they want *after the first three» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.17. Lifetime Discount earned at the most common churn point
- **Rule:** Advertise a lifetime lower rate, but make customers earn it by staying past the month where your average customer drops off.
- Why it works: Earning the lifetime discount at the month of greatest churn pulls customers through the point where they usually cancel, for a lifetime lower rate.
- Bad → Good: A rice company offered three options: a one-time price, 5% off on subscription, and 15% off for customers who stayed on the subscription five straight months, just beyond the point where most customers cancelled.
- When not to apply: A Lifetime Discount only works if you actually charge more when the offer ends; otherwise you are listing the price and pretending it is a discount (the Lost Chapters); show it off retail, as in 12.26.
- Anchor: «**Try Lifetime Discount At Your Most Common Churn Point.** You advertise» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, Important Notes

#### 12.18. Cancellation fee equals the discount, waived for an exit interview
- **Rule:** Settle the cancellation policy before launching a Continuity Discount Offer: make the fee for cancelling match the discount the customer received for committing, payable whenever they leave, and offer to waive it for any cancelling customer who comes in for an exit interview.
- Why it works: People do not always keep their commitments; a fee matching the discount is simple to explain and just puts them back at the month-to-month rate, and the waiver gives a real reason to give feedback: the author routinely saves a third of the customers who agree to an exit interview and rolls some into a higher level of service (2025).
- Bad → Good: A customer who took the year commitment with the $100 initiation fee waived tries to cancel early → absolutely, no problem: they cover the $100 that was waived and are switched to month-to-month (example — the Lost Chapters, Continuity Offer: Lifetime Discounts).
- When not to apply: The policy assumes every customer enters the Continuity Offer on a discount of some kind.
- Anchor: «Just make the cancellation fee *equal to the discount they agreed to» — $100M Money Models (2025), Continuity Discount Offers, CANCELLATIONS

## Waived Fee Offers

#### 12.19. The Waived Fee Offer
- **Rule:** For commitments of a year or longer, offer month-to-month with a startup fee of 3–5× the monthly rate (1.5–3× when you want more up-front cash), waive the whole fee for customers who commit to the term, and charge it if they cancel inside the term (2025).
- Why it works: Customers stay longer when leaving costs more than staying: avoiding the fee gets them to commit, and the fee owed on quitting early keeps them in; a smaller fee pushes more buyers to month-to-month and brings more cash up front, a larger one pushes them into the commitment.
- Bad → Good: Twelve months at $1,000 a month: option A, the $5,000 fee plus the first month's $1,000 up front, then $1,000 a month, cancel any time; option B, the $5,000 is waived for a twelve-month commitment at $1,000 a month and paid only if the commitment is broken early; the buyer initials that they understand they can still quit early by paying the waived fee.
- When not to apply: Not for commitments under a year: the minimum is a year, and it works especially for services slow to show results (SEO, investing, weight loss), because it keeps people committed when they get emotional.

#### 12.20. The fee ends when the commitment is served
- **Rule:** Once a customer has served out the full commitment, cancel the waived fee, so that from then on they can leave free of charge.
- Why it works: They have earned their free cancellation; a fee that does not stick forever keeps the arrangement equitable.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**Drop The Fee After The Customer Fulfills The Commitment.** If someone» — $100M Money Models (2025), Waived Fee Offer, Important Notes

#### 12.21. More than 5% wanting out early is a product signal
- **Rule:** When more than 5% of committed customers want to cancel early, investigate the product rather than tightening the terms (2025).
- Why it works: Pricing incentivizes sticking but cannot and should not overcome a terrible product: you want to nudge people, not handcuff them into paying for something they hate, or they will just hate you.
- Bad → Good: No example in the source.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «**If More Than 5% Of People Want To Cancel Early, Look Into It.**» — $100M Money Models (2025), Waived Fee Offer, Important Notes

#### 12.22. Donate the fee to a cause they hate
- **Rule:** To keep a Waived Fee customer extra motivated, ask what cause they absolutely hate and tell them that if they cancel early their setup fee will be donated to it.
- Why it works: It gives the customer two reasons to stay rather than one.

## Lifetime Upgrades

#### 12.23. Pair a “when” with a “what”
- **Rule:** Once customers have started a continuity program, give them bonuses on top of the product itself, each pairing one “when”, a delay (how long they wait) or a milestone (what they do or achieve), with one “what”, a one-time bonus, a variable bonus given on a schedule but different each time, or a Lifetime Upgrade (a permanent high-value change in continuity status); combine several, and make every milestone something that makes the customer more successful or advertises for you, ideally both.
- Why it works: A good offer gets them to start and good bonuses get them to stick: the time to the first bonus extends their stay once, each further bonus extends it again, so more incentives to start and stick beat one.
- Bad → Good: Local service: stay four months in a row to become an advanced member with access to the Annual Customer Appreciation Palooza, or stay four months and on the fifth get VIP first-in-line access to in-demand timeslots; digital product: one more module for every friend referred; butcher box: free bacon with every order for life, lost on cancelling; car lease: free servicing every 3,000 miles or six months; a public post that they are starting a weight loss challenge earns a bonus because it raises their adherence and advertises the business.
- When not to apply: Applies to anything that provides continuous value, once people have already started; unless a bonus is a huge and permanent improvement, keep it variable (12.24).
- Anchor: «When I make a bonus, I think of “when” and “what” For the “when” part, I use delays» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Upgrades

#### 12.24. Keep bonuses variable; announce the type, keep the surprise
- **Rule:** Make recurring bonuses variable, telling customers the type of bonus they will get and keeping the bonus itself a surprise, and make a bonus a Lifetime Upgrade only when it gives a huge and permanent improvement.
- Why it works: However good the thing is, customers get used to it, so new things given more often, even if less valuable, keep more customers interested longer; a surprise inside a known type keeps the business flexible and makes the bonus more valuable.
- Bad → Good: Gym Launch customers knew a new play was coming but not which one: one new money-making play every fourteen days, kept up for almost two years, took revenue from around $300,000 to around $4,000,000+ a month, more than 13X, not by selling more customers but by giving them good reasons to keep paying.
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source.
- Anchor: «variable bonuses or Lifetime Upgrades Unless your bonus gives a huge and permanent» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Upgrades, Important Notes

## Lifetime Discounts

#### 12.25. Lifetime Discount: reason, urgency, scarcity
- **Rule:** Build a Lifetime Discount (a cheaper price for as long as the customer stays on recurring payments) from six parts: retail price, the offer, the discounted price, a reason, urgency in the form of a limited time and scarcity in the form of a limited number; make the reason a real event in the life of the business or the owner, good or bad (Never waste a crisis).
- Why it works: Customers take it now because they get value at a discount now, and stick because if they leave they cannot get it back; a lifetime discount sounds almost too good to be true, so it needs an equally strong reason to be believed, and the best reasons are real and true life events.
- Bad → Good: The Opener, a grand-opening specialist for a billion-dollar gym franchise, opens every location with 400+ recurring members: a 14-day trial into a founding member lifetime discount offered only before opening; 80% of trials sign up, so 500 trials give 400 members billing on day 15 and a profit in month one, and most stick because they do not want to lose the discount.
- When not to apply: Know your numbers: the costs of getting and delivering will change while their rate stays locked, and you must still make a profit after the discount; the author himself uses it as an upsell (a Rollover Upsell discount kept only if they finish the credited payments) rather than as the attraction offer.
- Anchor: «To make Lifetime Discounts even more attractive, add urgency (limited time), scarcity» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Discounts

#### 12.26. Discount off retail; price protection only for a period
- **Rule:** State a Lifetime Discount as a percentage or an amount off retail rather than as a fixed price for life, and when you promise price protection at a fixed price, give it for a stated number of months.
- Why it works: Costs of getting and delivering change: percentage off and dollars off stay flexible while a fixed price for life does not, and one price forever limits the business, whereas a fixed term keeps the flexibility and still protects the customer.
- Bad → Good: Recurring local service: retail $400 a month, 50% off retail for life, $200 a month; digital product in early access: retail $39 a month, $20 off retail for life, $19 a month; loyalty version: $5 a month off for life once they have stayed five months, reason rewarding loyalty, no urgency and no scarcity.
- When not to apply: This display is what keeps a Lifetime Discount compatible with sheet 13's price-raise rules (no grandfathering, no one-time price for lifetime access): the customer keeps a discount off retail, not a locked price.
- Anchor: «Limit price protection for fixed periods I try to always give myself flexibility Giving» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Discounts, Important Notes

#### 12.27. One-time only means once
- **Rule:** When a discount has been announced as one time only, never offer it on the same thing again, never give a cancelled customer their Lifetime Discount back, and never offer two prices for the same thing to two people at the same time; to sell at that rate again, change what the offer includes.
- Why it works: Repeating or restoring the deal costs credibility with everyone who took it and kept paying to keep theirs.
- Bad → Good: A customer who cancelled a Lifetime Discount wants to return → do not give it back; offer a downsell that meets the same price with different features, which works best for price-sensitive people.
- When not to apply: Businesses test price points all the time; the limit is the same thing, at the same time, to different people.
- Anchor: «If you say you will only offer this discount once and never again, stay true to it To» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Discounts, Important Notes

#### 12.28. Loss leader conditional on retail
- **Rule:** When the business has two or more complementary services, give one at a steep founder's discount for life on condition that the other is bought at retail.
- Why it works: The insane founder's discount attracts the leads, and the profit is made on the upsell.
- Bad → Good: Two mid-priced offers, or a generic 25% off both → a steep lifetime founder's discount on one service with the other at retail, which often works better.
- When not to apply: It needs two or more complementary services.
- Anchor: «You can offer a Lifetime Discount on one thing so long as they buy another at retail» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Discounts, Important Notes

## Up-front fees and the Discount + One-Time Fee

#### 12.29. The Bigger The Head, The Longer The Tail
- **Rule:** When you want customers to stay longer, get them to commit more up front (an initiation fee, a paid-in-full discount, a waived fee tied to a term), raise the one-time startup fee where you want lower churn, and as soon as a customer likes the product, repeat the original offer: pay the difference of the down payment and lock in a permanently lower monthly rate.
- Why it works: The sunk cost fallacy: people keep investing where they have already put time or money, so the higher the barrier to entry, the higher the barrier to exit; asked which membership sticks longest, John, the tanning empire king, named the one where they pay the most up front ($100 to sign up and a rate lowered from $18 to $10 a month, and those customers call before their card changes to keep it); the buy-down brings in more cash and makes a stickier customer at the same time.
- Bad → Good: $19 down and $19 a month churned at a higher rate → a $100 sign-up fee with $10 a month churned at next to nothing (example — the Lost Chapters, Continuity Offer: Discount + One-Time Fee).
- When not to apply: No special caveats in the source; the Retention playbook's version of the same construct, a big one-time fee priced on one-time value with a small recurring fee, is 14.16.
- Anchor: «The more you can get people to commit up front the longer they’ll stick» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Lifetime Discounts, The Bigger The Head, The Longer The Tail

#### 12.30. Discount + One-Time Fee: the four steps
- **Rule:** For a Discount + One-Time Fee offer (a discounted first term plus one or more made-up fees), pick each fee's name, its price and its reason why, then charge, discount or waive it; use it especially where the customer has to do something to succeed: send information, fill out forms, show up, make selections, change behaviour.
- Why it works: The discount attracts the interest while the fee offsets the acquisition costs of marketing and sales; when people pay, they pay attention, so the fee lowers churn and deepens the customer's investment; and customers come in expecting to pay something, so it surprises fewer people than a free offer.
- Bad → Good: Any recurring service: the first month for $100 (95% off) with a $1,900 setup fee still charged, so $2,000 in the first month and $2,000 a month after; a 12-week program at $3,000 sold as $1,000 a month with 88% off the first month ($120) plus a $1,000 setup fee: $1,120 first, then two payments of $1,000.
- When not to apply: The fee is made up but not to be taken lightly: be clear about its reason with every customer, since you are doing that work anyway.
- Anchor: «So here are the four steps to creating your one-time fee(s):» — $100M Series: Lost Chapters (2025), Continuity Offer: Discount + One-Time Fee
